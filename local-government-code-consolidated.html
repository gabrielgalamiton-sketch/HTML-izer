I. FOUNDATIONS: AUTONOMY WITHIN A UNITARY STATE

1. Nature, policy, and decentralization — Constitution, Art. X; §§1–4, 15

Local autonomy means self-government within national sovereignty, not independence (Pimentel v. Aguirre).

  • Regular subdivisions: provinces, cities, municipalities, barangays.
  • Autonomous regions constitutionally authorized in Muslim Mindanao and the Cordilleras.
  • Dual nature, §15: governmental/political subdivision and proprietary/corporate entity.
  • R.A. 7160: approved October 10, 1991; effective January 1, 1992 (§536). B.P. Blg. 337 governed before effectivity.
  • Scope, §4: LGUs and other subdivisions created by law; national officials/agencies to the extent provided in the Code.

Section 2 policies:

  • Self-reliant communities and partnership in national goals through decentralization.
  • Accountability through recall, initiative, referendum.
  • National-agency consultations with LGUs, NGOs/POs, and concerned sectors before local projects.
Concept Meaning Example
Deconcentration Work moves to national field offices; line control remains. DENR/DPWH regional offices
Devolution Powers, services, and resources move to LGUs (§17). Municipal health services

Clarification: “Decentralization of administration/power” is a broader doctrinal distinction, particularly relevant to special regional autonomy. Do not equate every LGC devolution with the fullest transfer of political power. For class problems, distinguish national control from local discretion subject to supervision.

2. Thirteen operative principles — §3

  • (a) Effective allocation of powers, functions, responsibilities, and resources.
  • (b) Accountable, efficient, and dynamic local organization.
  • (c) Appointment/removal based on merit and fitness, subject to civil service law.
  • (d) Responsibilities accompanied by adequate resources: own revenues, national-tax shares, and national-wealth shares.
  • (e) Higher LGUs ensure component units act within their prescribed powers.
  • (f) Inter-local grouping, coordination, and pooling for common benefit.
  • (g) Enhance capacity, especially of municipalities/barangays, through participation in national programs.
  • (h) Continuing legislative, administrative, and organizational reforms for autonomy.
  • (i) Shared responsibility for ecological balance, subject to national policies.
  • (j) Stronger accountability to constituents and better local leadership.
  • (k) National coordination and technical/material assistance to deserving LGUs.
  • (l) Private-sector participation, especially in basic services.
  • (m) Decentralization must improve LGU performance and community life.

3. Rules of interpretation — §5

  • Local powers: construe liberally for the LGU; doubts favor devolution and the lower LGU.
  • Tax ordinances/revenue measures: doubts favor the taxpayer, strictly against the LGU.
  • Tax exemptions/incentives/reliefs: construe strictly against the claimant.
  • General welfare: construe liberally to expand lawful powers for development and quality of life.
  • Vested rights/obligations: existing contracts and rights remain governed by their original terms or the law when vested.
  • Customs and traditions: resort to them only when no applicable provision or jurisprudence governs; a custom must be proved as a fact (Yao Kee v. Sy-Gonzales).

Limit: Liberal construction cannot authorize a constitutional violation, conflict with national law, or unreasonable restraint of trade. The class example banning pottery made outside San Juan illustrates unlawful local protectionism.

Badua v. Cordillera Bodong Administration: the particular tribal body lacked judicial authority; distinguish unauthorized judgments from lawful customary dispute resolution recognized by later laws.

4. Cities, regions, and barangay governance

MMDA Coordinating agency; no general local lawmaking/police power (Bel-Air).
  • Art. X §11: Congress may create a special metropolitan political subdivision by law and plebiscite; component cities/municipalities retain autonomy; functions limited to basic metropolitan services. Distinguish this from MMDA.
  • Autonomous region: organic act + plebiscite; only favorably voting constituent units join.
  • Ordillo: Ifugao alone could not constitute a Cordillera autonomous region.
  • Sulu v. Medialdea: Sulu’s rejection excluded it from BARMM.
  • BARMM update: BOL, R.A. 11054, and BAA 49 Local Governance Code; not BAA 13.
  • Cordillera Broad Coalition / Chiongbian: administrative regional reorganization is different from LGU creation; it does not itself require an LGU plebiscite.

Barangay fusion: Punong Barangay is executive, legislative presiding officer, Lupon chairperson, and supervisor of tanods. The lecture’s “parliamentary” description concerns this concentration; the Lupon is not a criminal trial court.

  • Council: Punong Barangay + 7 regular members + SK chair ex officio (§390).
  • Secretary/treasurer: appointive; not council members; no legislative votes.
  • Punong Barangay has no ordinance veto.
  • §393 minimum monthly honoraria: ₱1,000 Punong Barangay; ₱600 regular members/secretary/treasurer. Higher compensation must obey budget/compensation rules; increased NTA does not automatically create salaries.

II. CREATION, REORGANIZATION, AND IDENTITY — §§6–15

5. Authority and viability requirements — §§6–10

Creation is legislative; a resolution requesting creation is not itself a charter.

  • Congress: provinces, cities, municipalities, and other political subdivisions; it may also create barangays.
  • Sangguniang Panlalawigan / Panlungsod: may create barangays within their respective jurisdiction by ordinance.
  • Sangguniang Bayan: cannot create a barangay.
  • President: cannot create municipalities simply by executive order (Pelaez v. Auditor General; Kapalong v. Moya).
  • Sema v. COMELEC: the ARMM legislature could not create a province because doing so necessarily implicated the creation of a congressional district, a power belonging to Congress.

Verifiable indicators, §7:

  • Income: sufficient for essential services; DOF certification.
  • Population: total inhabitants, not registered voters; PSA certification.
  • Land area: properly described by metes and bounds; LMB–DENR certification; contiguity subject to statutory exceptions.
Proposed LGU Income requirement Population / land requirement
Barangay, §386 No specific income minimum 2,000 inhabitants; 5,000 in Metro Manila, HUCs, or metropolitan political subdivisions; identified territory
Municipality, §442 ₱2.5 million average annual income, preceding 2 consecutive years, 1991 constant prices 25,000 population AND 50 km², subject to island exception
Component city, §450, R.A. 9009 / 11683 ₱100 million locally generated average annual income, last 2 consecutive years, 2000 constant prices 150,000 population OR 100 km²
Alternative cityhood route, R.A. 11683 Original ₱400 million locally generated average annual income, 2 years, 2012 constant prices; increases 5% every 3 years after the first 3 years 100,000 population OR 100 km²
Province, §461 ₱20 million average annual income, preceding 2 consecutive years, 1991 constant prices 250,000 population OR 2,000 km², subject to island doctrine

Exceptions/updates:

  • R.A. 11683: alternative cityhood requires 100,000 people OR 100 km², not waiver of both. First 5% income increase gives ₱420 million; obtain the proper DOF certification.
  • Islands: distinguish minimum-area exemptions from contiguity exceptions; §§442/450 and Navarro v. Ermita govern the respective situations.
  • Indigenous communities: §386 permits relaxation of the barangay population minimum for service delivery.
  • Tan: sea is not statutory land area.
  • §442(d): recognition of qualifying pre-Code municipalities is not a universal cure for unconstitutional LGUs.
  • §285: locally created barangays’ financial requirements belong to the creating LGU; creation validity and separate NTA entitlement differ.

Creation process:

  • Obtain DOF/PSA/LMB certifications and technical descriptions.
  • Enact the authorized law/ordinance; a local petition does not replace it.
  • COMELEC plebiscite: majority of votes cast in directly affected units; ordinarily within 120 days of enabling law/ordinance effectivity, unless another date is fixed.
  • Corporate commencement follows §14 or the charter’s special date.

Division/merger, §8: comply with creation requirements; division must not reduce the affected LGUs below minimum income/population/area or reduce the original unit’s income classification below its prior classification.

Abolition, §9: income, population, or land area must have been irreversibly reduced below the minimum, duly certified. The authorized law/ordinance specifies the unit absorbing the abolished LGU. A governor cannot abolish a municipality by executive declaration.

Plebiscite doctrines: Tan / Padilla / Novaliches — include the directly affected mother unit, not only the carved-out area. Miranda — Santiago’s ICC-to-component conversion needed a plebiscite. Agoo–Aringay illustration: land deficiency alone is not decisive where a population alternative applies.

6. Government sites, names, and corporate commencement — §§11–15

  • Seat selection, §11: centrality, access, transport/communication, drainage/sanitation, and development; need not be the poblacion.
  • Transfer of seat: changed conditions + public hearing + 2/3 of all sanggunian members; remain within the LGU’s territory.
  • Transfer/relocation/conversion of offices or facilities: public hearing + majority of all members. Distinguish this from transferring the seat of government.
  • Government center, §12: provinces/cities/municipalities shall endeavor to establish one; agencies bear their construction costs. §12 itself imposes no universal one-hectare minimum.
  • Corporate existence, §14: election and qualification of the chief executive AND a majority of sanggunian members, unless the creating law/ordinance fixes another time.
  • Dual legal nature is in §15; corporate powers are in §22.

Naming/renaming, §13:

  • Province: its enumerated properties; component city/municipality names on the concerned council’s recommendation.
  • City/municipality: its enumerated properties and barangay names on barangay recommendation. Barangay council has no unrestricted §13 renaming power.
  • Consult historical authority (NHCP); national-road renaming requires competent national authority.
  • No living person; justifiable reason; ordinarily once per 10 years.
  • Historical/cultural/ethnic significance: unanimous vote + historical consultation.
  • Schools: school board recommendation; health facilities: health board.
  • LGU name: plebiscite ratification.
  • Notify President’s Office, district representative, Bureau of Posts.

III. LOCAL POWERS, RESOURCES, PROPERTY, AND LIABILITY — §§16–24

7. General welfare and basic services — §§16–17

LGUs do not possess inherent sovereign police power or eminent domain. They exercise delegated powers. Local revenue-raising has a direct constitutional basis, but remains subject to congressional guidelines and limitations.

Section 16 authorizes powers:

  • Expressly granted.
  • Necessarily implied.
  • Necessary, appropriate, or incidental to efficient and effective governance.
  • Essential to the general welfare.

§16 welfare objectives:

  • Culture preservation/enrichment.
  • Health and safety.
  • Balanced ecology.
  • Appropriate, self-reliant science/technology.
  • Public morals.
  • Economic prosperity/social justice.
  • Full employment.
  • Peace/order and comfort/convenience.

Two branches: general legislative power implementing lawful duties; police power protecting community welfare.

Validity of police regulation:

  • Lawful subject: public interests generally require the intervention.
  • Lawful means: reasonably necessary and not unduly oppressive.
  • Respect constitutional rights, applicable procedural safeguards, and national law.
  • Summary emergency abatement: genuine nuisance per se, not merely an LGU label.

Cases: Bel-Air—MMDA lacks general police power; Batangas CATV—city cannot assume national cable-rate regulation; Magtajas—local casino ban cannot override national authorization.

Tier §17 service emphasis
Barangay Agricultural support; health/day care; sanitation and waste collection; barangay roads/bridges and community facilities
Municipality Agricultural extension; primary health and social welfare; waste disposal; municipal roads/drainage/irrigation; markets, slaughterhouses, cemeteries
Province Hospitals and specialized health services; provincial social welfare; intermunicipal infrastructure; environmental responsibilities and technical support
City Municipal + provincial services, plus city-specific urban facilities

Devolution includes personnel, assets, records, responsibilities. Coordinate overlaps; national/higher LGUs may augment inadequate services (§17).

8. Fiscal autonomy — §18; Constitution, Art. X §§5–7

Five fiscal powers/entitlements:

  • Establish organizations responsible for development plans and priorities.
  • Create revenue sources and levy authorized taxes, fees, and charges.
  • Receive a just share in national taxes, automatically and directly released.
  • Receive an equitable national-wealth share, with direct benefits for inhabitants.
  • Acquire, develop, lease, encumber, alienate, or dispose of property within legal limits.

Tax/fee authority requires an ordinance (§132). An official receipt cannot cure an unauthorized charge. A regulatory fee needs a genuine regulatory purpose and reasonable regulatory cost; its label is not decisive.

Mandanas–Garcia: the base includes national taxes, beyond BIR internal revenue taxes, subject to the ruling’s exclusions. Expanded NTA implementation began FY2022; non-tax receipts are not automatically included.

National wealth, §§289–294 — distinct from NTA:

  • Resource-host sharing: province 20% / component city or municipality 45% / barangay 35%; HUC/ICC sharing: city 65% / barangay 35%.
  • Energy-source proceeds: at least 80% of the relevant proceeds lowers host-locality electricity costs; not the universal use rule for every resource receipt.
  • Tiwi illustration: province cannot divert the municipality’s lawful share. Counsel’s valid fee claim still requires proper authorization/appropriation.

9. Eminent domain — §19

Requisites:

  • Exercise by the local chief executive pursuant to an ordinance; a resolution is insufficient.
  • Taking for public use, purpose, or welfare, or for the benefit of the poor and landless.
  • Payment of just compensation.
  • A valid and definite prior offer to the owner, not accepted.

Process: prior offer and ordinance → court action/deposit → judicial compensation determination/payment.

  • Immediate possession: file the expropriation proceedings and deposit with the court at least 15% of FMV based on the current tax declaration.
  • Deposit ≠ final compensation; court determines compensation using the applicable taking-date rule.
  • Prompt payment is part of just compensation (City of Makati v. CA).
  • Parañaque v. V.M. Realty: statutory demand for an ordinance cannot be replaced with a resolution.
  • Noble: LGU cannot evade a binding accepted acquisition agreement through expropriation.
  • Barangays may expropriate under the Code, complying with the same substantive conditions.

Distinguish: national infrastructure right-of-way acquisitions have special rules, including R.A. 10752; do not substitute them for §19 or apply them to every national taking.

10. Land reclassification and road closure — §§20–21

Reclassification Conversion
City/municipal ordinance after public hearings, identifying permissible land uses. DAR authorization, where required, for agricultural land to be used for another purpose.
A planning/zoning act under §20. Governed by agrarian reform law and conversion rules.
Does not itself dispense with necessary conversion approval. Must still be distinguished from building and other development permits.

Section 20 grounds — either:

  • Agricultural use is no longer economically feasible and sound, as determined by the DA; or
  • Land has substantially greater value for residential, commercial, or industrial purposes, as determined by the sanggunian.

Maximum share of agricultural land reclassified at ordinance passage:

  • 15%: HUCs and ICCs.
  • 10%: component cities and 1st–3rd class municipalities.
  • 5%: lower-class municipalities, stated in the original Code as 4th–6th classes.

Limits:

  • CARP-distributed land: local reclassification does not override R.A. 6657/DAR conversion.
  • Forest/mineral/public-domain classification is not unrestricted local power.
  • Public plaza remains outside commerce while devoted to public use; zoning alone cannot authorize private leasing.
  • President may allow excess reclassification under statutory conditions.
  • R.A. 11964: now five income classes; read old six-class brackets with implementing rules.

Permanent local road/park/square closure, §21(a)–(b):

  • Ordinance + at least 2/3 of all members.
  • Adequate substitute when necessary.
  • Maintain public safety.
  • A freedom park cannot be permanently closed without transfer/relocation.
  • Property lawfully withdrawn from public use may be used/conveyed under applicable property rules.

Temporary closure, §21(c):

  • May cover a national or local road, alley, park, or square for the specified emergency, fiesta, rally, fair, or public-work purposes.
  • Duration must be stated in the LCE’s written order.
  • Athletic/cultural/civic activities must be officially sponsored, recognized, or approved by the LGU.
  • Temporary local street/market arrangements under §21(d) require the appropriate ordinance.

Correction: The notes’ blanket statement that an LGU can never temporarily close a national road conflicts with §21(c). Permanent local-road authority and specified temporary national-road authority are different.

11. Corporate powers, grants, and liability — §§22–24

Corporate powers, §22:

  • Continuous succession in the corporate name.
  • Sue and be sued.
  • Have/use a corporate seal; new or changed seals are registered with DILG.
  • Acquire and convey real/personal property.
  • Enter into contracts.
  • Exercise other corporate powers, subject to law.

Contracts: LCE needs prior sanggunian authorization, unless otherwise provided; post a legible copy. Procurement, appropriation, and public-property limits remain.

Grants, §23: sanggunian-authorized LCE may secure grants for §17 services without ordinary higher clearance. National-security projects require agency approval: 30-day silence = approval. Report nature/amount/terms to President and Congress within 30 days of signing.

Property illustrations: public-purpose conveyances can include reversionary clauses protecting the LGU if the stated use ceases; apply the actual deed and law.

§24: LGUs/officials are not exempt from death, injury, or property-damage liability. Identify the function and applicable liability rule; suability and liability differ.

Case / trigger Rule to retain
San Fernando v. Firme — road-repair truck accident Applied the governmental-function defense to the municipality; decided before LGC effectivity. Do not use it to erase §24 or express statutory liability.
City of Manila v. IAC — premature cemetery exhumation Proprietary cemetery operation; city liable under the governing civil-law principles.
Torio v. Fontanilla — fiesta stage collapse Municipality liable for negligence in a proprietary undertaking; councilors absolved absent the necessary personal participation/bad faith or gross negligence.
Guilatco v. Dagupan — uncovered manhole Article 2189 requires control or supervision, not ownership, of the public work.
Manila v. Teotico — defective catch basin Specific Civil Code liability for defective streets prevailed over the invoked general charter exemption.

Article 2189 elements:

  • Death or personal injury.
  • Caused by a defective road, street, bridge, public building, or other public work.
  • The work is under the province’s, city’s, or municipality’s control or supervision.

Corrections: uploaded Torio digests reverse the result. National-road location alone also does not prove control/supervision. Official personal liability needs its own factual/legal basis.

IV. SUPERVISION, CONSULTATION, AND COOPERATION — §§25–38

12. Supervision and the proper review channel — §§25, 29–32

Control General supervision
Superior may alter/reverse an act and substitute judgment. Ensure acts are within legal powers and duties are performed.
President over national executive departments, bureaus, and offices. President over LGUs; governor/mayor over component units as provided by law.

Rule: supervisor cannot replace lawful local policy merely from disagreement. Compelling a legal duty is supervision; substituting discretion is control (Drilon; Mondano).

  • Direct presidential supervision: provinces, HUCs, ICCs; autonomous regions under Art. X §16. Pateros has no provincial intermediary.
  • Through provinces: component cities and municipalities.
  • Through cities/municipalities: component barangays.
  • Alter ego: national secretaries may act for the President within lawful authority; elected local executives are not presidential alter egos.
  • No arbitrary removal: a mayor cannot simply dismiss an elected Punong Barangay; apply the lawful disciplinary process.

Executive-order review, §30:

  • Component city/municipal mayor’s EO → governor.
  • Punong Barangay’s EO → city/municipal mayor.
  • Forward copies within 3 days from issuance.
  • 30 days of reviewing-executive inaction: order deemed consistent with law and valid.

Keep channels distinct: §30 EOs → executives; §§56–57 ordinances → sanggunians.

Legal questions, §31: municipal legal officer, if present → otherwise provincial legal officer → provincial prosecutor if absent. Follow the local advisory channel; an opinion is not a judicial judgment.

13. Environmental consultation and police relations — §§26–28

Projects within §§26–27 require:

  • Prior consultation with concerned LGUs, NGOs/POs, and affected sectors.
  • Explanation of objectives, environmental/community effects, and mitigating measures.
  • Prior approval of the sanggunian concerned.
  • For affected occupants: relocation safeguards before eviction under §27.

Consultation/approval must precede implementation; post-construction discussions do not supply prior consultation. Check statutory coverage; §27 is not an unrestricted veto over every national act.

  • PICOP Resources v. Base Metals: obtain the required participation/approval of all affected LGUs, not just a convenient one.
  • Province of Rizal v. Executive Secretary: the San Mateo landfill controversy illustrates enforceable environmental and prior-consultation safeguards.

Police, §28; R.A. 6975/8551:

  • PNP: national, civilian, not private local police.
  • NAPOLCOM deputation supplies defined powers; governor/mayor powers differ.
  • Mayor: statutory operational supervision/control; police-chief selection from 5 eligible nominees.
  • Governor: statutory selection/recommendation/oversight, not identical mayoral deployment authority.
  • Withdrawal/suspension grounds: frequent unauthorized absence, abuse of authority, support for criminals, acts inimical to national security.
  • Manalo / Soriano: PNP/PCG civilian officers are not AFP officers requiring CA confirmation; PCG civilian character does not create mayoral control.

14. Common projects, civil society, and procurement — §§33–38

Inter-LGU cooperation, §33:

  • Appropriate ordinances for common-benefit undertakings.
  • Sanggunian approval after public hearing for contributions.
  • Contributions may include funds, real estate, equipment/property, and personnel.
  • Define participating LGUs’ terms in a memorandum of agreement.

Landfill illustration: properly authorized contributions to a common facility outside municipal boundaries are valid where it serves the municipality.

NGOs/POs:

  • §34: promote them as partners in local autonomy.
  • §35: joint ventures/cooperation for services, livelihood, capability building, enterprise, and ecological/social development.
  • §36: LCE may provide assistance with sanggunian concurrence, for qualifying projects within the LGU.
  • Accreditation/selection and the particular body’s composition rules govern representation. Accreditation does not automatically make every NGO a voting member of every local body.

Procurement update, §§37–38: original PBAC/technical-committee rules have been superseded by the procurement framework. Apply R.A. 12009 + IRR + transition rules, succeeding R.A. 9184.

  • BAC membership follows the governing law, not invented executive arrangements.
  • LCE is Head of Procuring Entity, not automatically the old PBAC chair.
  • Civil society supplies required observers, not automatic voting BAC members.

15. Other lecture mechanisms

  • Recall, §§69–75/R.A. 9244: required voter petition for loss of confidence in an elective local official. PRA abolished; Congress members excluded. Evardone: confidence is political, but procedure remains judicially reviewable.
  • Initiative, §§120–125: voters directly propose/enact/amend a matter within local legislative power. Initial §122 presentation: 1,000 province/city voters; 100 municipal; 50 barangay. After 30-day non-action, invoke notice/signature/COMELEC procedures; initial numbers are not the complete signature requirement.
  • Referendum, §126: electorate approves/amends/rejects an enacted ordinance.
  • Correction, §125: no sanggunian repeal/amendment for 6 months; thereafter 3/4 of all members during the protected statutory period—3 years, or 18 months after approval for barangays. Not the notes’ “1 year/2/3.”
  • Defensor-Santiago: R.A. 6735 inadequate for constitutional initiative. Lambino: constitutional initiative allows amendments, not revisions; distinguish local initiative.
  • Fabian: Congress cannot expand SC appellate jurisdiction without its advice/concurrence—Art. VI §30, not Art. VIII §30; use the proper Ombudsman administrative appeal route.
  • Boundary disputes, §§118–119: barangays in one city/town → its council; municipalities in one province → provincial council; municipalities in different provinces → provincial councils jointly; specified city combinations → respective councils jointly. Settlement/decision precedes RTC appeal.

V. QUALIFICATIONS, ELECTIONS, TERMS, AND VACANCIES — §§39–47

16. Positive qualifications and domicile — §39

General qualifications:

  • Philippine citizenship; naturalized Filipinos may qualify for ordinary local offices.
  • Registered voter in the relevant LGU or sanggunian district.
  • Residence/domicile for at least 1 year immediately preceding election day.
  • Ability to read and write Filipino or another local language/dialect.
  • Required age on election day, subject to the special SK law.
Office Minimum age on election day
Governor, vice-governor, provincial board member; HUC mayor, vice-mayor, councilor 23
ICC/component city/municipal mayor and vice-mayor 21
Non-HUC city councilor; municipal councilor; Punong Barangay; barangay member 18
Elected SK chairperson/member under R.A. 10742, amended by R.A. 11768 18–24 inclusive

SK special qualifications:

  • Philippine citizen and qualified Katipunan ng Kabataan voter.
  • Barangay resident for at least 1 year before election.
  • Read/write Filipino, English, or local dialect.
  • No prohibited relationship within 2nd civil degree of consanguinity/affinity to an incumbent elected regional/provincial/city/municipal/barangay official in the locality.
  • No final conviction for a crime involving moral turpitude.
  • Appointed SK secretary/treasurer: special age range 18–30 on appointment day; do not confuse this or KK voting ages with elected SK eligibility.

Residence = domicile: permanent home with intent to return (Co; Faypon; Abella). Temporary study/work absence need not abandon it; establishing a permanent home elsewhere may. No property qualification.

Change of domicile requires concurrence of:

  1. Actual physical presence/removal to the new place.
  2. Bona fide intent to abandon the old domicile and remain in the new one.
  3. Acts corresponding to that intention (Dumpit-Michelena; Romualdez-Marcos).

Trap: reaching the required age after election does not cure deficiency. Registration and domicile remain distinct requirements.

17. Seven disqualifications — §40

  • (a) Final sentence: offense involving moral turpitude OR punishable by at least 1 year imprisonment; disqualification during service and within 2 years after serving sentence.
  • (b) Removal from office resulting from an administrative case.
  • (c) Final conviction for violating the oath of allegiance to the Republic.
  • (d) Dual citizenship, interpreted through the dual-allegiance doctrine.
  • (e) Fugitive from justice in criminal/nonpolitical cases here or abroad.
  • (f) Foreign permanent residents, or persons continuing to avail of the acquired right to reside abroad after Code effectivity.
  • (g) Insane or feeble-minded, using the statutory terminology.

Doctrines:

  • Moral turpitude: baseness/vileness/depravity against justice, honesty, modesty, or morals; not automatically decided by mala in se/prohibita labels.
  • Moreno: valid probation suspends execution, not “service of sentence”; final discharge restores relevant civil rights without erasing conviction.
  • Grego: administrative-removal ground is prospective from January 1, 1992; not a universal date limit on §40.
  • Carpio-Morales/Binay: reelection does not prospectively condone administrative misconduct.
  • Mercado/Valles: involuntary dual citizenship differs from voluntary dual allegiance.
  • R.A. 9225 / Sobejana-Condon: allegiance oath AND personal sworn renunciation of all foreign citizenships at/before COC filing. Reacquisition alone is insufficient.
  • Maquiling/Arnado: subsequent foreign-passport use can undo renunciation.
  • Marquez/Rodriguez: fugitive includes flight after charge to evade prosecution; intent to evade is essential.
  • Caasi: COC filing alone does not prove waiver of foreign permanent residence.

18. Manner of election and terms — §§41–43

  • At large: governor/vice-governor, mayor/vice-mayor, Punong Barangay, and regular barangay members.
  • Regular provincial/city/municipal members: by district as provided by law; apply R.A. 7166/charters/apportionment, not an assumption that every town has multiple districts.
  • SK: elected by qualified KK voters.
  • Ex officio: Liga and SK federation presidents at the relevant higher level; provincial sanggunian also includes the appropriate councilors’ league president.
  • Sectoral seats: women; workers; one from urban poor, indigenous cultural communities, disabled persons, or another designated sector, as provided by law. Distinguish indigenous mandatory representation.
  • Provincial regular seats: traditional statutory brackets 10 / 8 / 6; provinces with more than 5 legislative districts elect 2 per district under R.A. 8553. Read income-class references with R.A. 11964 and the governing apportionment.

Ordinary provincial/city/municipal officials:

  • Elections: second Monday of May every 3 years, unless law provides otherwise.
  • Term: 3 years, beginning noon June 30 following election.
  • Term: legally fixed period; tenure: actual time held.
  • Congress cannot extend a constitutionally fixed term merely through holdover (Osmeña). Barangay terms are statutorily determined under the constitutional exception.

Three-term bar — both requisites must concur:

  1. Elected for 3 consecutive terms to the same position.
  2. Fully served those 3 consecutive terms.
Event Effect on counting
Vice-mayor succeeds as mayor by operation of law Succession period is not an elected mayoral term (Borja).
Voluntary resignation during a term Does not interrupt continuity.
Preventive suspension No interruption: title remains (Aldovino).
Invalid election/proclamation and involuntary ouster before expiration Apply the actual loss-of-title/election facts; Lonzanida found the term requirements absent.
Entire term served; election protest lost only afterward Counts as a full term under Ong v. Alegre.

Barangay/SK update — R.A. 12326 amending R.A. 12232:

  • Term: 5 years.
  • Barangay officials: no more than 2 consecutive terms in the same position.
  • SK officials: no more than 1 term in the same position.
  • Next regular election: second Monday of November 2028, every 5 years thereafter.
  • Assumption: December 1 following election under the retained assumption provision.
  • Apply transitory term counting; incumbents on their last allowable term cannot reset the count for 2028.

This statutory update replaces the notes’ older term rules; election-postponement legislation remains subject to constitutional review.

19. Permanent vacancies and succession — §§44–45

Vacancy: no person lawfully authorized presently to exercise the office’s duties (Menzon v. Petilla). Permanent vacancy arises when an official:

  • Fills a higher vacant office.
  • Refuses to assume.
  • Fails to qualify.
  • Dies.
  • Is removed.
  • Voluntarily resigns.
  • Is otherwise permanently incapacitated.

Correction: COC filing does not automatically resign an elective incumbent; R.A. 9006 repealed that former rule. Distinguish appointive officials.

Automatic succession, §44:

  • Governor/mayor vacancy → vice-governor/vice-mayor.
  • Vice-executive vacancy → highest-ranking eligible regular sanggunian member.
  • Both executive and vice-executive offices vacant → highest-ranking regular member takes executive office; next-ranked member takes vice-executive office.
  • Punong Barangay vacancy → highest-ranking regular barangay member, then next if permanently unable.
  • Subsequent vacancies follow ranking; ties by drawing lots.
  • Successor serves the unexpired term.

Ranking: votes obtained ÷ district’s total registered voters at preceding election. Not raw votes across unequal districts. Ex-officio representatives are not automatically in the regular-member succession line.

Appointment to the remaining sanggunian vacancy, §45:

Vacant sanggunian Appointing authority
Provincial; HUC/ICC city President through Executive Secretary
Component city; municipality Governor
Barangay City/municipal mayor, on barangay sanggunian recommendation

Party rules:

  • Preserve the party of the member whose elevation caused the last sanggunian vacancy.
  • Require nomination AND membership certification from the highest party official.
  • Appointment lacking these required documents: void ab initio; appointing official may face administrative action.
  • Independent predecessor: qualified replacement upon required sanggunian recommendation; use the proper §45 appointing authority (Fariñas v. Barba).
  • Barangay seats: recommendation, not partisan nomination.
  • Ex-officio youth/barangay representation: official next in rank in the organization.
  • SK chair vacancy: highest-vote eligible SK member succeeds under the special SK law. SK membership vacancy: city/municipal mayor appoints a qualified person under the new R.A. 12326 §4-A rule.

20. Temporary incapacity, OIC, and leave — §§46–47

  • Physical/legal incapacity, including leave, foreign travel, or suspension → vice-executive or highest-ranking barangay member automatically exercises executive functions.
  • Cannot appoint, suspend, or dismiss employees unless incapacity exceeds 30 working days.
  • Acting executive does not simultaneously preside over the sanggunian (Gamboa v. Aguirre); elect a temporary presiding officer.
  • Domestic travel outside jurisdiction for not more than 3 consecutive days → LCE may designate an OIC in writing, specifying powers; personnel appointment/suspension/dismissal excluded.
  • No authorization: successor may assume on 4th day, §46(d). Foreign travel/legal incapacity follows automatic assumption, not this waiting rule.
  • Outside the limited OIC exception, the LCE cannot bypass the statutory successor by installing personal staff.
  • Return: written declaration to the sanggunian; for legal incapacity, also proof the legal cause has ceased.

VI. LOCAL LEGISLATION — §§48–59

21. Legislative authority, presiding officers, and internal rules — §§48–50

LGU Sanggunian Presiding officer
Province Panlalawigan Vice-governor
City Panlungsod City vice-mayor
Municipality Bayan Municipal vice-mayor

Subordinate legislation: stay within granted powers/national law. Presumed validity cannot save a proven defect.

Six valid-ordinance tests (Lagcao):

  1. Does not contravene Constitution/statute.
  2. Is not unfair/oppressive.
  3. Is not partial/discriminatory.
  4. Does not prohibit, but may regulate, trade within the governing doctrine.
  5. Is general and consistent with public policy.
  6. Is not unreasonable.

Also require authority and proper enactment. No inherent contempt power over nonmembers (NORECO II); distinguish §53 attendance compulsion over members.

Presiding officer, §49:

  • Votes only to break a tie.
  • If unable to preside: members present, with quorum, elect a temporary presiding officer; highest ranking does not automatically take the chair merely because the regular officer is absent.
  • Temporary presiding officer certifies enacted/adopted measures within 10 days.

Internal rules, §50: take up on the first regular session after election and adopt/update within 90 days.

  • Organization/officers, standing committees and their jurisdictions.
  • Order/calendar of business.
  • Legislative process.
  • Parliamentary procedures and conduct.
  • Discipline.
  • Other administrative rules.

Six mandatory committee subjects:

  1. Appropriations.
  2. Women and family.
  3. Human rights.
  4. Youth and sports development.
  5. Environmental protection.
  6. Cooperatives.

Malonzo: rules update is not prerequisite to legislation; rules may permit 3 readings in one day. Readings ≠ sessions. Members control the agenda; secretary supports/records.

Discipline:

  • Grounds: disorderly behavior or 4 consecutive unjustified session absences.
  • Sanctions: censure, reprimand, exclusion, suspension up to 60 days, or expulsion.
  • Suspension/expulsion: at least 2/3 of all members.
  • Automatic expulsion: final conviction to imprisonment of at least 1 year for a crime involving moral turpitude. Here, distinguish the combined requirement from §40(a)’s alternative grounds.

22. Financial disclosure, sessions, and quorum — §§51–53

Disclosure, §51:

  • Full business/financial disclosure upon assumption.
  • Specific disclosure when a measure affects the member’s business, financial/professional relationship, or a relative within 4th civil degree of consanguinity/affinity.
  • Includes relevant investments, contracts, and conflicts of interest.
  • Disclose before deliberating, before voting on 2nd/3rd readings if not previously participating, or before the relevant privilege speech/formal position.
  • File in writing with sanggunian secretary or committee secretary; disclosure becomes part of the record.

Sessions, §52:

  • Fix regular day/time/place by resolution on the first session.
  • Province/city/municipality: at least weekly; barangay: at least twice monthly.
  • Special session: LCE or majority of members, when public interest requires.
  • Special notice: written date/time/purpose, served personally at usual residence at least 24 hours before.
  • Special agenda: stated items only, unless 2/3 of members present, with quorum, agree to others.
  • Normally public; close by majority of those present, with quorum, for public interest, security, decency, or morality.
  • No two regular/special sessions on the same day; keep a journal and record of proceedings.

Quorum, §53: majority of all elected and qualified members.

  • Formula: floor(N ÷ 2) + 1, not a fractional “half plus one.”
  • Include lawful ex-officio membership and the presiding officer in the proper membership count (La Carlota v. Rojo).
  • 9 members → 5; 11 → 6; 13 → 7.
  • Clarification: mere absence does not erase membership; Avelino does not automatically subtract every detained/suspended/overseas local member from §53’s denominator.
  • No quorum: roll call/recess or day-to-day adjournment; majority present may compel absent members’ attendance through a designated member assisted by police.
  • Without quorum, transact no other business.

Quorum ≠ passage threshold: lawful session does not guarantee the required majority/2/3 of all votes.

23. Ordinance approval and veto — §§54–55

Ordinance Resolution
Local legislative enactment, generally of continuing/general character. Expression/decision on a particular matter, often specific or temporary.
Governing legislative process requires 3 readings. Third reading ordinarily unnecessary unless the required majority orders it under the IRR.
Required where the Code expressly says ordinance, e.g., §19 expropriation. Cannot substitute merely because it obtained many votes.

Executive approval, §54:

  • Provincial/city/municipal ordinance presented to governor/mayor.
  • Express approval: sign each and every page.
  • Veto: return with written objections; sanggunian may reconsider.
  • Veto must be communicated within 15 days for governor / 10 days for mayor, from receipt; otherwise deemed approved.
  • Override: 2/3 of all members; executive signature no longer needed.
  • Barangay ordinance: majority of all barangay sanggunian members, signed by Punong Barangay; no separate executive veto.

De los Reyes v. Sandiganbayan: executive approval is discretionary, not merely ministerial. Gallego v. People: the lecture’s judicial-notice rule concerns municipal ordinances within the trial court’s jurisdiction; do not equate ordinances with national statutes for every court.

Veto grounds, §55:

  • Ultra vires—outside lawful legislative powers.
  • Prejudicial to public welfare.

Item veto permitted for:

  1. Appropriation ordinances.
  2. Ordinances/resolutions adopting local development plans and public investment programs.
  3. Ordinances directing payment of money or creating liability.

Only the objected-to items are affected. Unless overridden, corresponding prior-year appropriation items, if any, are deemed reenacted. The LCE may veto the measure only once.

Veto can assess welfare; supervisory review checks legality, not preferred policy.

  • §56 legal referral: provincial attorney/prosecutor gives comments within 10 days of receipt.
  • Moday: review checks legality/power, not policy; its older resolution facts do not displace §19’s ordinance requirement.
  • HUC/ICC ordinances: no provincial review, but still comply with enactment/effectivity rules and remain judicially reviewable.
  • §58: attempting to enforce an officially disapproved ordinance or the specified plan/program resolution is a ground for suspension or dismissal of the responsible official/employee.

Effectivity, §59:

  • Default: after 10 days from required posting, unless the measure specifies another lawful date.
  • Post at hall/capitol entrance + at least 2 other conspicuous local places.
  • Secretary causes posting within 5 days after approval, recording approval/posting dates.
  • Disseminate/post in Filipino or English AND the language understood by the majority.
  • Penal ordinance: publish the gist in a provincial newspaper of general circulation; if none, statutory posting in all cities/municipalities of the province.
  • HUC/ICC: publish main features once in a local newspaper, or another newspaper of general circulation if none exists locally, in addition to posting.

Penal safeguard, §511: 3 consecutive weeks’ posting plus publication where available, subject to its barangay exception. Default effectivity: day after publication or end of posting, whichever later; not automatically §59’s ordinary 10-day rule.

Tax route, §187: public hearing; DOJ appeal within 30 days from effectivity → decision within 60 days → court recourse within 30 days of decision/decision-period lapse. Appeal does not suspend collection/effectivity. Correction: not 60 days from publication.

Separate: authority/procedure → approval → review → posting/publication/effectivity. Higher review does not automatically suspend every ordinance pending action.

26. Ten quick applications — cover the answers first

  1. A governor dislikes a lawful municipal traffic schedule. May he replace it with his preferred schedule?
  2. A barangay issues official receipts for clearance fees, but no ordinance authorizes the charge. Is the collection valid?
  3. A municipality meets cityhood income and population requirements but has only 70 km². Is the land shortfall necessarily fatal?
  4. A barangay expropriates a lot pursuant to a resolution approved unanimously. Is that enough?
  5. An ordinance reclassifies agricultural land as commercial. Can the owner ignore required DAR conversion approval?
  6. An uncovered national-road manhole injures a pedestrian. What fact matters more than LGU ownership?
  7. A vice-mayor succeeds as mayor, then wins two mayoral elections. Is another elected term automatically barred?
  8. The mayor is abroad, and names a personal secretary as acting mayor. Does the short domestic-travel exception apply?
  9. The vice-mayor is temporarily absent from a session. Does the highest-ranking councilor automatically preside?
  10. A provincial board finds a valid municipal ordinance unwise. Is that enough to invalidate it? Does approval alone make a penal ordinance enforceable?

Answer key:

  • 1: No. General supervision checks legality; it does not substitute policy judgment (§§25, 29–30).
  • 2: No. A receipt does not supply the missing legislative authority (§132).
  • 3: No. Under the applicable §450 route, qualifying population is an alternative to land area; verify all remaining requirements.
  • 4: No. §19 requires an ordinance, even for barangays.
  • 5: No. Reclassification does not waive independently required conversion approval.
  • 6: Control or supervision of the defective public work, plus injury and causation (Art. 2189).
  • 7: No. The succession portion is not an elected mayoral term (Borja).
  • 8: No. Foreign travel triggers the statutory acting successor; the ≤3-day OIC exception concerns domestic travel (§46).
  • 9: No. With quorum, elect a temporary presiding officer (§49).
  • 10: Policy unwisdom is insufficient for §56 review; enforceability separately requires the applicable posting/publication and effectivity conditions (§§59, 511).

For an ALAC answer: give the result, cite the exact governing provision/doctrine, identify the decisive facts, and conclude. For succession problems, identify permanent or temporary first. For ordinance problems, identify authority, procedure, vote, approval, review, and effectivity separately.
