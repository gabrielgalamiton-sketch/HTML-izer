PART 1 — FOUNDATIONS OF ARRAIGNMENT AND PRE-PLEA REMEDIES

Part 1 Overview & Scope of Topics: I. Arraignment — constitutional rights, duties, and courtroom mechanics; II. Motion to Quash — purpose, timing, procedural requirements, and distinction from other remedies.

I. ARRAIGNMENT: THE ACCUSED MUST UNDERSTAND THE CHARGE BEFORE ANSWERING IT

A. The big picture: Why is arraignment necessary?

Imagine this courtroom situation.

Pedro is arrested and charged with Murder. He is brought before the Regional Trial Court. The clerk reads several paragraphs from the Information, immediately asks whether Pedro is guilty, and records his answer.

But Pedro does not understand English. He does not understand what treachery means. He does not have a lawyer.

Can we consider his arraignment valid merely because he physically appeared in court?

No. Physical appearance and the mechanical reading of a charge do not necessarily constitute a valid arraignment.

Understand it first

Arraignment is the point in a criminal prosecution where the court formally tells the accused what crime the State alleges he committed and asks him how he pleads.

It has two connected purposes:

  1. Information: The accused must understand the nature and cause of the accusation.
  2. Response: The accused must be given the opportunity to enter a plea to that accusation.

This implements the constitutional right under Article III, Section 14(2), of the 1987 Constitution.

State it correctly — Rule 116, Section 1(a)

The arraignment must be conducted:

  • Before the court where the complaint or Information was filed or assigned for trial.
  • In open court, by the judge or clerk.
  • By furnishing the accused with a copy of the complaint or Information.
  • By reading the charge in a language or dialect known to the accused.
  • By asking whether the accused pleads guilty or not guilty.

Under Section 1(b), the accused must personally enter the plea, and the arraignment and plea must be made of record.

Why this matters: An arraignment that fails to afford the accused meaningful notice or counsel may violate due process and invalidate the resulting conviction. The consequences must be evaluated according to the nature of the defect and the proceedings.

B. The court's mandatory duty to provide counsel

Rule 116, Sections 6–8

The professor emphasizes the responsibility of the judge when an accused appears without legal representation.

Consider this dialogue:

Judge: Do you have a lawyer?
Accused: No, Your Honor. I cannot afford one.
Judge: Very well. How do you plead?

That is improper. The court must first address the accused's right to counsel.

Under Rule 116, Section 6, the court must inform the accused of his right to counsel and ask whether he desires counsel of his choice. Where the accused cannot secure counsel, the court must appoint counsel de oficio, subject to the rules allowing competent self-representation.

In practice, a lawyer from the Public Attorney's Office may be appointed.

But appointment is not the end of the obligation.

Rule 116, Section 8 requires the court to allow counsel de oficio reasonable time to consult privately with the accused before arraignment.

A lawyer who has just been assigned to the case must have a meaningful opportunity to understand the accusation and advise the accused.

Recitation point: The right to counsel is a substantive protection, not a ceremonial appointment of an attorney to satisfy the minutes of a hearing.

C. Language, translation, and qualifying circumstances

Rule 116, Section 1(a); People v. Solar

Suppose an accused from La Union understands Ilocano but has limited proficiency in English.

The clerk reads:

That the accused, with treachery and evident premeditation, did willfully, unlawfully, and feloniously attack and kill the victim.

The accused hears the word killed and understands that somebody died. But does he understand the particular kind of unlawful killing alleged?

Not necessarily.

The Information alleges Murder, rather than simply Homicide, because it identifies qualifying circumstances.

The Supreme Court in People v. Solar, G.R. No. 225595, emphasized that an accused must be sufficiently informed of the qualifying or aggravating circumstances alleged against him. Arraignment is an appropriate occasion to clarify those allegations.

Two important examples from the lecture

1. Treachery (alevosia)

Under Article 14(16) of the Revised Penal Code, treachery involves employing means, methods, or forms of execution that directly and specially ensure the execution of the crime without risk arising from the defense the offended party might make.

For the accused, the explanation should convey the essence of an attack deliberately executed in a manner that deprives the victim of an effective opportunity to defend himself.

An unexpected attack is not automatically treacherous. The required method of execution must be established.

2. Evident premeditation (premeditacion conocida)

A mere desire to kill is insufficient.

The doctrine ordinarily requires proof of:

  1. The time when the accused determined to commit the crime.
  2. An act manifestly indicating persistence in that determination.
  3. Sufficient time between determination and execution to allow reflection.

The accused must therefore understand that the prosecution alleges prior, deliberate planning, not merely a spontaneous confrontation.

What about Reckless Imprudence?

Article 365 of the RPC involves a different principle.

The prosecution alleges that the accused's voluntary act or omission, without malice, caused harm because of an inexcusable lack of precaution.

For example, the court interpreter might explain to an Ilocano-speaking driver that he is accused of causing an accident because he failed to exercise the care required under the circumstances.

Professor's practical lesson: Translating the words is insufficient if the accused still does not understand their legal significance.

The court must ensure meaningful comprehension. The precise wording, language, and method of interpretation may vary, but the constitutional protection remains the same.

D. What if the accused refuses to plead?

Rule 116, Section 1(c)

Imagine the following exchange:

Judge: How do you plead?
Accused: I refuse to answer.

Or:

Accused: I am guilty, provided the court releases me immediately.

What should the judge do?

The Rule provides:

When the accused refuses to plead or makes a conditional plea, a plea of not guilty shall be entered for him.

The court cannot treat the accused's silence as a confession.

Why?

Because a guilty plea must be legally sufficient, while the accused remains presumed innocent until proven guilty beyond reasonable doubt.

Accused's response Procedural consequence
Unconditional plea of Not Guilty Not Guilty is entered.
Refuses to plead Not Guilty is entered.
Makes a conditional plea Not Guilty is entered.
Unequivocally pleads Guilty Court applies the safeguards appropriate to the offense and plea.

Memory aid: Silence is not surrender. A refusal to plead results in a Not Guilty plea.

E. Suspension of arraignment: When may the court postpone it?

Rule 116, Section 11

The professor discusses several reasons why counsel might request the resetting or suspension of arraignment.

A particularly important distinction is that not every procedural request is an enumerated ground under Section 11.

The Rule expressly provides three grounds.

Additional procedures discussed in the lecture

The lecture also refers to a motion for preliminary investigation and a motion for a bill of particulars. These can affect the arraignment schedule, but they should not be memorized as two additional express grounds in Rule 116, Section 11.

A motion for a bill of particulars, under Rule 116, Section 9, asks the prosecution to supply necessary factual detail enabling the accused properly to plead and prepare for trial.

An accused who was unlawfully deprived of a preliminary investigation to which he was entitled may also seek appropriate relief before entering a plea.

Further, Rule 116, Section 1(g) excludes periods when a motion to quash, a motion for bill of particulars, or another justified cause suspends arraignment from the ordinary arraignment time computation.

The important correction concerning a copy of the Information

The source suggests that the court has no obligation to furnish a copy at arraignment.

That proposition needs qualification.

Rule 116, Section 1(a) expressly requires furnishing the accused with a copy of the complaint or Information. There is a distinction between demanding advance service on counsel as an independent precondition and the accused's mandatory right to receive a copy as part of the arraignment procedure.

A judge may reject an unjustified request for postponement, but cannot disregard the Rule's actual requirements.

F. Summary procedure versus regular procedure

The professor next contrasts the remedies available in regular criminal cases with those governed by summary procedure.

Understand it first

The same procedural motion is not necessarily available in every kind of criminal case.

Summary procedure exists to resolve covered cases expeditiously, minimizing unnecessary pleadings.

Under the 2022 Rules on Expedited Procedures in the First Level Courts, A.M. No. 08-8-7-SC, the criminal coverage includes specified categories such as traffic, rental-law, ordinance, and B.P. Blg. 22 violations, as well as other offenses within prescribed penalty thresholds.

For other covered offenses, the general threshold is imprisonment not exceeding one year or a fine not exceeding ₱50,000, or both, subject to the Rules' qualification on imprisonment and special provision for damage to property through criminal negligence. These Rules took effect on April 11, 2022, and apply prospectively.

Can the accused file a Motion to Quash?

Regular criminal procedure 2022 summary procedure
The accused may move to quash based on any applicable Rule 117, Section 3 ground. A motion to quash is generally prohibited.
Nine enumerated grounds are available. Only two exceptions are expressly permitted.
Pre-plea objections are governed by Rule 117. Special expedited rules prevail where inconsistent with the regular rules.

Under Rule II, Section 2(a) of the 2022 Expedited Rules, the two exceptions are:

  1. Lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter.
  2. Failure to comply with the barangay conciliation requirement under R.A. No. 7160, where applicable.

A motion for a bill of particulars is likewise among the prohibited pleadings under these expedited procedures.

Why is barangay conciliation important?

Consider two neighbors whose dispute falls within the authority of the Katarungang Pambarangay system.

If the law requires prior barangay conciliation, the parties ordinarily must first pursue that procedure before going to court.

But barangay conciliation is a condition precedent, not the source of the trial court's subject-matter jurisdiction.

That distinction matters because failure to comply with a condition precedent ordinarily produces a procedural objection which may be waived, whereas true subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be created by agreement or waiver.

The professor also contrasts the ability of courts under summary procedure to examine and dismiss defective cases outright with the defense's duty to raise waivable objections under regular procedure.

Study the principle carefully: the court's authority to dismiss a case on its own initiative must be grounded in the applicable rule or an independently recognized ground for dismissal. Do not assume that the absence of a Certification to File Action automatically authorizes dismissal at any stage in every procedural setting.

II. MOTION TO QUASH: CHALLENGING THE ACCUSATION BEFORE THE TRIAL BEGINS

A. What is a Motion to Quash?

Rule 117, Sections 1–3

Suppose Pedro is charged with a crime. His lawyer examines the Information and discovers that the facts alleged, even if entirely true, do not constitute an offense.

Must Pedro undergo a full trial before challenging the charge?

No. His lawyer may file a Motion to Quash.

A Motion to Quash is a procedural remedy filed before the accused enters a plea, challenging the complaint or Information on the grounds enumerated in Rule 117, Section 3.

The legal logic

There are two very different questions:

  • Is the accusation legally sufficient to support a prosecution?
  • Can the prosecution prove the accused guilty?

A Motion to Quash ordinarily addresses the first question, not the second.

This is why factual arguments requiring the court to weigh trial evidence generally do not belong in an ordinary motion to quash.

Under Rule 117, Section 1, the motion must be filed before the accused enters his plea.

This is crucial because Section 9 generally treats defects not raised before plea as waived, except for four specified grounds:

  • Section 3(a): Facts charged do not constitute an offense.
  • Section 3(b): Court lacks jurisdiction over the offense.
  • Section 3(g): Criminal action or liability has been extinguished.
  • Section 3(i): Previous jeopardy for the same offense.

We will examine each ground individually in Part 2.

C. Form and contents of the Motion to Quash

Rule 117, Section 2

A motion to quash must:

  1. Be in writing.
  2. Be signed by the accused or counsel.
  3. Distinctly specify its factual and legal grounds.

The court generally considers only the grounds stated in the motion, except lack of jurisdiction over the offense charged.

Professor's practical emphasis: A vague pleading alleging that an Information is legally defective is not a substitute for identifying the actual defect and its statutory basis.

The lecture also mentions a mandatory three-day hearing notice under Rule 15. This should not be treated as a universally fixed modern rule for every criminal motion. The 2019 amendments to the Rules of Civil Procedure changed the handling of litigious motions, including discretionary hearings. Counsel must observe the applicable criminal procedural rules, service requirements, and relevant court issuances.

D. Motion to Quash versus Demurrer to Evidence

This is one of the most examinable distinctions in the lecture.

Think of the proceedings as two checkpoints.

At the first checkpoint, the defense asks:

"Is the accusation legally defective?"

At the second checkpoint, after the prosecution presents evidence, the defense asks:

"Has the prosecution presented enough evidence to convict?"

Point of comparison Motion to Quash Demurrer to Evidence
Legal basis Rule 117 Rule 119, Sec. 23
Timing Before entry of plea After prosecution rests
Main concern Defects in the complaint, Information, or legal basis for prosecution Insufficiency of prosecution evidence
Leave of court Not required May be filed with or without leave
If granted Generally allows amendment or another prosecution, subject to exceptions Amounts to acquittal
Double jeopardy Generally does not arise from pre-plea quashal Generally bars further prosecution
If denied Case proceeds Effect depends on whether leave was obtained

The danger of demurring without leave

Suppose the prosecution finishes presenting its witnesses. Defense counsel is confident the evidence is insufficient.

He files a Demurrer to Evidence without leave of court.

The judge denies the demurrer.

What happens?

The accused waives the right to present defense evidence.

However, if counsel obtained leave before filing the demurrer, and it is denied, the accused may still present defense evidence.

Practice takeaway: The decision whether to seek leave is strategic. Requesting leave ordinarily protects the opportunity to present a defense, but counsel must observe the applicable period and the court's action on the request.

E. Absence of preliminary investigation and prosecutor's certification

Rule 112; Rule 117; Crespo v. Mogul

Situation: The prosecutor files an Information against Pedro without first providing a preliminary investigation, even though Pedro was entitled to one.

Pedro's lawyer argues that the Information must be quashed immediately.

Is this the proper remedy?

Generally, no.

Preliminary investigation serves to determine whether sufficient grounds exist to bring the accused to trial. It is an important statutory protection, but its absence does not automatically deprive the trial court of jurisdiction or invalidate an otherwise sufficient Information.

The defense ordinarily seeks an appropriate preliminary investigation and the corresponding suspension of proceedings before entering a plea, rather than relying on Rule 117 as though every deprivation of preliminary investigation were an independent ground for quashal.

Recall also Rule 114, Section 26: Posting bail does not itself bar the accused from questioning an illegal arrest or the absence or irregularity of preliminary investigation, provided the objection is raised before plea.

What does Crespo v. Mogul teach?

Once an Information is filed in court, the disposition of the criminal case becomes subject to the court's judicial authority.

The judge is not automatically bound by the prosecutor's subsequent decision or a DOJ directive to withdraw the charge.

The court must independently evaluate whether the case should continue or be dismissed.

What about a missing prosecutor's certification?

The absence of the investigating prosecutor's certification is not, by itself, an additional enumerated ground under Rule 117, Section 3.

Distinguish:

  • A missing or defective certification concerning the investigation.
  • An Information filed by an officer without legal authority.

They are not necessarily the same defect. The second may implicate Rule 117, Section 3(d), which we discuss in Part 2.

Part 1 recall checkpoint: Before filing a motion, identify what is defective, which remedy addresses it, and whether the accused has already pleaded.

PART 2 — THE NINE GROUNDS FOR QUASHAL AND DOUBLE JEOPARDY

Part 2 Overview & Scope of Topics: III. The nine grounds under Rule 117, Section 3, their legal consequences, and waiver; IV. Constitutional double jeopardy, its requisites, effect of acquittal, and appellate standing.

III. THE NINE GROUNDS FOR A MOTION TO QUASH

A. First understand the organizing principle

A Motion to Quash is not a general invitation for the accused to present every possible defense.

Rule 117, Section 3 identifies nine specific grounds, labeled (a) through (i).

Think of them as nine possible reasons why the prosecution should not proceed on the existing accusation.

Ground What is being challenged? Waivable upon plea?
(a) Facts charged do not constitute an offense No
(b) Court lacks jurisdiction over the offense No
(c) Court lacks jurisdiction over the accused's person Yes
(d) Officer filing the Information lacked authority Yes
(e) Information does not substantially conform to the prescribed form Yes
(f) More than one offense is improperly charged Yes
(g) Criminal action or liability has been extinguished No
(h) Averments show a legal excuse or justification Yes
(i) Previous jeopardy for the same offense No

The governing waiver classification comes directly from Rule 117, Section 9.

B. Section 3(a): Facts charged do not constitute an offense

This ground is best understood through a hypothetical.

Situation: Pedro is charged with Theft.

The Information alleges that Pedro took his own bicycle, which Juan had merely borrowed, and returned it to his house.

Pedro argues that the facts do not constitute Theft because the property described in the Information is his own.

Step 1: Hypothetically admit the allegations

For this particular motion, Pedro does not need to establish through evidence that he owns the bicycle.

Instead, his argument is:

Even assuming everything written in the Information is true, those facts do not satisfy the elements of Theft.

This is the hypothetical admission rule.

The court generally evaluates the sufficiency of the allegations, not whether the prosecution can prove them.

Step 2: Apply the four-corners test

The judge examines the Information itself.

He asks whether the allegations, if accepted as true, constitute the offense charged.

The judge should not resolve factual disputes by weighing Pedro's anticipated evidence or Juan's contradictory version.

Matters aliunde refers to matters outside the pleading or other relevant record. Such extraneous factual matters ordinarily cannot be used to transform this ground into a premature trial.

Step 3: Consider amendment

What if the prosecutor simply forgot to allege an essential element?

Rule 117, Section 4 provides an important safeguard:

If the defect can be corrected by amendment, the court must afford the prosecution the opportunity to correct it.

If the prosecution fails to amend, or the amended Information still suffers from the same fatal defect, the motion may be granted.

Professor's practical lesson: Successfully attacking a defective Information is not necessarily the same as permanently defeating the prosecution. The Information may be corrected and the criminal case may continue.

C. Section 3(b): Court has no jurisdiction over the offense

Subject-matter jurisdiction

Jurisdiction over the offense means the court's legal authority to hear and decide that category of criminal case.

Example

An Information for Murder is filed before an ordinary Municipal Trial Court.

Can the accused and the prosecutor agree to let the MTC proceed because the RTC is inconveniently located?

No.

Subject-matter jurisdiction is conferred by law, not by agreement, consent, convenience, or the failure of a litigant to object.

The general allocation of criminal jurisdiction under B.P. Blg. 129, as amended, includes offenses punishable by imprisonment not exceeding six years within the first-level courts' ordinary criminal jurisdiction, subject to important statutory exceptions and special jurisdictional rules.

The RTC generally exercises original jurisdiction over criminal offenses beyond the first-level courts' jurisdiction, again subject to special laws.

Two concepts often confused

Jurisdiction over the offense Territorial jurisdiction
Asks which class of court may try the offense. Asks where the criminal action may properly be instituted and tried.
Commonly depends on the charge and the penalty prescribed by law. Generally depends on where the offense or an essential ingredient occurred, subject to statutory exceptions.

For criminal prosecutions, territorial jurisdiction is an important jurisdictional requirement, not merely a matter of convenience.

Application: A case filed in a court that fundamentally lacks jurisdiction over the offense cannot become valid simply because the accused enters a plea.

This is one reason Section 3(b) is non-waivable.

D. Section 3(c): No jurisdiction over the person of the accused

Now change the example.

Pedro is arrested without a warrant. The officers did not witness him committing an offense, and the circumstances did not satisfy any exception authorizing a warrantless arrest.

Pedro wants to challenge the legality of his arrest.

The relevant issue is jurisdiction over the person, which differs from jurisdiction over the offense.

Jurisdiction over the accused may be acquired through arrest or voluntary submission to the court.

What if Pedro pleads Not Guilty without objecting?

He generally waives objections to the irregularity of his arrest and submits to the court's jurisdiction over his person.

However, an illegal arrest does not, by itself, extinguish the criminal charge. The appropriate relief depends on whether the accused challenges the arrest, the court's acquisition of jurisdiction over him, or evidence obtained during an unlawful search.

The critically important distinction

Suppose the officers seized an unlicensed firearm during the illegal arrest.

Pedro participated in arraignment without questioning his arrest.

Has he also waived his constitutional objection to the firearm's admissibility?

No.

The waiver of an objection to an illegal arrest does not automatically waive the exclusionary rule governing evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search or seizure.

The Supreme Court reiterated this distinction in Patotoy v. People, G.R. No. 257910, decided in March 2025.

Keep these two questions separate:

  • Jurisdiction: Can the court validly exercise authority over this accused?
  • Admissibility: May the prosecution constitutionally use the seized evidence?

The answer to one does not automatically determine the other.

Even when the seized evidence is inadmissible, acquittal follows only if the remaining admissible evidence is insufficient to establish guilt.

E. Section 3(d): Officer filing the Information had no authority

An Information must be filed by an officer authorized by law.

The lecture emphasizes the necessary authority or approval of the proper prosecuting official.

Imagine an assistant prosecutor signing and filing an Information without the approval required by the applicable rules and governing prosecutorial authority.

The accused may invoke Section 3(d).

The key issue is whether the officer had legal authority to institute the prosecution.

Do not confuse this with the mere omission of a certification concerning the preliminary investigation.

Defect Legal significance
Missing investigating prosecutor's certification Ordinarily a procedural irregularity, not necessarily fatal to the Information.
Information filed by an officer legally unauthorized to file it Potential ground for quashal under Sec. 3(d).

Important qualification: Because Section 3(d) is not among the exceptions in Section 9, it is generally waivable if not raised before plea. The source's description of the defect as categorically incurable after arraignment should not override the express waiver provision.

F. Section 3(e): Information does not substantially conform to prescribed form

Under Rule 110, the complaint or Information must comply substantially with the governing requirements concerning its contents.

These include the identification of the accused, designation of the offense, statement of the acts or omissions complained of, offended party, approximate date, and place of commission, as applicable.

Example

An Information alleges an offense without sufficiently stating the accused's criminal acts or omissions.

Is it necessarily valid because the prosecutor placed the correct statutory title at the top?

No.

The allegations must adequately inform the accused of what he must defend against.

However, not every clerical mistake invalidates an Information.

Under Rule 117, Section 4, curable defects should be addressed through amendment.

The legal distinction is between a defect that prevents compliance with substantial requirements and a minor irregularity that does not prejudice the accused's protected rights.

G. Section 3(f): More than one offense is charged

This is called duplicity of offenses.

General rule

Under Rule 110, Section 13, an Information must charge only one offense, except when the law prescribes a single punishment for various offenses.

Example: An Information improperly combines independent offenses of Theft and Estafa involving distinct criminal acts, even though no applicable rule authorizes charging them together.

That Information may be challenged for duplicity before plea.

But there are recognized exceptions

For example, the law recognizes certain complex or special composite crimes in which multiple criminal acts or results are legally treated together.

Article 48 of the RPC deals with complex crimes, while certain special complex crimes, such as Robbery with Homicide, are governed by their specific statutory provisions.

The lecture also discusses reckless imprudence under Article 365. That quasi-offense has a separate doctrinal treatment because criminal negligence arising from one act generally cannot be fragmented into different prosecutions according to its various consequences.

We will examine this under Ivler in Part 3.

What if the accused fails to object?

Duplicity is a waivable defect.

If the accused enters a plea without timely objecting, the court may proceed, and a conviction for multiple offenses sufficiently alleged and proven may result, subject to the governing law.

Recitation shortcut: One offense per Information is the rule; a legally authorized combination is the exception.

H. Section 3(g): Criminal action or liability has been extinguished

This ground protects against prosecution where the State's right to prosecute or the accused's criminal liability has already been extinguished.

The lecture principally discusses prescription.

Understand prescription through a timeline

Suppose a particular offense has a prescriptive period established by law.

If that period expires without an event that legally interrupts prescription, the criminal action may already be extinguished.

The accused may invoke that extinction as a ground to quash.

But the calculation is not always as simple as subtracting the commission date from the filing date.

Depending on the offense and governing statute, one must determine:

  • When prescription began running.
  • Which prescriptive period applies.
  • Whether and when prescription was interrupted.
  • Whether a legally relevant event caused the period to run again.

The applicable provisions may include Articles 89–91 of the RPC or Act No. 3326 for offenses under special laws, subject to jurisprudential qualifications.

Is prescription waivable under Rule 117?

No. Section 3(g) is among the exceptions under Section 9.

That does not eliminate the need to establish the factual and legal basis of prescription.

It simply means the objection is not lost merely because the accused entered a plea without previously raising it.

I. Section 3(h): Information itself alleges a legal excuse or justification

This provision is frequently misunderstood.

Example: Pedro is charged with homicide.

The Information itself alleges that Pedro was attacked unlawfully, used reasonably necessary means to defend himself, and did not sufficiently provoke the aggression.

Those allegations, if legally sufficient, might disclose a justifying circumstance on the face of the Information.

Contrast that with a different situation:

The Information simply alleges that Pedro deliberately stabbed Juan. Pedro claims that Juan attacked first, but the Information contains no such allegation.

Can the court grant a Motion to Quash based solely on Pedro's unproven assertion of self-defense?

No.

The alleged justification depends on contested facts that must ordinarily be established through evidence.

Three related concepts

Circumstance Relevant provision Basic idea
Justifying Article 11, RPC The act is considered justified under the legally required circumstances.
Exempting Article 12, RPC Criminal liability is excluded despite conduct otherwise falling within an offense.
Absolutory cause Specific provisions The law exempts the actor from the ordinary penal consequence in the specified circumstances.

The lecture mentions accident under Article 12(4), Article 247's exceptional circumstances, and Battered Woman Syndrome under R.A. No. 9262.

These have distinct requisites. They should not be treated as interchangeable defenses.

Important: Article 247 does not simply make every retaliatory killing or injury between spouses excusable. Its specific statutory conditions must be satisfied.

Similarly, Battered Woman Syndrome involves statutory and evidentiary requirements; it cannot ordinarily be resolved merely because the accused invokes it in a motion.

J. Section 3(i): Previous conviction, acquittal, or dismissal without consent

This is the ground involving double jeopardy.

It is founded on Article III, Section 21 of the Constitution and will be studied in detail immediately below.

For the moment, remember that the State cannot repeatedly prosecute a person for the same offense once the constitutional requirements for former jeopardy have been satisfied.

K. Arguments that do not automatically justify quashal

The professor identifies two recurring courtroom situations.

1. Affidavit of Desistance

Juan files a criminal complaint against Pedro. Later, they reconcile, and Juan executes an Affidavit of Desistance.

Does the affidavit automatically extinguish the criminal action?

No.

A criminal prosecution is generally prosecuted in the name of the People of the Philippines, under the direction and control of the public prosecutor.

A private complainant's change of mind does not, by itself, remove the State's authority to prosecute a public offense.

The affidavit may affect the prosecution's assessment of its available evidence. But its evidentiary value, the nature of the offense, and the stage of proceedings all matter.

A statement of desistance is not an independent Rule 117, Section 3 ground.

2. Judicial determination of probable cause

The lecture describes a gambling prosecution in which the police arrive after the alleged participants have left. Their evidence of the offense is weak.

The judge may independently examine the prosecutor's resolution and supporting evidence under the applicable procedure for judicial determination of probable cause.

This is different from the accused's pre-plea Motion to Quash.

However, lack of probable cause is not the same as lack of evidence beyond reasonable doubt.

IV. DOUBLE JEOPARDY: WHEN THE STATE HAS ALREADY HAD ITS OPPORTUNITY

A. Constitutional foundation

Article III, Section 21 of the 1987 Constitution protects an accused against being twice put in jeopardy of punishment for the same offense.

Understand it first

Imagine Pedro is validly prosecuted for Theft, tried, and acquitted.

The prosecutor is dissatisfied with the judgment and decides to file the same Theft case again, hoping a different judge will convict Pedro.

The Constitution generally prohibits that second attempt.

The reason is not simply judicial efficiency.

It is to prevent the State from continually exposing a person to the anxiety, expense, harassment, and danger of repeated criminal prosecutions for the same offense.

B. The requisites of double jeopardy

A useful way to memorize the doctrine is to divide its elements into first jeopardy and second jeopardy.

The first proceeding must have satisfied these conditions:

  1. A court of competent jurisdiction.
  2. A valid complaint or Information or other sufficient formal charge.
  3. The accused was validly arraigned and pleaded.
  4. The accused was convicted, acquitted, or the case was dismissed or otherwise terminated without his express consent, subject to recognized exceptions.

Then the subsequent prosecution must be for the same offense, an attempt or frustration thereof, or an offense necessarily including or included in the former charge.

Why is the plea so important?

Suppose the court dismisses a case before Pedro's arraignment because the prosecutor has insufficient evidence to establish probable cause.

Can Pedro automatically invoke double jeopardy if a legally permissible new prosecution is subsequently instituted?

No.

The requirement of a valid arraignment and plea was absent.

The first proceeding had not reached the point necessary for constitutional jeopardy to attach.

C. What is the effect of an acquittal?

As a general rule, a judgment of acquittal is immediately final and unappealable.

The State cannot ordinarily appeal simply because it believes the trial judge evaluated the evidence incorrectly.

Even an acquittal based on an erroneous appreciation of the evidence is generally protected.

But there is a narrow distinction:

A judgment issued through a sham proceeding or such grave abuse of discretion that the prosecution was denied due process may be void. In appropriate circumstances, extraordinary review under Rule 65 may be available.

This is not an ordinary appeal on the merits.

D. Yasa v. Chavez: Who can challenge an acquittal?

Yasa v. Chavez, G.R. No. 273948, June 23, 2026

The lecture cites Yasa v. Chavez, but gives its date as June 23, 2021. The Supreme Court's published decision confirms that the correct date is June 23, 2026.

Material facts

Francisco Yasa was the private complainant in cyberlibel-related prosecutions against Mary Jean Chavez and Beberly Cuasito.

The trial court granted the accused's Demurrer to Evidence, finding the prosecution's electronic evidence inadequate.

The ruling resulted in their acquittal.

Yasa attempted to challenge the ruling without the conformity of the Office of the Solicitor General.

Issue

May a private complainant independently challenge an acquittal in order to revive the criminal prosecution?

Ruling

No, under the circumstances presented.

The Supreme Court emphasized that the criminal action belongs to the State. Representation of the People before the Court of Appeals and Supreme Court is vested in the Office of the Solicitor General.

A private complainant cannot independently overturn a criminal acquittal merely by arguing that the evidence should have been appreciated differently.

Important distinction: Criminal versus civil aspect

The private offended party may have standing to question the civil aspect of the judgment.

However, the survival of civil liability depends on the basis of the acquittal and the applicable civil-law principles.

For example, an acquittal expressly based on the finding that the act or omission from which civil liability might arise did not exist may also extinguish civil liability ex delicto.

Qualification from Austria v. AAA and BBB

The Supreme Court's 2022 Austria decision established guidelines on the private offended party's standing, the need for OSG conformity, and exceptional petitions alleging grave abuse of discretion or denial of due process.

Accordingly, it is too broad to say that a private complainant can never file any Rule 65 petition touching a criminal proceeding. The nature of the challenge and the OSG's required participation are decisive.

E. The accused's express consent to dismissal

What happens if Pedro himself asks the court to dismiss his case?

As a general rule, a dismissal with the accused's express consent does not qualify as a termination that bars a second prosecution on double-jeopardy grounds.

But the professor highlights two important exceptions:

1. Demurrer to Evidence

Even though the accused files the demurrer, its grant is an acquittal for insufficiency of evidence.

A subsequent prosecution for the same offense is ordinarily barred.

2. Dismissal for violation of the right to speedy trial

An accused may seek dismissal because of an established violation of the constitutional right to a speedy trial.

When the resulting dismissal constitutes a termination protected by double jeopardy, the State cannot circumvent it by refiling the same charge.

The mere fact that the accused requested dismissal does not remove these protections.

Recitation takeaway: Express consent normally defeats the double-jeopardy defense arising from dismissal, but a dismissal equivalent to acquittal — including a granted demurrer or qualifying speedy-trial dismissal — is treated differently.

V. ADVANCED APPLICATIONS OF DOUBLE JEOPARDY

A. Ivler v. Modesto-San Pedro: One negligent act, several consequences

Ivler v. Modesto-San Pedro, G.R. No. 172716, November 17, 2010 | Carpio, J.

This is among the most important cases discussed in your lecture.

Understand the problem

Imagine that Pedro carelessly drives through an intersection.

As a result of the same collision:

  • Passenger A sustains slight physical injuries.
  • Passenger B dies.
  • Another vehicle is damaged.

The prosecutor files three separate Informations against Pedro, dividing the accident according to its consequences.

Pedro pleads guilty to the first charge involving slight physical injuries.

Can the State continue prosecuting him for the death and property damage arising from the same negligent act?

The answer is explained by Ivler.

Material facts of the case

Jason Ivler was involved in a vehicular accident that caused injuries to Evangeline Ponce, the death of her husband Nestor Ponce, and damage to their vehicle.

Two Informations were filed:

  1. Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Slight Physical Injuries.
  2. Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Homicide and Damage to Property.

Ivler pleaded guilty to the first charge and received the penalty of public censure.

He then invoked double jeopardy against the second prosecution.

Issue

Does a conviction for reckless imprudence resulting in slight physical injuries bar a separate prosecution for reckless imprudence resulting in homicide and damage to property arising from the same incident?

Ruling

Yes. The second prosecution was barred by double jeopardy.

The Supreme Court held that reckless imprudence under Article 365 of the RPC is itself a distinct quasi-offense.

It is not merely a manner of committing homicide, physical injuries, or damage to property.

The resulting harms are material to the applicable penalty, but they do not automatically create separate crimes of reckless imprudence for every injured person or damaged object arising from the same negligent act.

Why does Article 48 not solve this by creating a complex crime?

Article 48 addresses complex crimes under its own statutory conditions.

But Ivler teaches that an Article 365 quasi-offense has a distinct legal character.

The prosecution cannot avoid double jeopardy by fragmenting one act of reckless imprudence into several independently prosecuted offenses.

Bar examination lesson: Identify the punishable act of negligence first. Do not assume that every harmful consequence necessarily represents a different criminal offense.

B. What if one act violates two different laws?

Now consider a different situation.

Pedro issues a worthless check, knowing that his account lacks sufficient funds, to deceive Juan into delivering merchandise.

The act may potentially implicate:

  • Estafa under Article 315 of the RPC, if its statutory elements are present.
  • Batas Pambansa Blg. 22, if its separate statutory elements are established.

Can Pedro be prosecuted under both laws?

Generally, yes.

Although the charges may arise from the same transaction, the offenses have different legal elements.

Estafa involving deceit requires matters not necessarily required for B.P. 22. Conversely, B.P. 22 punishes the issuance of a check under the conditions specified by that law, without requiring the same deceit-based elements as Estafa.

A related distinction appears in prosecutions involving Illegal Recruitment and Estafa, when the facts satisfy the respective elements of both crimes.

Do not overgeneralize the doctrine. Article III, Section 21 also expressly addresses an act punished by both a law and an ordinance, and the constitutional rules on the same offense or necessarily included offenses must always be considered.

C. Exceptions under Rule 117, Section 7

We previously learned that a conviction generally bars another prosecution for the same offense or an offense necessarily including or included in it.

But Rule 117, Section 7 recognizes three situations in which a prior conviction does not bar prosecution for a graver offense necessarily including the original charge.

1. Supervening event

Pedro seriously injures Juan in an assault.

Pedro is convicted of Physical Injuries.

Two weeks later, Juan dies from the injuries Pedro inflicted.

Can the prosecution bring a subsequent charge for Homicide?

Yes, provided the statutory and factual requirements are established.

The graver offense developed because of a supervening event: the victim's subsequent death.

At the time of the earlier conviction, the homicide had not yet occurred.

The prosecution must establish that the death resulted from the accused's criminal act.

Distinction: Do not automatically apply the same reasoning to Ivler, where the various consequences of the single reckless act already formed the basis of the separate prosecutions.

2. Facts discovered after plea

Suppose Pedro is prosecuted for a lesser offense.

After he has entered a plea, facts constituting a graver offense are discovered.

Rule 117, Section 7(b) allows the appropriate subsequent prosecution in the circumstances specified by the Rule.

Notice its exact temporal requirement: the facts constituting the graver charge became known or were discovered only after a plea was entered in the earlier proceeding.

3. Plea of guilty to a lesser offense without required consent

Suppose Pedro is charged with a serious offense but wishes to plead guilty to a lesser included offense.

Under Rule 116, Section 1(f), the necessary consent ordinarily includes that of:

  • The public prosecutor.
  • The offended party.

The Rule also provides for the situation where the offended party fails to appear despite due notice.

A lesser plea taken without compliance with the applicable consent requirements may fall within the exception under Rule 117, Section 7(c).

Recitation lesson: An accused cannot necessarily manufacture a double-jeopardy bar through an improperly obtained plea to a lesser offense.

D. Why double jeopardy does not apply to preliminary investigation

Consider a complaint dismissed by the prosecutor during preliminary investigation.

The complainant subsequently discovers additional evidence and seeks reconsideration or otherwise pursues a legally available remedy.

Does the respondent automatically have a double-jeopardy defense?

No.

A preliminary investigation is not a trial before a court of competent jurisdiction in which the accused has been arraigned and pleaded.

Consequently, the necessary requirements for former jeopardy have not arisen.

Likewise, criminal and administrative proceedings are legally distinct. An acquittal in a criminal case does not automatically prevent administrative liability arising from the same events.

The respective proceedings apply different rules and standards of proof.

VI. PROVISIONAL DISMISSAL AND THE TIME-BAR RULE

Rule 117, Section 8

A. What is a provisional dismissal?

Imagine that Pedro is being prosecuted for a criminal offense.

A witness essential to the prosecution becomes temporarily unavailable. The parties discuss whether to terminate the case for the time being, with the possibility of reviving it later.

Instead of dismissing the prosecution permanently, the court provisionally dismisses it.

This is a provisional dismissal: a temporary dismissal which may permit revival, subject to the rules governing its validity and duration.

The crucial consideration is that provisional dismissal is not the same as acquittal.

B. Requisites for a valid provisional dismissal

The express requirements of Rule 117, Section 8 are:

  1. Express consent of the accused.
  2. Notice to the offended party.

The jurisprudence in People v. Lacson, G.R. No. 149453, further explains the requirements for invoking the time-bar rule, including the court's provisional-dismissal order and service of that order on the public prosecutor.

What qualifies as express consent?

The accused must positively and unequivocally agree to the provisional dismissal.

It may be manifested orally on the record or in an appropriate written manifestation.

Mere silence or failure to object does not automatically amount to the express consent required by the Rule.

Why must the offended party receive notice?

Because the private offended party has a legally recognized interest in the proceedings, particularly the civil aspect, and should have the opportunity to be heard concerning the contemplated provisional dismissal.

Practical application: When checking a provisional-dismissal order, defense counsel should inspect not only its wording but also the record showing consent, notice, and service.

C. The one-year and two-year time-bar rule

Once a qualifying provisional dismissal has occurred, Rule 117, Section 8 establishes periods within which the prosecution must be revived.

Classification Ordinary period
Offenses punishable by imprisonment not exceeding six years, or a fine of any amount, or both One year
Offenses punishable by imprisonment exceeding six years Two years

These periods are based on the penalty prescribed for the offense, not simply on whether the case happens to be filed before an MTC or RTC.

The ordinary statutory reckoning is from the issuance of the provisional-dismissal order. The jurisprudential requirements for the application of the time-bar must also be satisfied.

Example

An offense punishable by imprisonment not exceeding six years is provisionally dismissed on January 15, 2026, with the necessary legal requirements satisfied.

If the case is not validly revived within the applicable one-year period, the provisional dismissal becomes permanent under the Rule.

This is different from ordinary prescription of crimes.

Prescription generally concerns the State's time to institute the criminal action. The provisional-dismissal time-bar regulates revival after a criminal case has already been instituted and provisionally dismissed.

Is the expiration automatically a permanent bar?

The Rule provides that the provisional dismissal becomes permanent when the applicable period expires without revival.

People v. Lacson also discusses exceptional questions concerning justifiable necessity for delay. Accordingly, the practical application must account for the governing jurisprudence and the established procedural facts rather than assuming that every order labeled "provisional dismissal" triggers the time-bar.

D. Provisional dismissal, civil compromise, and bail

Suppose Pedro and Juan enter into a civil compromise agreement requiring Pedro to repay money in installments.

The criminal proceedings are provisionally dismissed while Pedro fulfills the settlement.

Three months later, Pedro defaults.

Can the criminal case be revived?

Potentially, yes, if a legally proper revival is sought while the applicable period and procedural requirements permit it.

But the compromise itself does not automatically control the State's criminal prosecution.

Who may seek revival?

The lecture states that only the public prosecutor may validly move for revival.

The governing general principle is that criminal prosecutions are conducted under the public prosecutor's direction and control.

However, there is an important qualification.

In a 2015 Supreme Court ruling involving G.R. Nos. 211933 and 211960, the Court recognized that although the prosecutor generally should have filed the motion to revive, the defect arising from a private person's filing was cured by the public prosecutor's subsequent conformity and participation.

Therefore, the lecture's absolute statement that a privately filed motion to revive is invariably an incurable nullity is too broad.

What happens to bail?

When a criminal case is provisionally dismissed, the court may address the posted bail bond and order its cancellation or the release of the deposit when legally appropriate.

The practical point is that dismissal of the active criminal proceeding has consequences for the bond securing the accused's appearance.

But cancellation and release of cash or other security must follow the appropriate court order and governing bail procedures.

Can the complainant enforce the civil compromise?

If the civil compromise has been validly incorporated into an enforceable judgment, the offended party may pursue execution under the applicable rules.

But distinguish an enforceable judgment from an ordinary private settlement contract.

The latter does not automatically become a judgment enforceable by writ of execution merely because it was mentioned in the criminal proceedings.

E. The professor's important defense scenario: Provisional dismissal versus speedy trial

Consider this situation:

A criminal case has repeatedly been set for trial.

The prosecutor fails to present a principal witness on five successive trial dates and repeatedly seeks postponement.

Eventually, the prosecutor requests provisional dismissal.

Should defense counsel immediately consent?

Not without evaluating the accused's right to a speedy trial.

Consenting may permit the prosecution to revive the case within the applicable time-bar, whereas a dismissal based on an established constitutional speedy-trial violation can carry different consequences.

The correct analytical approach

Counsel should consider the established speedy-trial factors:

Practical application

If the delays are unreasonable, attributable to the prosecution, repeatedly objected to, and prejudicial to the accused, counsel may seek dismissal based on the violation of the right to speedy trial.

If the court validly grants a dismissal protected by double jeopardy, the prosecution cannot simply start again.

The essential distinction: A provisional dismissal permits potential revival; a qualifying speedy-trial dismissal may permanently terminate the prosecution.

VII. WITHDRAWAL, DISMISSAL, AND CRIMINAL PRE-TRIAL

A. Motion to Withdraw Information versus Motion to Dismiss

The professor emphasizes that criminal litigators must be precise about the relief sought.

The title of the motion may matter, but the actual nature, basis, timing, and legal consequences of the court's action are more important.

Crespo v. Mogul remains central: Once the criminal Information is filed, the judge has an independent judicial role in determining whether the prosecution should continue.

In Torres v. Aguinaldo, G.R. No. 164268, the Supreme Court also distinguished withdrawal of an Information from provisional dismissal for purposes of Rule 117, Section 8.

Application

Suppose a prosecutor petitions the court to withdraw an Information because the DOJ has recommended a different disposition.

Can the judge simply sign the motion without examining the case?

The judge must exercise independent judgment.

The prosecutor's position is important, but it does not automatically dictate the court's action.

B. Final versus interlocutory orders

Another recurring source of confusion is the difference between an interlocutory order and a final order.

Interlocutory order

An interlocutory order resolves an incidental issue without completely terminating the action.

Examples include:

  • Denial of a Motion to Quash.
  • Denial of a request for postponement.
  • Certain evidentiary rulings.

Generally, an interlocutory order is not immediately appealable by ordinary appeal.

An extraordinary petition for certiorari may be available only when the governing requisites are established.

Final order

A final order disposes of the action or otherwise terminates the proceedings on the matter involved.

Appealability depends on the nature of the ruling and whether appellate review would violate double jeopardy.

The professor contrasts this with the immediately final character of an acquittal.

Remember also that a conviction and an acquittal do not have identical consequences for appellate review.

C. Criminal pre-trial under Rule 118

After arraignment, when the prosecution continues toward trial, the court conducts the appropriate pre-trial proceedings.

Pre-trial is mandatory in criminal cases covered by Rule 118.

The purpose is to identify what must actually be litigated and what can be resolved or agreed upon beforehand.

Rule 118, Section 1 identifies matters including:

  1. Plea bargaining.
  2. Stipulation of facts.
  3. Marking evidence for identification.
  4. Waiver of objections to admissibility.
  5. Modification of the order of trial where the accused admits the charge but interposes a lawful defense.
  6. Other matters promoting a fair and expeditious trial of the criminal and civil aspects.

How does stipulation of facts work?

Suppose Pedro is charged with Murder.

During pre-trial, the parties agree that Juan died on a particular date.

That admitted fact may no longer require the same degree of evidentiary contest.

However, Pedro does not necessarily admit that he unlawfully killed Juan simply by stipulating to the fact of death.

The existence of the death and the accused's criminal responsibility are distinct questions.

Under Rule 118, Section 2, agreements or admissions made during pre-trial must be reduced to writing and signed by the accused and counsel; otherwise, they cannot be used against the accused.

This is a protection against informal or unauthorized admissions being treated as binding concessions.

D. Corpus delicti in a Murder prosecution

The professor closes the lecture with an example involving preparation of evidence in a Murder case.

What is corpus delicti?

Literally, the expression refers to the body or substance of the crime.

But in criminal procedure, corpus delicti does not necessarily mean the physical corpse.

In a homicide or murder prosecution, the prosecution must establish the fact of death and that the death was caused by criminal agency.

The prosecution must also establish the accused's criminal responsibility to obtain a conviction.

Evidence discussed in the lecture

Evidence Purpose

The source describes a death certificate and autopsy report as highly important evidence.

That is a useful trial preparation point, but it should not be transformed into a rule that corpus delicti can be established only through those documents.

The Supreme Court recognizes that corpus delicti may also be established through circumstantial evidence.

Professor's final practice lesson: Good pre-trial preparation requires the prosecutor and defense counsel to distinguish uncontested facts from matters still requiring proof.
