PART I. CONSTITUTIONAL FOUNDATIONS

A. Territorial and Political Subdivisions (Art. X, Sec. 1)

  • The territorial and political subdivisions of the Republic are:
    • Provinces;
    • Cities;
    • Municipalities;
    • Barangays; and
    • Autonomous regions in Muslim Mindanao and the Cordilleras.
  • An LGU is a political subdivision constituted by law, possessing substantial control over local affairs and endowed with corporate powers to promote the general welfare.
  • Unitary State Doctrine: The Philippines remains a unitary republic. Local autonomy does not diminish the unitary character of the State. LGUs possess only such powers as are expressly granted by the Constitution or statute, or necessarily implied therefrom. LGUs are intra-sovereign units, not sovereign entities.
  • Constitutional anchors (Art. X):
    • Sec. 2 — all territorial and political subdivisions shall enjoy local autonomy.
    • Sec. 3 — Congress shall enact a local government code providing for a more responsive and accountable local government structure through a system of decentralization.
    • Sec. 4 — the President exercises general supervision over local governments.
    • Sec. 5 — each LGU has the power to create its own sources of revenue and to levy taxes, fees, and charges (direct constitutional grant).
    • Sec. 6 — just share in the national taxes, automatically released.
    • Sec. 7 — equitable share in the proceeds of the utilization and development of the national wealth within their areas.
    • Sec. 10 — plebiscite requirement for creation, division, merger, abolition, or substantial alteration of boundaries.
    • Sec. 11 — metropolitan political subdivisions.
    • Secs. 15–21 — autonomous regions.

C. Supervision vs. Control (Art. X, Sec. 4; Sec. 25, LGC)

Point Control General Supervision
Source Art. VII, Sec. 17 Art. X, Sec. 4; Sec. 25, LGC
Over whom Executive departments, bureaus, offices LGUs
Scope Power to alter, modify, nullify, or set aside what a subordinate has done and to substitute one's judgment Power to see to it that subordinates perform their duties according to law; if they fail, to take proper administrative or judicial steps (Mondano v. Silvosa)
Discretionary acts Superior may substitute judgment Cannot substitute judgment or disturb discretionary acts within lawful bounds (Drilon v. Lim)
Relationship Qualified political agency (alter ego) No alter ego relationship — local elective officials derive authority from the electorate and statute
  • Rule against substitution: The supervising authority may only check whether the LGU acted ultra vires or contrary to law — not the wisdom of the act.
  • Alter ego doctrine (Villena v. Secretary of the Interior) operates only within the executive department; it cannot be used to control LGUs.
  • Pimentel v. Lina: A DILG directive commanding local executives to enforce national anti-gambling laws under pain of administrative sanction is valid supervision, not control — it compels performance of a mandatory legal duty, not substitution of discretion.
  • Ganzon v. CA: Directives to enforce anti-gambling, anti-drug, or environmental laws under threat of administrative sanctions are a valid exercise of supervisory authority.
  • Pimentel v. Aguirre: Local autonomy does not place LGUs beyond national supervision.
Illustration: The DILG cannot nullify a mayor's traffic-routing executive order merely because it disagrees with the scheme; nullification requires showing a violation of law, through proper (judicial) action.

D. Autonomous Regions (Art. X, Secs. 15–21)

  • Only two autonomous regions may be created: Muslim Mindanao and the Cordilleras — consisting of provinces, cities, municipalities, and geographical areas sharing common and distinctive historical and cultural heritage, economic and social structures, and other relevant characteristics (Sec. 15).
  • Creation requisite (Sec. 18): Organic act approved by a majority of the votes cast by the constituent units in a plebiscite; only provinces, cities, and geographic areas voting favorably shall be included.
  • Muslim Mindanao evolution: ARMM (R.A. 6734, as amended by R.A. 9054) → BARMM (R.A. 11054, Bangsamoro Organic Law), a parliamentary form led by a Chief Minister; BARMM is now governed by its own Bangsamoro Local Governance Code (BAA No. 13).
  • Province of Sulu v. Medialdea (2024): Sulu voted "No" in the 2019 BOL plebiscite but was dragged in because the ARMM was tallied as a single unit. Held: "Constituent units" refers to each province/city individually; Sulu cannot be compelled to join. Sulu is not part of BARMM.
  • Ordillo v. COMELEC (1990): Only Ifugao approved R.A. 6766 (Cordillera Autonomous Region). Held: A single province cannot constitute an autonomous region — Sec. 15 uses the plural "provinces, cities, municipalities, and geographical areas."
  • Sema v. COMELEC (2008): The ARMM Regional Assembly cannot create a province (Shariff Kabunsuan). Creating a province necessarily creates a legislative district, which only Congress may do (Art. VI, Sec. 5; Art. X, Sec. 19).
  • Badua v. Cordillera Bodong Administration: Tribal courts are not part of the Judiciary (R.A. 6766 was rejected, so the proposed regional courts never came into existence). However, a voluntary customary settlement may be enforced as a compromise agreement.

E. Metropolitan Political Subdivisions (Art. X, Sec. 11)

  • Congress may create special metropolitan political subdivisions:
    • By law;
    • Subject to a plebiscite in the units directly affected;
    • Component LGUs retain their basic autonomy and their own executive and legislative bodies;
    • Jurisdiction limited to basic metropolitan services requiring coordination (traffic, solid waste, flood control).
  • MMDA v. Bel-Air Village Association (2000): The MMDA is an administrative coordinating agency, not an LGU. It has no police power and no ordinance-making power. It could not open a private subdivision road. Its head is a presidential appointee, not an elected official.
  • MMDA has no review power over ordinances of Metro Manila LGUs.
Historical illustration: The Metropolitan Manila Commission under Marcos was headed by an appointed Governor (Imelda Marcos) — an executive creation, not an LGU.

PART II. THE LOCAL GOVERNMENT CODE: ENACTMENT, POLICY, PRINCIPLES, SCOPE, AND CONSTRUCTION

A. Enactment and Effectivity

  • Principal author: Senate President Aquilino "Nene" Pimentel, Jr.
  • Title: "Local Government Code of 1991" (Sec. 1). (Do not confuse with R.A. 7653, the New Central Bank Act.)
  • Approved: October 10, 1991 (Pres. Corazon C. Aquino); published in Malaya.
  • Effectivity (Sec. 536): January 1, 1992.
  • Interim law: Between Oct. 10, 1991 and Jan. 1, 1992, B.P. Blg. 337 (LGC of 1983) governed. R.A. 7160 is not retroactive.

B. Declaration of Policy (Sec. 2)

  • LGUs shall enjoy genuine and meaningful local autonomy to enable them to attain their fullest development as self-reliant communities and make them more effective partners in the attainment of national goals.
  • National agencies must coordinate with, not control, LGUs.
  • Sec. 2(c): national agencies must conduct periodic consultations with LGUs, NGOs, POs, and other concerned sectors before any project is implemented (see Secs. 26–27).

C. The Thirteen Operative Principles of Decentralization (Sec. 3[a]–[m])

  1. (a) Allocation of powers, responsibilities, and resources among the tiers according to capacity (operationalized through Secs. 16, 17, 18, 19).
  2. (b) Accountable, efficient, and dynamic organizational structure responsive to community needs.
  3. (c) Merit and fitness in the appointment and discipline of local personnel (civil service laws; ban on nepotism; due process; security of tenure).
  4. (d) Fiscal autonomy — right to create own revenues, just share in national taxes (Mandanas), equitable share in national wealth (Tiwi).
  5. (e) Supervisory hierarchy — provinces over component cities/municipalities; cities/municipalities over barangays.
  6. (f) Inter-local cooperation — LGUs may group, consolidate, and coordinate efforts and resources (justifies extra-territorial expenditures; see Sec. 33).
  7. (g) Participation of municipalities and barangays in planning and implementing national programs (e.g., lack of local coordination in DPWH flood control projects).
  8. (h) Continuing mechanism to enhance local autonomy.
  9. (i) Shared responsibility for ecological balance — LGUs cannot treat environmental enforcement as solely DENR's duty.
  10. (j) Accountability of local officials — public office is a public trust (Sec. 60 discipline, Ombudsman, COA, recall).
  11. (k) National technical, financial, and material assistance (e.g., national intervention during COVID-19 despite devolved health services).
  12. (l) Participation of the private sector, NGOs, and POs — representation in local special bodies:
    • Local Health Board;
    • Local School Board;
    • Local Peace and Order Council;
    • Local Development Council.
  13. (m) Sustained performance improvement — devolution cannot be an excuse for local failure.

D. Scope of Application (Sec. 4)

  • Applies to:
    • All provinces, cities, municipalities, and barangays;
    • Other political subdivisions created by law; and
    • National government officials, offices, and agencies, to the extent provided in the Code.
  • Does not supersede organic acts of autonomous regions (e.g., BARMM), except where the organic act adopts its provisions.
  • National agencies (DPWH, DENR, DOH) are bound to coordinate and consult (Secs. 2[c], 26, 27).

E. Rules of Interpretation (Sec. 5)

Rule Content
5(a) Powers of LGUs Liberally interpreted in favor of the LGU; doubts resolved in favor of devolution and of the existence of the power.
5(b) Tax ordinances/revenue measures Construed strictly against the LGU and liberally in favor of the taxpayer. Taxing powers are never presumed.
5(c) General Welfare Clause Liberally interpreted to give more powers to LGUs to accelerate economic development and upgrade the quality of life.
5(d) Customs and traditions In the absence of law or jurisprudence, the customs and traditions of the place where the controversy arose may be applied.
  • Limit on 5(a) — Ultra vires/Bill of Rights: Liberal construction does not save an ordinance violating the Constitution, national policy, or constituting unreasonable restraint of trade.
    Illustration: San Juan, La Union ordinance banning sale of pottery made outside San Juan → unconstitutional (protectionist monopoly; restraint of trade).
  • Regulatory fee vs. revenue measure: A regulatory fee is valid only to the extent of the reasonable cost of regulation, inspection, and licensing. A "fee" without regulatory standards, inspection, or enforcement mechanisms is a disguised revenue measure.
    Illustration: ₱1.00/head "regulatory fee" on pigs and poultry with no animal health or inspection provisions → disapproved by the provincial board.
  • General welfare (liberal): Zoning ordinance ordering relocation of abaca warehouses/junk yards (fire hazards) out of residential areas → valid.
  • Custom (5[d]):
    • Definition: A rule of conduct formed by repetition of acts, uniformly observed as a social rule, legally binding and obligatory.
    • Custom must be proved as a fact (Art. 12, Civil Code; Yao Kee v. Sy-Gonzales) — it cannot be assumed.
    • Illustrations: the kutsero custom of securing reins to the carriage while loading (negated negligence); the Bangar livestock custom (firstborn offspring belongs to the caretaker).

PART III. CREATION, CONVERSION, DIVISION, MERGER, AND ABOLITION OF LGUs (Secs. 6–14)

A. Authority to Create (Sec. 6)

  • An LGU may be created, divided, merged, abolished, or its boundaries substantially altered:
    • By law enacted by Congress — for provinces, cities, municipalities, and other political subdivisions; or
    • By ordinance of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan or Sangguniang Panlungsod — for barangays only.
    • The Sangguniang Bayan has no power to create barangays (it may only recommend to the SP).
  • Always subject to the plebiscite requirement (Sec. 10) and the criteria of Sec. 7.
  • Pelaez v. Auditor General (1965) & Municipality of Kapalong v. Moya (1988): The power to create municipal corporations is purely legislative. Sec. 68 of the Revised Administrative Code (allowing the President to create municipalities by E.O.) was an undue delegation of legislative power.
  • Financial caveat on Sanggunian-created barangays: Funded from the creating LGU's own funds; not entitled to a direct NTA share unless recognized by Congress.
    Illustration: La Union SP created Barangay Antonino (Balaoan) out of Cabuaan — the province had to fund it; hence only one barangay has been so created.
  • Curative/Grandfather provisions:
    • LGUs created before the Code through defective means (e.g., executive orders) and actually functioning are deemed regular/de jure (curative effect, Sec. 442[d]).
    • The Sec. 442 thresholds apply prospectively; pre-1992 municipalities that fall short remain valid.

B. Procedure for Creation

  1. Petition — resolution of the Sanggunian concerned (IRR). → Procedural, NOT jurisdictional. Congress may create an LGU motu proprio without a petition.
  2. Mandatory certifications (cannot be dispensed with):
    • Income — Department of Finance (DOF);
    • Population — Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA, formerly NSO);
    • Land area — Land Management Bureau (LMB-DENR);
    • Boundary maps/metes and bounds — LMB with DPWH.
  3. Enactment of the law (or ordinance, for barangays).
  4. Plebiscite conducted by COMELEC — approval by majority of votes cast in the units directly affected.
  5. Corporate existence begins (Sec. 14) — upon election and qualification of the chief executive and a majority of the sanggunian members.

C. Criteria for Creation (Sec. 7; Secs. 386, 442, 450, 461)

  • The three indicators:
    • Income — sufficient to provide for essential government facilities and services (certified by DOF);
    • Population — total number of inhabitants (not registered voters; includes minors), certified by PSA;
    • Land area — contiguous, unless comprising two or more islands or separated by an LGU independent of the others; sufficient for development; certified by LMB.

  • Province: Income threshold is non-waivable; land area may be relaxed for island provinces.

Land Area Doctrine

  • Tan v. COMELEC (1986) [Negros del Norte]: Proponents counted marine waters to meet the 3,500 sq. km. requirement (B.P. 337) when actual land mass was ~2,856 sq. km. Held: "Territory" means land mass, not waters. B.P. 885 void ab initio.
  • "Territory" vs. "Land area":
    • Territory — broader area of jurisdiction, including inland and municipal waters (up to 15 km, Fisheries Code).
    • Contiguous land area — certified, dry terrestrial mass; this is the creation criterion. Waters are excluded.
  • Navarro v. Ermita (2011) [Dinagat Islands]: Land area requirement is waived for provinces composed of islands (Art. 9, IRR).
  • Enclave exception: Benguet remains a valid province though Baguio City sits within it.
Illustration — Agoo: Met income but failed both the 100 sq. km. land area and 150,000 population; coastal waters cannot be counted → cityhood stalled. A merger with Aringay ("Agoray") failed because Aringay insisted on its civic identity.

E. Division and Merger (Sec. 8); Abolition (Sec. 9)

  • Division/Merger: Shall not reduce the income, population, or land area of the LGUs concerned below the Sec. 7 minimums — both the new unit and the remaining parent unit must each comply.
  • Abolition (Sec. 9): When income, population, or land area has been irreversibly reduced to less than the minimum, as certified by the national agencies.
    • Requires an Act of Congress (or ordinance, for barangays) and a plebiscite.
    • A Governor cannot declare a municipality abolished.
    • The law must specify the LGU that will absorb the territory, assets, records, and liabilities.
  • Boundary disputes (Sec. 118): Settled amicably by the sanggunians concerned; municipalities of different provinces → jointly by the respective Sangguniang Panlalawigans.
    Illustration (Pugo v. Tuba): The professor, elected to the SP, refused to inhibit from the joint panel since his seat derived from his election.

F. Plebiscite Requirement (Art. X, Sec. 10; Sec. 10, LGC)

  • No creation, division, merger, abolition, or substantial alteration of boundaries without approval by a majority of the votes cast in a plebiscite in the political unit or units directly affected.
  • Conducted by COMELEC; a condition precedent — without it, the law is inchoate.
  • "Units affected" doctrine: Includes the entire parent LGU, not just the area carved out (Tan v. COMELEC; Padilla v. COMELEC).
    Novaliches Plebiscite (R.A. 8535): Plebiscite for creating Novaliches City out of 15 barangays had to include all of Quezon City; voters rejected it.
  • Miranda v. Aguirre (1999) [Santiago City]: Downgrading an ICC to a component city (R.A. 8528) requires a plebiscite — it substantially alters the political and economic rights of residents (provincial taxes, voting for provincial officials, provincial supervision). R.A. 8528 unconstitutional.

G. Seat of Government, Government Center, Naming (Secs. 11–13)

  • Sec. 11 — Site: Chosen for accessibility to the public, transportation, and room for expansion. No requirement that it be in the poblacion (e.g., Urdaneta City Hall relocated to the highway; the La Union capitol relocation proposal).
  • Sec. 12 — Government center: Per the notes, site of at least 10,000 sq. m. (1 hectare) under the IRR.
  • Sec. 13 — Naming:
Authority Coverage
Congress National roads, highways, national structures
Sangguniang Panlalawigan Provincial roads, bridges, facilities
Sangguniang Panlungsod / Bayan City/municipal roads, plazas, markets, structures
Sangguniang Barangay Barangay roads and facilities
  • Restrictions:
    • Public places may be named only after deceased persons; naming after a living person is prohibited.
    • May be named after a family that contributed substantially to the community.
    • Exercise decency (do not name comfort rooms after historical leaders).
Illustrations: "Amparo Park" (San Juan, LU) — named after a living Vice Governor → ordinance repealed. Pennsylvania Ave. → "Joaquin 'Titing' Ortega Avenue" — a national road, so it required an Act of Congress; the sanggunian could only pass an endorsing resolution.

H. Beginning of Corporate Existence (Sec. 14)

  • Corporate existence commences upon the election and qualification of its chief executive and a majority of the members of its sanggunian, unless some other time is fixed by the law or ordinance creating it.
  • Rationale: A legislative body cannot transact business without a quorum.
  • If the Vice Mayor is not yet qualified but a majority of the council has qualified, corporate existence begins; the members present elect a temporary presiding officer (no automatic chair by the top vote-getter), unless a member has been formally designated acting vice mayor.

PART IV. POWERS OF LGUs (Secs. 15–24)

A. Dual Nature of LGUs (Sec. 15)

B. Delegated Inherent Powers

  • LGUs have no inherent sovereign powers. Police power, taxation, and eminent domain are exercised only by delegation.
Power Provision Limit
Police power Sec. 16 (General Welfare Clause) Due process, equal protection, consistency with national law
Taxation Sec. 18; Secs. 128–196; Art. X, Sec. 5 (direct constitutional grant) Guidelines and limitations set by Congress

C. General Welfare Clause (Sec. 16)

  • Two branches:
    1. General legislative power — to enact ordinances and regulations not repugnant to law necessary to carry into effect the powers and duties conferred by law.
    2. Police power proper — to enact ordinances necessary to promote health, safety, morals, peace, good order, comfort, convenience, and general welfare of the community and inhabitants.
  • Objectives the LGU must promote within its territory:
    • Preservation and enrichment of culture;
    • Promotion of health and safety;
    • Enhancement of the right of the people to a balanced ecology;
    • Development of self-reliant scientific and technological capabilities;
    • Improvement of public morals;
    • Enhancement of economic prosperity and social justice;
    • Promotion of full employment;
    • Maintenance of peace and order; and
    • Preservation of the comfort and convenience of inhabitants.
  • Tests of valid exercise (limits):
    • Lawful subject — the interests of the public generally, as distinguished from those of a particular class, require interference;
    • Lawful means — the means are reasonably necessary for the purpose and not unduly oppressive upon individuals;
    • Consistency with national law — an ordinance cannot prohibit what a statute permits, nor permit what a statute prohibits;
    • Procedural due process — notice and hearing, as a rule.
  • Summary abatement exception: A nuisance per se (e.g., a rabid dog loose on the street) may be summarily abated without notice and hearing.
  • Batangas CATV v. CA (2004): A city cannot fix cable TV rates under the General Welfare Clause — that power is vested by national law in the NTC. Delegated police power is subordinate to national law.
  • MMDA v. Bel-Air: Police power requires an express legislative grant; MMDA has none.
Illustration: The professor's mandatory 14-day COVID-19 quarantine (despite a negative test on day 10) — valid police power; liberty yields to reasonable, non-discriminatory health measures.

D. Basic Services and Facilities (Sec. 17)

LGU Devolved Services (core examples)
Barangay Agricultural support (seedling distribution); health and day-care centers; general sanitation and garbage collection; maintenance of barangay roads and bridges
Municipality Agricultural extension and on-site research; primary health care, maternal and child care; solid waste disposal; communal irrigation; municipal buildings, public markets, slaughterhouses, cemeteries

  • Coverage expands at each level; overlapping responsibilities require cooperation (province provides tertiary/technical support; municipalities and barangays deliver primary services).

E. Power to Generate and Apply Resources (Sec. 18)

  • Five facets:
    1. Establish an organization to be responsible for the efficient and effective implementation of development plans and programs;
    2. Create own sources of revenue and levy taxes, fees, and charges, which shall accrue exclusively to the LGU;
    3. Just share in national taxes, automatically and directly released;
    4. Equitable share in proceeds of national wealth within its territory; and
    5. Acquire, develop, lease, encumber, alienate, or dispose of real or personal property.
  • Ordinance requirement (Secs. 18, 132): The power to impose a tax, fee, or charge is exercised only by the sanggunian through an ordinance.
    • An executive (Punong Barangay, Mayor, Governor) has no authority to collect fees without an ordinance.
    • An official receipt does not cure the lack of an ordinance. Unauthorized collection = illegal exaction; possible liability under Sec. 3(e), R.A. 3019.
    Illustration: ₱200 barangay residency certificate fee, with O.R., but no barangay ordinance → unlawful.
  • Mandanas v. Ochoa (2018; Res. 2019): Sec. 284 limiting the LGU share to "national internal revenue taxes" (BIR collections only) is unconstitutional. Art. X, Sec. 6 says "national taxes" — which includes customs duties and other national taxes collected by the BOC and other agencies. The IRA became the National Tax Allotment (NTA), to be automatically and directly released.
  • Share in national wealth (Art. X, Sec. 7; Secs. 289–294):
    • Covers mineral, hydroelectric, timber, geothermal resources.
    • Distribution among host units: Province 20% — Municipality 45% — Barangay 35%.
    • 80% of proceeds from energy resources is applied to lower the cost of electricity in the host LGU; the remainder goes to development and livelihood projects.
    • Tiwi, Albay: The host LGU has an enforceable right to its share. The Albay provincial officials (Governor, Vice Governor, SP members) who diverted Tiwi's share were dismissed. Tiwi's private counsel who recovered the share could compel, through mandamus, the Sanggunian to appropriate his fees — an LGU contracting in its corporate capacity cannot evade valid obligations.
  • Tax ordinance appeal (Sec. 187): Questions on the constitutionality or legality of tax ordinances go on appeal to the Secretary of Justice, at all levels including HUCs and provinces. (See Part XI on the period.)
  • Barangay compensation (Sec. 393): Honoraria minimum — Punong Barangay ₱1,000/month; Kagawads, Secretary, Treasurer ₱600/month. With greater fiscal capacity (post-Mandanas), the Sangguniang Barangay may convert honoraria into salaries by appropriation ordinance, within the personal services ceilings (Sec. 325). Public office remains a public trust.

F. Eminent Domain (Sec. 19)

  • Requisites:
    1. An ordinance enacted by the sanggunian authorizing the local chief executive to exercise eminent domain — a resolution is insufficient (Municipality of Parañaque v. V.M. Realty Corp.);
    2. For public use, purpose, or welfare, or for the benefit of the poor and the landless;
    3. Payment of just compensation; and
    4. A valid and definite offer previously made to the owner, and not accepted.
  • Noble v. City of Manila (1938): Once the owner accepts the LGU's definite offer, a contract of sale is perfected; the LGU cannot abandon it and expropriate at a lower price.
  • Immediate possession:
    • LGU: deposit of 15% of the fair market value based on the current tax declaration.
    • National government (R.A. 8974 / R.A. 10752): 100% of BIR zonal value plus replacement cost of improvements.
  • Just compensation is a judicial determination; computed as of the time of taking; requires prompt payment (City of Makati v. CA).
    Illustration (Tubao–Baguio road): Land taken in the 1960s, paid only in 1994 at 1960s values (time of taking).
  • Barangays may expropriate (Sec. 19 covers "LGUs," which include barangays) — via a barangay ordinance, not a resolution. The Punong Barangay has no veto, since he presides over and votes in the Sangguniang Barangay.

H. Closure and Opening of Roads (Sec. 21)

  • An LGU may close or open only local roads, alleys, parks, or squares within its jurisdiction — not national highways (DPWH).
  • Permanent closure:
    • By ordinance approved by an extraordinary majority of the sanggunian;
    • Must provide an adequate substitute route;
    • The property, if no longer needed for public use, may be used or conveyed for any purpose for which other real property of the LGU may be lawfully used (reverts to the patrimonial property).
  • Temporary closure: for athletic, cultural, civic events, fiestas, public emergencies, or public works — must be temporary.

I. Corporate Powers (Sec. 22)

  • Every LGU, as a corporation, has the following powers:
    1. Continuous succession in its corporate name;
    2. To sue and be sued;
    3. To have and use a corporate seal (registered with the DILG);
    4. To acquire and convey real or personal property;
    5. To enter into contracts — through the local chief executive, with prior sanggunian authorization; and
    6. To exercise such other powers as are granted to corporations, subject to the Code.
  • Property practices: Inter-LGU property exchanges (San Fernando–La Union); Sagayad resettlement (lots sold at historical cost); reversionary clauses in transfers to national agencies (NBI, Hall of Justice, Camp Florendo) — title reverts when the public purpose ceases.

B. Ecological Balance & Prior Consultation (Secs. 26–27)

  • Sec. 26 — Duty of national agencies: Every national agency or GOCC involved in planning and implementing any project or program that may cause pollution, climate change, depletion of non-renewable resources, loss of crop land, rangeland, or forest cover, extinction of animal or plant species, or displacement of communities must consult with the LGUs, NGOs, and other sectors concerned and explain:
    • The goals and objectives of the project;
    • Its impact upon the people and the community in terms of environmental or ecological balance; and
    • The measures to be undertaken to prevent or minimize adverse effects.
  • Sec. 27 — Prior consultations required: No such project shall be implemented unless:
    1. The consultations under Secs. 2(c) and 26 are complied with; and
    2. Prior approval of the sanggunian concerned is obtained.
  • Timing: Consultation must be prior — during the planning phase. Post-construction or mid-construction consultation does not cure the defect.
  • PICOP Resources v. Base Metals (2011): If a project covers several LGUs, consultation and sanggunian approval are required from all affected LGUs; one endorsement is not enough.
  • Province of Rizal v. Executive Secretary (2005): The San Mateo Landfill, established without prior consultation and SP approval → ordered closed.
Illustration: DPWH flood-control dike built across three municipalities without prior sanggunian approval → vulnerable to injunction/mandamus; a belated meeting does not cure it.

C. Local Chief Executives and the Police (Sec. 28; R.A. 6975; R.A. 8551)

  • Evolution:
    1. Pre-martial law — local police under the mayor (abused as private armies);
    2. INP under the PC (P.D. 765) — militarized;
    3. 1987 Constitution (Art. XVI, Sec. 6) — one police force, national in scope and civilian in character, administered by NAPOLCOM (R.A. 6975; R.A. 8551).
  • Civilian character: The PNP is under the DILG, not the AFP. The PCG is a civilian maritime service under the DOTr (R.A. 9993).
    • Manalo v. Sistoza: PNP senior officers do not require Commission on Appointments confirmation.
    • Soriano v. Lista: PCG flag officers do not require CA confirmation.
  • LCEs as NAPOLCOM deputies:
    • Exercise operational supervision and control over local PNP units (deploy, inspect, recommend);
    • Mayors choose the Chief of Police from a list of five eligibles recommended by the PNP Provincial Director.
  • Grounds for suspension/withdrawal of deputation by NAPOLCOM:
    1. Frequent unauthorized absences;
    2. Abuse of authority;
    3. Providing material support to criminal elements; or
    4. Engaging in acts inimical to national security or that negate the effectiveness of the peace and order campaign (e.g., illegal drugs, corruption).
    Example: Mayor Rolando Espinosa — upon withdrawal, the LCE loses all operational authority.

D. Provincial Supervision; Review of Executive Orders (Secs. 29–30)

  • Sec. 29: The province, through the governor, exercises general supervision over component cities and municipalities to ensure they act within their prescribed powers. The governor cannot revoke a mayor's valid executive order or substitute judgment.
  • Sec. 30 — Review of executive orders:
    • Punong Barangay → copies of EOs to the city/municipal mayor within 3 days;
    • Component city/municipal mayor → copies of EOs to the provincial governor within 3 days;
    • Standard: whether the EO is within the powers conferred by law. If no action is taken within 30 days, the EO is deemed consistent with law and valid.
Illustration: Mayor bans trucks on a municipal road 6–9 AM; the governor cannot strike it down merely because he prefers another schedule.

E. Legal Questions (Sec. 31); Barangay Supervision (Sec. 32)

  • Sec. 31 — Routing of legal questions:
    • Component municipality (no municipal legal officer) → Provincial Legal Officer → (if absent/disqualified) Provincial Prosecutor.
    • Component city → City Legal Officer → (if vacant) City Prosecutor.
    • Bypassing the Provincial Legal Officer and going directly to the DILG Central Office is improper; the DILG routinely declines (Bacnotan experience).
  • Sec. 32: Cities and municipalities exercise general supervision over component barangays. A mayor cannot summarily remove a Punong Barangay — discipline follows Secs. 60–68.

F. Cooperative Undertakings (Sec. 33)

  • LGUs may, through appropriate ordinances, group themselves, consolidate, or coordinate efforts, services, and resources for purposes commonly beneficial to them.
  • General territorial rule: Public funds may be spent only within the LGU's territory.
  • Sec. 33 exception: Under a joint cooperative agreement, an LGU may appropriate funds for facilities outside its territory (e.g., a shared sanitary landfill in San Fernando used by San Juan, Bacnotan, Bauang, Naguilian).
  • Process: Each LGU enacts an ordinance → joint MOA (cost-sharing, management) → common project.

G. NGOs, POs, Local Special Bodies, BAC (Secs. 34–38)

  • Secs. 34–35: LGUs shall promote the establishment and operation of POs and NGOs and may enter into joint ventures with them.
  • Accreditation by the sanggunian is a prerequisite to representation in local special bodies:
    • Local Development Council;
    • Local Health Board;
    • Local School Board;
    • Local Peace and Order Council;
    • Bids and Awards Committee.
  • Sec. 36 — Assistance: An LGU may provide financial or material assistance for economic, socially-oriented, environmental, or cultural projects for public purpose; loans must have clear repayment terms (COA rules on waste of public funds).
  • Secs. 37–38 (BAC; now R.A. 9184): Composition is fixed by statute. The LCE cannot add or remove members by executive order; procurement by an improperly constituted BAC is defective.

PART VI. ELECTIVE OFFICIALS (Secs. 39–47)

A. Qualifications (Sec. 39)

  • Positive qualifications (Sec. 39) work with negative qualifications (Sec. 40). Qualifications are continuing requirements throughout incumbency.
  • General qualifications:
    • Citizen of the Philippines — naturalized citizens qualify (unlike members of Congress);
    • Registered voter in the barangay, municipality, city, or province — or, for sanggunian members, in the district where he intends to be elected;
    • Resident therein for at least one (1) year immediately preceding the day of the election; and
    • Able to read and write Filipino or any other local language or dialect.
  • No property qualification — residing in a rented house or with relatives suffices (Co v. HRET; Maquera v. Borra).
  • Age — reckoned ON ELECTION DAY (not on filing of the COC, not on assumption). Purpose: avoid the ambiguity of the Ninoy Aquino precedent (34 on election day, 35 on oath-taking).

  • District rule: An SP candidate must be a voter of the specific district; a Governor may be a voter anywhere in the province.

Residence = Domicile

  • "Residence" in election law is synonymous with domicile — a fixed permanent residence to which, whenever absent, one has the intention of returning (animus revertendi) (Co v. HRET; Ong Huan Tin; Ujano).
  • Absence for study, business, or profession does not cause loss of domicile (Faypon v. Quirino; Abella v. COMELEC). Registering as a voter elsewhere for convenience does not by itself abandon domicile.
  • But establishing a permanent family home elsewhere without intent to return = abandonment (Abella).
  • Requisites for change of domicile (Romualdez-Marcos; Aquino; Domino; Dumpit-Michelena v. Boado):
    1. Actual removal or change of domicile;
    2. Bona fide intention of abandoning the former residence and establishing a new one (animus non revertendi + animus manendi); and
    3. Definite acts corresponding with that purpose.

B. Disqualifications (Sec. 40)

  • The following are disqualified from running for any elective local position:
    1. (a) Those sentenced by final judgment for an offense involving moral turpitude or for an offense punishable by one (1) year or more of imprisonment, within two (2) years after serving sentence;
      • Moral turpitude: an act of baseness, vileness, or depravity contrary to justice, honesty, modesty, or good morals (In re Gutierrez; Teves v. COMELEC).
      • Moreno v. COMELEC: A probationer is not disqualified — he does not "serve sentence"; the 2-year period does not run; probation restores civil rights.
    2. (b) Those removed from office as a result of an administrative case — removal on or after January 1, 1992 (Grego v. COMELEC); decision must be final.
      • Carpio-Morales v. CA & Binay: the condonation doctrine is abandoned (re-election does not condone prior misconduct).
    3. (c) Those convicted by final judgment for violating the oath of allegiance to the Republic;
    4. (d) Those with dual citizenship — construed as dual allegiance (Mercado v. Manzano; Valles v. COMELEC):
      • Dual citizenship arises involuntarily (concurrent laws, e.g., jus soli + jus sanguinis) → not disqualified; filing a COC with the oath suffices.
      • Dual allegiance arises from a voluntary act → disqualified unless compliant with R.A. 9225.
      • R.A. 9225 twin requirements: (1) Oath of allegiance; and (2) personal and sworn renunciation of any and all foreign citizenship before an authorized officer, at or before filing the COC (Jacot v. Dal).
      • Sobejana-Condon v. COMELEC (2012): The R.A. 9225 oath is not a renunciation; failure to execute a separate sworn renunciation → disqualified (Vice Mayor of Caba, La Union).
      • Maquiling v. COMELEC: Use of a foreign passport after renunciation recants it.
    5. (e) Fugitives from justice in criminal or non-political cases here or abroad — includes not only those who flee after conviction, but also those who, after being charged, flee to avoid prosecution (Marquez v. COMELEC; Rodriguez v. COMELEC). Intent to evade must be shown (charge or conviction already existing at time of flight).
    6. (f) Permanent residents in a foreign country or those who acquired the right to reside abroad and continue to avail of it after Jan. 1, 1992 (green card holders) — Caasi v. CA: filing a COC is not a waiver; there must be an independent act of waiver prior to filing the COC.
    7. (g) The insane or feeble-minded (declared by competent authority).

C. Manner of Election (Sec. 41)

  • At large: Governor, Vice Governor, City/Municipal Mayor and Vice Mayor, Punong Barangay, Sangguniang Barangay members.
  • By the KK: SK Chairperson and Kagawads.
  • By district: Regular members of the SP, Sangguniang Panlungsod, and SB (R.A. 7166; De Guia v. COMELEC — to reduce the number of candidates per ballot).
    • SP seats: 1st & 2nd class provinces — 10; 3rd & 4th class — 8; 5th & 6th class — 6; provinces with more than 5 legislative districts — 2 per district (R.A. 8553).
  • Ex officio members — presidents of:
    • The League of Councilors (in the SP);
    • The Liga ng mga Barangay; and
    • The Pederasyon ng mga Sangguniang Kabataan.
  • Sectoral representatives (3): one from women; one from workers; one from urban poor, indigenous cultural communities, disabled persons, or other sector as determined by the sanggunian.

D. Date of Election and Term of Office (Secs. 42–43)

  • Sec. 42: Every three (3) years, on the second Monday of May, unless otherwise provided by law.
  • Sec. 43(a): Term of three (3) years, starting noon of June 30 following the election (except barangay officials).
  • Art. X, Sec. 8: The 3-year term of local officials (except barangay) is constitutional — Congress cannot extend it, even by hold-over (Osmeña v. COMELEC). Barangay terms are statutory.
  • Term vs. tenure (Dimaporo v. Mitra): Term is the period fixed by law during which an officer may claim to hold office as of right; tenure is the actual period the officer holds office. Shorter tenure does not alter the term.
  • De Leon v. Esguerra: Barangay officials enjoy security of tenure during their term.

Three-Term Limit (Sec. 43[b])

  • No local elective official shall serve for more than three consecutive terms in the same position.
  • Requisites (both must concur) (Borja v. COMELEC; Ong v. Alegre):
    1. The official has been elected for three consecutive terms in the same local government post; and
    2. He has fully served three consecutive terms.
  • Voluntary renunciation of office for any length of time is not an interruption.
  • Involuntary interruption (e.g., unseated by an election protest before the term expires) breaks continuity (Lonzanida; Dizon).

E. Permanent Vacancies in Executive Offices (Sec. 44)

  • Causes of permanent vacancy (Sec. 44[d]): When an elective local official:
    1. Fills a higher vacant office;
    2. Refuses to assume office;
    3. Fails to qualify;
    4. Dies;
    5. Is removed from office;
    6. Voluntarily resigns; or
    7. Is otherwise permanently incapacitated to discharge the functions of his office.
  • Rules of succession:
    • Governor/Mayor vacant → the Vice Governor/Vice Mayor becomes Governor/Mayor.
    • Both Governor and Vice Governor (or Mayor and Vice Mayor) vacant → the highest-ranking sanggunian member becomes Governor/Mayor; the second highest-ranking becomes Vice Governor/Vice Mayor; subsequent vacancies filled by the next in rank.
    • Punong Barangay vacant → the highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member.
    • Ties → resolved by drawing of lots.
    • Successors serve only the unexpired term.
  • Ranking: Based on the proportion of votes obtained by each winning candidate to the total number of registered voters in each district in the immediately preceding election (not the raw vote) (Victoria v. COMELEC). Ex officio members are not in the line of executive succession.
  • Menzon v. Petilla: A vacancy exists when there is no person lawfully authorized to exercise the office. Where the Code is silent (temporary vacancy in the Vice Governor's office), the President, through the DILG, may make a temporary designation to avoid an interregnum.
  • Unda v. COMELEC: The Vice Mayor is next in line upon the death of the Mayor.

F. Permanent Vacancies in the Sanggunian (Sec. 45)

  • Appointing authority (where automatic succession does not apply):
    • President (through the Executive Secretary) — SP and Sangguniang Panlungsod of HUCs and ICCs;
    • Governor — Sangguniang Panlungsod of component cities and SB;
    • City/Municipal Mayor — Sangguniang Barangay, upon recommendation of the Sangguniang Barangay concerned.
  • Party rule: Except for the Sangguniang Barangay, the appointee must come from the same political party as the member who caused the vacancy, and must have:
    • A nomination; and
    • A certificate of membership — both issued by the highest official of the political party.
    These are conditions sine qua non; an appointment without them is null and void ab initio and a ground for administrative action against the appointing official.
  • Member belonging to no political party → the LCE appoints upon the recommendation of the sanggunian concerned.
    • Fariñas v. Barba: "Local chief executive" in Sec. 45(c) refers to the appointing authorities in Sec. 45(a); the sanggunian's recommendation is a condition sine qua non.
  • Party certificates NOT required when:
    1. The vacating member was independent (no party);
    2. The vacancy is in the Sangguniang Barangay (non-partisan);
    3. The vacancy is in the youth (SK) representation; or
    4. The vacancy is in the barangay (Liga) representation.
  • Youth and barangay representatives: filled automatically by the official next in rank of the organization concerned.
  • Per 10/03 notes (R.A. 12326): vacancies in SK Kagawad seats are filled by appointment by the City/Municipal Mayor.

G. Temporary Vacancy in the Office of the LCE (Sec. 46)

  • Automatic assumption: When the Governor, Mayor, or Punong Barangay is temporarily incapacitated (physical or legal reasons — leave, travel abroad, suspension), the Vice Governor, Vice Mayor, or highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member automatically exercises the powers and duties of the office — no appointment needed.
  • Withheld powers: The acting official cannot appoint, suspend, or dismiss employees, unless the temporary incapacity exceeds thirty (30) working days.
  • Gamboa v. Aguirre: An acting LCE cannot simultaneously preside over the sanggunian; the members elect a temporary presiding officer.
  • Termination of temporary incapacity:
    • Physical causes — upon submission to the sanggunian of a written declaration that he has reported back to office;
    • Legal causes — upon submission of documents showing that the legal causes no longer exist.
  • Travel rules:
    • Within the country, ≤ 3 consecutive days: the LCE may designate an OIC in writing, specifying powers (but not appoint, suspend, dismiss).
    • If the LCE fails or refuses to designate → the Vice LCE/highest-ranking member assumes on the fourth (4th) day of absence.
    • Except for such travel, the LCE shall in no case authorize any local official other than the Vice LCE/highest-ranking member to assume — this bars designating secretaries/administrators/subordinates as OICs.

PART VII. LOCAL LEGISLATION (Secs. 48–59)

A. Local Legislative Power (Sec. 48)

  • Exercised by:
    • Sangguniang Panlalawigan — province (powers: Sec. 468);
    • Sangguniang Panlungsod — city (Sec. 458);
    • Sangguniang Bayan — municipality (Sec. 447);
    • Sangguniang Barangay — barangay (Sec. 391).
  • Delegated, subordinate legislation — ordinances must conform to national statutes (Solicitor General v. MMA). Exception: taxing power is granted directly by Art. X, Sec. 5.
  • No inherent contempt/subpoena power — sanggunians cannot punish non-members for contempt or issue compulsory subpoenas absent express grant (NORECO II v. Sangguniang Panlungsod of Dumaguete).
  • Presumption of constitutionality of ordinances (Tano v. Socrates).
  • Requisites of a valid ordinance (Lagcao v. Labra; Magtajas v. Pryce Properties) — it must:
    1. Not contravene the Constitution or any statute;
    2. Not be unfair or oppressive;
    3. Not be partial or discriminatory;
    4. Not prohibit but may regulate trade;
    5. Be general and consistent with public policy; and
    6. Not be unreasonable.
    Plus: it must be enacted within the corporate powers of the LGU and passed according to the procedure prescribed by law.

B. Presiding Officer (Sec. 49) and the Barangay's Parliamentary Setup

  • Presiding officers:
    • Vice Governor — SP;
    • City Vice Mayor — Sangguniang Panlungsod;
    • Municipal Vice Mayor — SB;
    • Punong Barangay — Sangguniang Barangay.
  • The presiding officer votes only to break a tie.
  • Inability of the presiding officer → members present, constituting a quorum, elect a temporary presiding officer from among themselves (no automatic succession). He certifies measures enacted in that session within ten (10) days.
  • Historical shift: Before R.A. 7160, Governors and Mayors presided over the local boards. The Code separated them, giving the LCE the veto as a check.
  • Barangay = parliamentary form. The Punong Barangay is simultaneously:
    • Chief executive of the barangay;
    • Presiding officer of the Sangguniang Barangay (Sec. 390);
    • Lupon chairman of the Katarungang Pambarangay (Sec. 399) — mediation and conciliation; and
    • Exercises direct operational supervision over barangay tanods (Sec. 389). (Mayors/Governors only have operational supervision over PNP units, which are national.)
    Illustration: The PB presides over the session enacting a curfew, signs it, enforces it that night through the tanods, and mediates the violation the next morning as Lupon chairman.

Composition of the Sangguniang Barangay (Sec. 390)

  • Punong Barangay (presiding officer);
  • Seven (7) regular members (elected at large); and
  • SK Chairperson (ex officio).
  • The Barangay Secretary and Treasurer (Secs. 394–395) are appointive (by the PB with concurrence of the majority of all SB members) and are not members — they cannot deliberate, move, or vote.
    Illustration (Cotabato): An ordinance passed with the votes of the Secretary and Treasurer to reach the majority → void.

C. Internal Rules of Procedure (Sec. 50)

  • Adopted or updated on the first regular session following the election, within ninety (90) days.
    • Malonzo v. Zamora: The sanggunian need not finish its rules before legislating; passing an ordinance on all three readings in one day is allowed unless the rules prohibit it.
  • Rules shall provide for:
    • Organization, election of officers, creation of standing committees, including committees on:
      1. Appropriations;
      2. Women and family;
      3. Human rights;
      4. Youth and sports development;
      5. Environmental protection; and
      6. Cooperatives;
    • Order and calendar of business;
    • Legislative process;
    • Parliamentary procedures and conduct of members;
    • Discipline of members; and
    • Other rules.
  • Calendar of business: Rules that delegate the preparation of the calendar/order of business exclusively to the Secretary to the Sanggunian are defective — setting legislative priorities belongs to the members (Committee on Rules/floor leadership). The Secretary's duties (Sec. 469) are ministerial.
  • Discipline:
    • Grounds: (1) Disorderly behavior; (2) absence without justifiable cause for four (4) consecutive sessions.
    • Penalties: censure, reprimand, exclusion from the session, suspension for not more than sixty (60) days, or expulsion.
    • Suspension or expulsion requires 2/3 vote of all members.
    • Automatic expulsion: final conviction for a crime involving moral turpitude with imprisonment of at least one (1) year.

D. Full Disclosure of Financial and Business Interests (Sec. 51)

  • Upon assumption of office — full disclosure of business and financial interests.
  • Transaction-specific — disclose any business, financial, or professional relationship, or relation by affinity or consanguinity within the fourth civil degree, which may result in a conflict of interest, including:
    • Ownership of stock or capital, or investment, in the entity or firm to which the ordinance/resolution may apply; and
    • Contracts or agreements with any person or entity that the ordinance/resolution under consideration may affect.
  • When: In writing, before participating in deliberations; if not participating, before voting on second/third reading; or when delivering a privilege speech on the matter.
  • Filed with: the Secretary of the Sanggunian or the Secretary of the committee concerned.

  • Sessions are open to the public, unless a closed session is ordered by an affirmative vote of a majority of members present (there being a quorum), in the public interest or for reasons of security, decency, or morality.
  • No two sessions (regular or special) in a single day.

F. Quorum (Sec. 53)

  • A majority of all the members of the sanggunian who have been elected and qualified.
    • "Elected and qualified" = proclaimed and assumed office; unproclaimed contested seats are excluded.
    • The presiding officer is included in computing the quorum (La Carlota City v. Rojo).
  • Business without a quorum is void.
  • No quorum → the presiding officer may declare a recess until a quorum is constituted, or a majority of members present may adjourn from day to day and compel the immediate attendance of absent members — the majority present may arrest absent members (through a designated member, assisted by a peace officer) to compel their presence only.
  • Avelino v. Cuenco (1949) — application: Members beyond the coercive process (abroad) are excluded from the base; 12 of 23 senators constituted a quorum.
    • Detained legislators cannot attend or vote (People v. Jalosjos; Trillanes v. Pimentel).
    • Professor's illustration: Sanggunian of 13; two members abroad and one preventively suspended → base of 10; 6 present = quorum.
    • Impeachment conviction requires 2/3 of "all the Members of the Senate" — debate on whether that means 24 seats or actual sitting members.

G. Approval of Ordinances (Sec. 54)

  • Every ordinance of the SP, Sangguniang Panlungsod, or SB is presented to the Governor or Mayor:
    • Approval: by affixing his signature on each and every page;
    • Veto: return it with his objections to the sanggunian.
  • Approval is discretionary, not ministerial (De los Reyes v. Sandiganbayan).
  • Deemed approved if not acted upon within:
    • 15 days (Governor);
    • 10 days (Mayor) — after receipt.
  • Three modes of approval: (1) signature on each page; (2) inaction within the period; (3) override by 2/3 of all members.
  • Barangay ordinances: Approved by a majority of all SBgy members, signed by the Punong Barangay — no veto.
  • Ordinance vs. resolution:
Metric Ordinance Resolution
Nature Local law of general and permanent character Declaration of sentiment or opinion; temporary character
Readings Third reading mandatory Third reading not required unless decided by majority (IRR Art. 107)
Eminent domain Valid basis Not a valid basis (Parañaque v. V.M. Realty)
  • Courts take judicial notice of ordinances within their territorial jurisdiction (Gallego v. People).

H. Veto Power (Sec. 55)

  • Who: Governor, City Mayor, Municipal Mayor. The Punong Barangay has no veto.
  • Grounds:
    1. The ordinance is ultra vires; or
    2. It is prejudicial to the public welfare — stating his reasons in writing.
  • Item veto — on particular items of:
    1. Appropriation ordinances;
    2. Ordinances or resolutions adopting a local development plan and public investment program; and
    3. Ordinances directing the payment of money or creating liability.
    Effect of item veto on appropriations: the corresponding item of the previous year's appropriation ordinance is deemed reenacted.
  • Veto only once.
  • Override: 2/3 vote of all members → the ordinance becomes effective without the LCE's approval.

J. Effectivity (Sec. 59)

  • General rule: Ordinances take effect ten (10) days after posting (i.e., on the 11th day), unless otherwise stated.
  • Posting:
    • By the Secretary to the sanggunian, within five (5) days after approval;
    • At a bulletin board at the entrance of the capitol or city/municipal/barangay hall, and in at least two (2) other conspicuous places;
    • In Filipino or English and in the local language or dialect understood by the majority.
  • Penal ordinances: The gist must be published in a newspaper of general circulation within the province; if none, posting in all municipalities and cities of the province.
  • HUCs and ICCs: Main features of ordinances published once in a local newspaper of general circulation within the city; absent such, posting in all barangays.

PART VIII. DIRECT DEMOCRACY: RECALL, INITIATIVE, REFERENDUM

A. Recall (Secs. 69–75)

  • Definition: The termination of official relations of an elective local official, by the registered voters, before the end of his term.
  • Sole ground: Loss of confidence.
  • Who may be recalled: Local elective officials (Governor down to Kagawad). Members of Congress cannot be recalled — they are national officers.
  • Loss of confidence is a political question — for the electorate, not the courts (Evardone v. COMELEC).
  • Initiation: Originally (a) petition of voters or (b) Preparatory Recall Assembly (PRA). R.A. 9244 abolished the PRA (abused by incumbents, e.g., the Angono Vice Mayor). Now: only by petition of registered voters.

B. Initiative

  • Definition: The legal process whereby the registered voters directly propose, enact, or amend ordinances or laws.
  • Three kinds (R.A. 6735):
    1. On the Constitution (amendments);
    2. On statutes (national legislation); and
    3. On local legislation.
  • Legislative power is originally in the people; delegated to Congress and sanggunians; reserved through initiative and referendum (Art. VI, Sec. 1).
  • Constitutional initiative — amendments only, never revisions:
    • Defensor-Santiago v. COMELEC (1997): R.A. 6735 is inadequate for initiative on the Constitution.
    • Lambino v. COMELEC (2006): Shift from presidential to parliamentary = revision — only via Con-Ass or Con-Con.
  • Local initiative: In a barangay, 50 registered voters may file the petition (if the sanggunian refuses to act). Once approved, the measure is protected from repeal or amendment by the sanggunian for a period, and thereafter amendable only by a qualified vote of all members. (See Part XI on the exact periods/votes.)

C. Referendum

  • Definition: Submission of an ordinance or resolution already passed by the sanggunian (or law passed by Congress) to the registered voters for approval or rejection.
  • Initiative = citizen-proposed; Referendum = legislature-enacted, people-ratified.

D. Aside — Fabian v. Desierto (1998)

  • Sec. 27, R.A. 6770 (appeals from Ombudsman administrative decisions directly to the SC) is unconstitutional — Congress cannot increase the SC's appellate jurisdiction without its advice and concurrence (Art. VI, Sec. 30). Appeals go to the CA under Rule 43.

  2. SK age. Three versions: 15 to below 18 (09/26 — the original LGC); 18 to 24 (10/03 — R.A. 10742, for SK officials); "15 to 30" (09/19 — that is the age range for KK membership, not for SK officials). This reviewer uses 18–24 for SK officials.
