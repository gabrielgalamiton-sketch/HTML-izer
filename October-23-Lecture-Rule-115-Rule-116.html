RULE 115: RIGHTS OF THE ACCUSED AT THE TRIAL

Rule 115 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure enumerates the procedural and constitutional guarantees accorded to an accused in a criminal prosecution. Under Section 1 of Rule 115, in all criminal prosecutions, the accused is entitled to the following fundamental rights:

1
PRESUMPTION
Innocence until contrary proved beyond reasonable doubt
2
INFORMATION
Nature and cause of the accusation charged
3
COUNSEL & PRESENCE
Present and defend in person and by competent counsel
4
EXEMPTION
Privilege against compelled self-incrimination
Section 1, Rule 115: (a) To be presumed innocent until the contrary is proved beyond reasonable doubt. (b) To be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him. (c) To be present and defend in person and by counsel at every stage of the proceedings, from arraignment to promulgation of the judgment... (d) To testify as a witness in his own behalf but subject to cross-examination on matters covered by direct examination. His silence shall not in any manner prejudice him. (e) To be exempt from being compelled to be a witness against himself. (f) To confront and cross-examine the witnesses against him at the trial... (g) To have compulsory process issued to secure the attendance of witnesses and production of other evidence in his behalf. (h) To have a speedy, impartial and public trial. (i) To appeal in all cases allowed and in the manner prescribed by law.

MODULE 1: PRESUMPTION OF INNOCENCE & QUANTUM OF PROOF

1. Presumption of Innocence

  • Constitutional Basis: The right is anchored in Section 14(2), Article III of the 1987 Constitution, which provides that "In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall be presumed innocent until the contrary is proved".
  • Burden of Proof: The prosecution bears the sole burden of overcoming this presumption. In doing so, the prosecution must rely on the strength of its own evidence and not on the weakness of the defense.
  • Presumption of Regularity vs. Presumption of Innocence: While Section 3(m), Rule 131 establishes the disputable presumption that "official duty has been regularly performed," this administrative presumption cannot preponderate over or defeat the constitutional presumption of innocence. Where the performance of official duty is tainted with irregularities, the presumption of regularity is completely destroyed.

2. Proof Beyond Reasonable Doubt

🔍
1. Fact of Crime
Corpus Delicti established beyond reasonable doubt
👤
2. Identity of Accused
Positive identification as actual perpetrator
⚖️
Required Quantum
Moral certainty produced in an unprejudiced mind
  • Quantum of Evidence: Every criminal conviction requires the prosecution to prove two distinct things beyond reasonable doubt: (1) the fact of the crime (corpus delicti), and (2) the identity of the accused as the perpetrator.
  • Meaning of Proof Beyond Reasonable Doubt: Under Section 2, Rule 133, proof beyond reasonable doubt does not mean such a degree of proof as, excluding possibility of error, produces absolute certainty. Only moral certainty is required—or that degree of proof which produces conviction in an unprejudiced mind.
  • Doubt as to Innocence: The overriding consideration in criminal cases is not whether the court doubts the innocence of the accused, but whether it entertains a reasonable doubt as to his guilt. Where reasonable doubt exists, acquittal must follow as a matter of right.

3. The Equipoise Rule

Evidence in Criminal Case is Evenly Balanced / Inculpatory Facts Have Two Explanations
One explanation consistent with innocence, the other consistent with guilt
↓
Constitutional Presumption of Innocence Tilts the Scales
Evidence fails the requisite test of moral certainty
↓
ACQUITTAL OF THE ACCUSED AS A MATTER OF RIGHT
Cannot convict where moral certainty is absent
  • Definition & Application: The equipoise rule provides that where the evidence in a criminal case is evenly balanced, the constitutional presumption of innocence tilts the scales in favor of the accused.
  • Two-Explanation Test: If the inculpatory facts and circumstances are capable of two or more explanations—one consistent with the innocence of the accused and the other consistent with his guilt—the evidence fails the test of moral certainty, and the court must acquit.
  • Inapplicability: The equipoise rule cannot be invoked where the evidence presented is not equally weighty or where the prosecution’s evidence is overwhelming.

4. Effect of a Plea of Self-Defense

  • Shift in the Burden of Evidence: Generally, the prosecution carries the burden of proof throughout the trial. However, when an accused admits to killing or injuring the victim but invokes self-defense, the burden of evidence shifts to the accused to prove the justifying circumstance by clear, satisfactory, and convincing evidence.
  • Implied Admission: Invoking self-defense inherently carries an admission that the accused committed the physical act.

MODULE 2: RIGHT TO BE INFORMED OF THE NATURE & CAUSE OF ACCUSATION

1. Purpose and Implementation

To satisfy this constitutional mandate, the complaint or information must clearly state the acts or omissions constituting the offense, including all attendant qualifying and aggravating circumstances, in ordinary and concise language sufficient to enable a person of common understanding to know what offense is charged.

2. Strict Jurisprudential Guidelines

  • Facts Control, Not Captions: Circumstances not specifically alleged in the information cannot be appreciated to increase the penalty or upgrade the felony, even if fully proven at trial.
  • Unalleged Qualifying Circumstances: Convicting an accused of a qualified or aggravated form of an offense when the qualifying circumstances were omitted from the charge sheet violates due process. For instance, where an information charges simple carnapping, a court cannot impose the penalty for qualified carnapping (violence/force) if that element was not explicitly alleged.
  • Variance in Identity of Objects or Dates:
    • A fatal discrepancy between the identity of a check described in the information (e.g., check number) and the check presented in evidence nullifies a conviction for violation of B.P. Blg. 22.
    • Similarly, a substantial variance between the date alleged in the information and the date proved by documentary evidence renders the conviction fatally defective.
  • Included Offenses: An accused charged with a specific crime may be validly convicted of a lesser offense necessarily included therein (e.g., conviction for ephedrine possession under a charge involving methamphetamine, where ephedrine is an included precursor).
  • Ambiguity Resolved in Favor of Accused: Ambiguity regarding the identity of the subject matter of the crime (e.g., specific drug seized) must be resolved in favor of the accused, warranting acquittal.

MODULE 3: RIGHT TO COUNSEL & CUSTODIAL INVESTIGATION (R.A. 7438)

1. Scope of the Right to Counsel

  • Constitutional & Statutory Basis: Guaranteed under Section 14(2) and Section 12(1), Article III of the Constitution, and implemented under Section 1(c) of Rule 115 and Republic Act No. 7438.
  • Temporal Reach: The right to counsel attaches from the moment a person is arrested, detained, or subjected to custodial investigation, and continues through every critical stage of the trial up to promulgation of judgment.

2. Custodial Investigation & R.A. 7438

  • Definition: Custodial investigation refers to any questioning initiated by law enforcement authorities after a person has been taken into custody or otherwise deprived of his freedom of action in any significant manner.
  • Extended Meaning under R.A. 7438: Section 2 of R.A. 7438 explicitly extends the concept of custodial investigation to include the practice of issuing an "invitation" or "request for appearance" to a person who is investigated in connection with an offense.
  • Inadmissibility of Uncounselled Admissions: Any extrajudicial confession or admission obtained without the assistance of counsel is inadmissible in evidence, regardless of the absence of physical coercion or the voluntary nature of the statement.

3. Critical Distinctions and Limitations

  • No Right to Counsel in a Police Line-up: A police line-up is not part of custodial interrogation because the process has not yet shifted from the investigatory to the accusatory stage. The suspect is not being interrogated to elicit incriminating statements at that precise point.
  • Choice of Counsel Not Plenary: The right to choose counsel is not absolute. If chosen counsel deliberately fails to appear, the court may appoint a counsel de oficio to prevent unwarranted delays in the administration of justice.
  • Waiver Requirements: Rights under custodial investigation can only be waived in writing AND in the presence of counsel. Silence or mere acquiescence does not constitute a valid waiver.
  • Competent and Independent Counsel: Counsel provided must be willing to fully safeguard the constitutional rights of the accused—not merely perform a routine, perfunctory recitation of rights. A mayor's legal consultant or a police-engaged lawyer lacking independent standing does not meet the legal standard of an "independent counsel".
  • Administrative Proceedings: The right to counsel is not mandatory in purely administrative investigations, as administrative inquiries are conducted to determine fitness for public service rather than criminal liability.

MODULE 4: RIGHT TO SPEEDY TRIAL & SPEEDY DISPOSITION OF CASES

1. Distinctions Between Speedy Trial and Speedy Disposition

Feature Right to Speedy Trial (Rule 115, Sec. 1[h]) Right to Speedy Disposition of Cases (Art. III, Sec. 16)
Applicability Exclusively available to an accused in a criminal case. Available to all parties in all cases (civil, criminal, administrative, judicial, or quasi-judicial).
Forum Invoked strictly before courts of law trying criminal cases. Invoked before any judicial, quasi-judicial, or administrative body (e.g., Ombudsman, CSC).

2. Violations and the Doctrine of Inordinate Delay

  • Test of Violation: The right is violated when proceedings are attended by vexatious, capricious, and oppressive delays, or when long periods elapse without justifiable cause.
  • Reckoning Point (Cagang v. Sandiganbayan Rule): Fact-finding investigations prior to the filing of a formal administrative or criminal complaint are not counted in determining inordinate delay. Inordinate delay is reckoned strictly from the filing of the formal complaint and the initiation of preliminary investigation.
  • No Duty to Follow Up: An accused or respondent has no legal duty to continuously follow up or bring his own case to trial; the burden rests on the State to administer justice without undue delay (Coscolluela v. Sandiganbayan).

3. The Balancing Test (Barker v. Wingo)

The Supreme Court rejects rigid "fixed-time" or "demand-waiver" rules and adopts the balancing test, weighing four ad hoc factors:

1
LENGTH
Length of the delay
2
REASON
Reason for the delay
3
ASSERTION
Defendant's assertion of the right
4
PREJUDICE
Prejudice to defendant (incarceration, anxiety, defense impairment)
  1. Length of delay;
  2. Reason for the delay;
  3. Defendant's assertion of the right; and
  4. Prejudice to the defendant (e.g., oppressive pretrial incarceration, anxiety, impairment of defense).

4. Effects and Remedies

Violation of Trial Time Limits / Inordinate Delay
↓
Motion to Dismiss Filed PRIOR to Trial
Failure to move prior to trial constitutes a waiver (Rule 119)
↓
DISMISSAL EQUIVALENT TO ACQUITTAL ON THE MERITS
Double jeopardy attaches immediately; prosecution barred from refiling
  • Dismissal Equals Acquittal: A dismissal grounded on the violation of the right to speedy trial or speedy disposition is equivalent to an acquittal on the merits and attaches double jeopardy.
  • Remedy under Rule 119: Where trial time limits are violated, the accused must file a Motion to Dismiss prior to trial; failure to move prior to trial constitutes a waiver.

MODULE 5: PRIVILEGE AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION

1. Scope and Meaning of Compulsion

  • Constitutional Provision: Section 17, Article III provides that "No person shall be compelled to be a witness against himself".
  • Testimonial vs. Physical Acts: The privilege protects an individual strictly against testimonial compulsion or the extraction of communicative evidence.
  • Meaning of Compulsion: Compulsion is not limited to physical violence; it encompasses moral coercion or pressure that overbears the free will of the individual.

2. Accused vs. Ordinary Witness

Aspect The Accused Ordinary Witness
Scope of Right May refuse to take the witness stand entirely and decline all questions. Must take the stand and obey a subpoena; can invoke the right only as specific incriminatory questions are asked.
Waiver Waives the privilege by voluntarily testifying in his own behalf (subject to cross-examination on direct matters). Waives the privilege if an incriminatory question is answered without objection.

3. Evidentiary Applications

  • Permissible Physical Exams (Not Covered by Privilege):
    • Physical body examinations, pregnancy tests (Villaflor v. Summers).
    • Extracting body substances, morphine from mouth, or testing for diseases (US v. Tan Teng).
    • Paraffin casting of hands (People v. Fieldan).
    • DNA testing (Herrera v. Alba).
    • Wearing specific clothing or shoes for measurement.
  • Prohibited Incriminatory Compulsions:
    • Forced Re-enactments: Compelling an accused to re-enact a crime is an incriminatory act equivalent to an uncounselled extrajudicial confession (People v. Olvis).
    • Handwriting Exemplars: Compelling an accused to write dictation to compare handwriting requires intelligence and creates non-existent evidence, violating the privilege (Beltran v. Samson). (Distinguished from signing a receipt for seized mail merely to authenticate independent evidence—Marcelo v. Sandiganbayan).

4. Immunity Statutes

  • Transactional Immunity: Completely protects the witness from future prosecution for any crime connected to the testimony.
  • Use-and-Derivative-Use Immunity: Prohibits the use of the witness's testimony or evidence derived therefrom against him in a subsequent prosecution, but does not bar prosecution based on independent evidence.
  • Statutory Grants: Found in the Ombudsman Act (Sec. 17), PCGG (E.O. 14), Witness Protection Act (R.A. 6981), and Parliamentary Privileges.

MODULE 6: RIGHTS TO DEFEND, TESTIFY, CONFRONT, COMPULSORY PROCESS, & APPEAL

1. Right to Defend & Be Present

  • Presence at Trial: The accused has the right to be present at every stage from arraignment to promulgation.
  • Trial In Ausencia: Trial may proceed despite the absence of the accused provided:
    1. he has been arraigned,
    2. he was duly notified of the trial, and
    3. his absence is unjustified.
  • Escape as Waiver: An accused under custody who escapes automatically waives the right to be present on all subsequent trial dates.

2. Right to Testify

  • Limited Cross-Examination: An accused who testifies in his own behalf is subject to cross-examination limited strictly to matters covered in direct examination. (In contrast to an ordinary witness who may be cross-examined on any relevant matter under Rule 132).
  • Silence Not Prejudicial: Choosing not to testify cannot be taken as an admission or used to prejudice the accused.

3. Right to Confrontation & Cross-Examination

  • Purpose: To afford the accused the opportunity to test the truthfulness and accuracy of adverse testimony.
  • Opportunity Suffices: The right does not command that actual cross-examination occur, but that the real opportunity to cross-examine was afforded.
  • Effect of Unjust Deprivation: If a party is deprived of the opportunity to cross-examine an adverse witness through no fault of his own, the direct testimony of that witness must be stricken from the record.
  • Inapplicability: The right of confrontation does not apply in preliminary investigations.

4. Right to Compulsory Process & Appeal

  • Compulsory Process: The right to secure court processes (subpoena ad testificandum and subpoena duces tecum) to compel witness attendance and document production. Failure of a subpoenaed witness to appear warrants arrest for contempt.
  • Right to Appeal: A statutory right that must be exercised in the manner prescribed by law.
  • Scope of Appellate Review: In criminal cases, an appeal throws the entire case wide open for review. The appellate court may correct errors, evaluate unassigned errors, or modify penalties and civil liability even if not raised by the parties. However, factual findings of trial courts regarding witness credibility are accorded highest respect on appeal.

CHAPTER VIII: ARRAIGNMENT AND PLEA (RULE 116)

A. Basic Concepts

1. Meaning and Importance of Arraignment

  • Definition: Arraignment is the formal mode and manner of implementing the constitutional right of an accused to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him.
  • Core Purpose: Its purpose is to formally apprise the accused why he is being prosecuted by the State. As an indispensable requirement of procedural due process, it cannot be regarded lightly or brushed aside peremptorily.
  • Importance in Criminal Proceedings:
    1. Notification of Charges: It is indispensable in bringing the accused to court and notifying him of the reason for his indictment, the specific charges he is bound to face, and the corresponding penalty that could possibly be meted out against him.
    2. First Opportunity to Know the Charge: Arraignment is the precise stage where the accused, for the first time, is given the opportunity to know the exact charge confronting him.
    3. Awareness of Consequences: It ensures that the accused is made fully aware of the possible loss of his freedom or even his life, depending on the severity of the imputed crime.

2. Effect of the Absence of Arraignment

  • Nullity of Proceedings: The total absence of an arraignment results in the nullity of all proceedings conducted before the trial court.
  • Bar to Trial In Absentia: If the accused has not been validly arraigned, he cannot be tried in absentia under Article III, Section 14(2) of the 1987 Constitution. A valid arraignment is a prerequisite before a trial in absentia can proceed.

3. Duty of the Court Before Arraignment

Under Section 6, Rule 116 of the Rules of Court, before proceeding with the arraignment, the trial court has three mandatory duties:

1
INFORM
Inform accused of right to counsel
2
ASK
Ask if accused desires counsel
3
ASSIGN
Assign counsel de oficio if unrepresented
  1. Inform the accused of his right to counsel;
  2. Ask him if he desires to have one; and
  3. Assign a counsel de oficio to defend him, unless the accused:
    • Is explicitly allowed by the court to defend himself in person; or
    • Has already employed a counsel of his own choice.
  • Mandatory Character: This duty is strict and mandatory. The court can only proceed with an arraignment without counsel if a capable accused validly waives his right to counsel and is allowed to represent himself.
  • Protection Against Unwise Waivers: If an accused states he cannot afford a lawyer and is not in a position to validly waive his rights, the judge must appoint a counsel de oficio. The judge cannot simply accede to an accused’s insistence to be arraigned without representation, as the court is duty-bound to protect the constitutional rights of the accused even against his own wishes when he is incapable of validly waiving them.
  • Qualifications of Counsel De Oficio (Sec. 7, Rule 116): The court must appoint members of the bar in good standing who, by reason of their experience and ability, can competently defend the accused. In localities where members of the bar are unavailable, the court may appoint any resident of the province who is of good repute for probity and ability.
  • Reasonable Consultation Time (Sec. 8, Rule 116): The assigned counsel de oficio must be granted a reasonable time to consult with the accused regarding his plea before the court proceeds with the arraignment.

4. Options of the Accused Before Arraignment and Plea

Prior to entering a plea during arraignment, an accused may avail himself of several procedural remedies and options:

(a) Motion for a Bill of Particulars (Sec. 9, Rule 116)

  • Purpose: To enable the accused to properly plead and prepare for trial by clarifying vague or deficient allegations in the complaint or information.
  • Requisites: The motion must specify: (1) the alleged defects of the complaint or information, and (2) the specific details desired.
  • Example: If an information alleges only the month and year of the offense without sufficient detail, the accused should move for a bill of particulars.
  • Waiver: Failure to move for a bill of particulars before entering a plea amounts to a waiver of the defect or detail desired.
  • Note under Continuous Trial Guidelines: A motion for a bill of particulars that does not conform strictly to Section 9, Rule 116 is treated as a prohibited motion.

(b) Motion to Suspend Arraignment (Sec. 11, Rule 116)

Upon proper motion, arraignment may be suspended strictly on the following statutory grounds:

  1. Unsound Mental Condition: The accused appears to suffer from an unsound mental condition that effectively renders him unable to fully understand the charge or to plead intelligently. In such cases, the court must order his mental examination and, if necessary, his confinement in a hospital or asylum.
  2. Prejudicial Question: A valid prejudicial question exists in a pending civil case.
    • Continuous Trial Limitation: Under the Revised Guidelines of Continuous Trial, a motion to suspend based on a prejudicial question is prohibited if no civil case has actually been filed yet.
  3. Pending Petition for Review: A petition for review of the prosecutor's resolution is pending before either the Department of Justice (DOJ) or the Office of the President.
    • 60-Day Cap: The period of suspension shall not exceed sixty (60) days counted from the filing of the petition with the reviewing office.
    • Waiver of DOJ Review Upon Arraignment: Once the accused is arraigned, the Secretary of Justice can no longer entertain an appeal or petition for review, as the accused is deemed to have waived or abandoned the executive appeal by submitting to arraignment.
    • Filing Motion for Reconsideration Before Ombudsman: A pending second motion for reconsideration before the Ombudsman does not suspend or bar arraignment.

(c) Motion to Quash (Rule 117)

  • At any time before entering his plea, the accused may move to quash the complaint or information on any of the statutory grounds listed under Rule 117, Section 3 (e.g., facts do not constitute an offense, lack of jurisdiction, officer had no authority).

(d) Challenge Arrest, Warrant, or Absence of Preliminary Investigation (Sec. 26, Rule 114)

  • Objection to Custody/Arrest: Any objection against the legality of the arrest or the acquisition of jurisdiction over the person of the accused must be raised at or before arraignment.
  • Waiver of Preliminary Investigation: Submitting to arraignment constitutes a waiver of the right to a preliminary investigation or reinvestigation, which is tantamount to agreeing to a finding of probable cause.

5. Arraignment Under an Amended Information vs. Substituted Information

When a charge sheet is modified or replaced, the necessity of a new arraignment depends on whether the alteration is formal or substantial, or if it constitutes a substitution:

Modification Type Nature & Test Re-Arraignment & Preliminary Investigation
Substantial Amendment (Post-Plea) Modifies the nature of offense, essential elements, or changes defense averments. Mandatory Re-arraignment. Proceeding to trial without re-arraigning constitutes reversible due process error.
Formal Amendment Merely formal defects (e.g., changing alleged date of homicide where nature/elements unaffected). No need for a new preliminary investigation or retaking of plea.
Substituted Information Original charge replaced by completely different offense. Requires new preliminary investigation and accused must plead anew.
  • Substantial Amendment (Post-Plea): Where the information has already been pleaded to and is subsequently substantially amended, re-arraignment on the amended information is mandatory. Proceeding to trial and conviction under a substantially amended information without re-arraigning the accused constitutes reversible error because it infringes on his constitutional right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation.
  • Formal Amendment: If the amendment is merely as to form, there is no need for a new preliminary investigation or the retaking of the plea of the accused.
    • Example: A post-plea amendment changing merely the alleged date of commission of a homicide—where the change does not alter the nature of the crime, does not affect the essence of the offense, and does not deprive the accused of the opportunity to meet the new averment—is a formal amendment that does not require a second plea.
  • Substituted Information: In a substitution of information, the original charge is replaced by a completely different offense. Substitution entails a new preliminary investigation, and the accused must plead anew to the substituted information.

CHAPTER VIII: ARRAIGNMENT AND PLEA (RULE 116)

B. HOW ARRAIGNMENT AND PLEA ARE MADE

1. Where Arraignment is to be Made

Section 1(a), Rule 116 (in part): "The accused must be arraigned before the court where the complaint or information was filed or assigned for trial..."
  • Jurisdictional Forum: The arraignment must be conducted before the specific court where the complaint or information was officially filed or to which the case was assigned for trial following raffle.

2. How Arraignment is Made

Section 1(a), Rule 116: "The accused must be arraigned before the court where the complaint or information was filed or assigned for trial. The arraignment shall be made in open court by the judge or clerk by furnishing the accused with a copy of the complaint or information, reading the same in the language or dialect known to him, and asking him whether he pleads guilty or not guilty. The prosecution may call at the trial witnesses who are not named in the complaint or information."

  3. Reading in Known Language/Dialect: The information must be read to the accused in a language or dialect known to and understood by him.
  4. Entering a Plea: The judge or clerk formally asks the accused whether he pleads guilty or not guilty.

3. Waiver of Reading of the Information

  • General Rule: The reading of the complaint or information is mandatory to satisfy the constitutional requirement that the accused be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation.
  • Exception under Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial in Criminal Cases (A.M. No. 15-06-10-SC):
    "In multiple cases, the court, upon personal examination of the accused, may allow a waiver of the reading of the information upon the full understanding and express consent of the accused and his/her counsel, which consent shall be expressly stated in both the minutes/certificate of arraignment and the order of arraignment. The court shall explain the waiver to the accused in the language or dialect known to him/her, and ensure the accused's full understanding of the consequences of the waiver before approving the same."

4. When Arraignment is to be Made (Bar 2012)

A. Rule 116, Section 1(g) Baseline Period:

  • Unless a shorter period is provided by special law or Supreme Court circular, the arraignment shall be held within thirty (30) days from the date the court acquires jurisdiction over the person of the accused.

    • Non-Detained Accused: Set within thirty (30) calendar days from the date the court acquires jurisdiction (either by arrest or voluntary surrender).
  2. Simultaneous Setting: Arraignment and pre-trial must be set on the same date and held simultaneously to eliminate procedural delays.
  3. Summary Procedure Cases: Arraignment and the preliminary conference are held simultaneously.

5. Arraignment After Submission of the Case for Decision

  • Issue: Can a late arraignment conducted after trial and after the case has been submitted for decision cure the initial failure to arraign the accused?
  • Jurisprudential Doctrine (People v. Pangilinan, 518 SCRA 358):
    • Belated arraignment does not prejudice the accused where his defense counsel actively participated in the trial without raising any objection that his client had not yet been arraigned.
    • Counsel's active participation (e.g., cross-examining prosecution witnesses, presenting defense evidence) demonstrates that the accused was fully aware of the charges against him.
    • Jurisdiction Over Person: The court acquires jurisdiction over the person of the accused upon his arrest or voluntary appearance—not upon arraignment. Thus, active participation during trial cures the procedural defect of a belated arraignment.

6. Record of Arraignment

Section 1(b), Rule 116 (in part): "The arraignment and plea shall be made of record, but failure to do so shall not affect the validity of the proceedings."
  • While the court is required to record the arraignment and plea in the minutes and order, an omission or failure in the record-keeping does not invalidate the proceedings if an arraignment actually took place in open court.

7. Presence of the Accused

Section 1(b), Rule 116 (in part): "The accused must be present at the arraignment and must personally enter his plea..."
  • Personal Plea Required: The accused must be physically present and must personally enter his plea.
  • Prohibition Against Plea by Proxy: Counsel cannot enter a plea on behalf of the accused.
  • Prerequisite for Trial In Absentia: An accused cannot be validly tried in absentia under Article III, Section 14(2) of the Constitution unless he has been first validly arraigned and entered his plea in person.

8. Presence of the Offended Party

Section 1(f), Rule 116: "The private offended party shall be required to appear at the arraignment for the purpose of plea bargaining, determination of civil liability, and other matters requiring his presence. In case the offended party fails to appear despite due notice, the court may allow the accused to enter a plea of guilty to a lesser offense which is necessarily included in the offense charged with the conformity of the trial prosecutor alone."

Purposes of Requiring Offended Party's Presence:

  1. Plea bargaining negotiations;
  2. Determination of civil liability (ex delicto); and
  3. Resolving other matters requiring personal presence.
  • Effect of Unjustified Absence: If the private offended party fails to appear despite due notice, the court may allow plea bargaining (plea of guilty to a lesser included offense) upon the conformity of the trial prosecutor alone.
  • Victimless Crimes: Under the Continuous Trial Guidelines, in victimless crimes where there is no private offended party, the presence of the arresting officer is required to give consent, with the conformity of the public prosecutor.

9. When a Plea of "Not Guilty" Shall Be Entered (Bar 1992, 1993, 1996)

Section 1(c), Rule 116: "If the accused refuses to plead or makes a conditional plea, a plea of not guilty shall be entered for him."
Section 1(d), Rule 116: "When the accused pleads guilty but presents exculpatory evidence, his plea shall be deemed withdrawn and a plea of not guilty shall be entered for him."

Instances Where a Plea of "Not Guilty" Must Be Entered:

1
EXPLICIT PLEA
Accused expressly pleads "not guilty"
2
STANDS MUTE
Accused refuses to enter any plea
3
CONDITIONAL
Guilty plea subject to conditional terms/penalty
4
EXCULPATORY
Plea of guilty but presents exculpatory evidence
  1. Actual Entry: The accused explicitly pleads "not guilty".
  2. Refusal to Plead: The accused stands mute or refuses to enter a plea.
  3. Conditional Plea: The accused enters a conditional plea (e.g., pleading guilty provided a specific light penalty is imposed).
  4. Plea of Guilty Accompanied by Exculpatory Evidence: The accused pleads guilty, but during the hearing or presentation of evidence to prove mitigating circumstances, introduces evidence that exculpates him or establishes a complete defense (e.g., self-defense).

Key Jurisprudential Clarifications:

  • Exculpatory Evidence (People v. Balisacan, 17 SCRA 1119): When an accused pleads guilty but subsequently testifies that he acted in self-defense, his plea of guilty is automatically vacated and a plea of "not guilty" must be entered. If the court renders a judgment of acquittal without re-arraigning him or entering a "not guilty" plea, there is no standing plea, and the judgment is void (meaning double jeopardy does not attach).
  • Pleas for Leniency vs. Conditional Pleas (People v. Comendador, 100 SCRA 155): A plea of guilty accompanied by a request for a lighter penalty is not a conditional plea if the accused unconditionally admits all material allegations of the information without qualifying his admission.

10. Plea of Guilty as a Judicial Confession; Effect on Aggravating Circumstances

  • General Rule: A plea of guilty is a judicial confession—an unqualified admission of all the material facts alleged in the complaint or information, including aggravating circumstances.
  • Mitigating Circumstance: An unconditional plea of guilty entered during arraignment serves as a mitigating circumstance under Article 13(7) of the Revised Penal Code.

11. Exception to the Admission of Aggravating Circumstances

  • The Rule of Exception (People v. Gravino, 122 SCRA 123; People v. Comendador): Although a plea of guilty generally admits aggravating circumstances alleged in the information, aggravating or qualifying circumstances that are disproved by the evidence must be disallowed in the judgment.
  • Disproved Circumstances: A plea of guilty cannot be held to include qualifying/aggravating circumstances (such as treachery or evident premeditation) if the evidence adduced before the court fails to establish their actual existence.
  • Duty of Court: A plea of guilty does not dispense with the presentation of evidence if the court receives evidence to determine the proper penalty or where the offense is capital.

12. Plea of Guilty to a Lesser Offense; Plea Bargaining (Bar 1995, 2002, 2012)

Section 2, Rule 116: "At arraignment, the accused, with the consent of the offended party and prosecutor, may be allowed by the court to plead guilty to a lesser offense which is necessarily included in the offense charged. After arraignment but before trial, the accused may still be allowed to plead guilty to said lesser offense after withdrawing his plea of not guilty. No amendment of the complaint or information is necessary."
  • Definition: Plea bargaining is a process whereby the accused and the prosecution work out a mutually satisfactory disposition of the case subject to court approval, usually involving the accused pleading guilty to a lesser offense or to fewer counts in exchange for a lighter sentence.

13. Requisites for a Plea of Guilty to a Lesser Offense (Bar 2012)

Under Section 2, Rule 116, for a plea of guilty to a lesser offense to be valid, all of the following requisites must concur:

🧩
1. Lesser Included
Offense necessarily included in charge
🤝
2. Offended Party
Express consent (waived if absent on due notice)
⚖️
3. Prosecutor
Express consent of trial prosecutor
🏛️
4. Court Approval
Sound judicial discretion of trial court
  1. Lesser Included Offense: The lesser offense must be necessarily included in the offense charged.
  2. Consent of Offended Party: Express consent of the private offended party (or the arresting officer in victimless crimes).
    • Exception: Consent is waived if the offended party fails to appear at arraignment despite due notice.
  3. Consent of Prosecutor: Express consent of the trial prosecutor.
  4. Court Approval: Approval by the trial court in the exercise of its sound judicial discretion.

Meaning of "Necessarily Included" (Bar 2002):

An offense is necessarily included in another when some of the essential ingredients of the former constitute the latter, or when the essential ingredients of the former constitute or form part of those constituting the latter (Rule 120, Sec. 5).

  • Example: A person charged with Robbery or Theft cannot plead guilty to Estafa, because the essential elements of Estafa are not included in Robbery or Theft.

14. Plea Bargaining in Drug Cases & Unconstitutionality of Section 23, R.A. 9165

A. Statutory Prohibition and Unconstitutionality (Estipona v. Lobrigo, G.R. No. 226679, Aug. 15, 2017):

  • Section 23 of R.A. 9165 previously declared that "Any person charged under any provision of this Act regardless of the imposable penalty shall not be allowed to avail of the provision on plea bargaining."
  • The Ruling: In Estipona, the Supreme Court En Banc unanimously declared Section 23 of R.A. 9165 UNCONSTITUTIONAL for encroaching upon the Supreme Court's exclusive rule-making power under Section 5(5), Article VIII of the 1987 Constitution. Plea bargaining is fundamentally a rule of procedure.

B. Adoption of the Plea Bargaining Framework in Drug Cases (A.M. No. 18-03-16-SC):

  • Following Estipona, the Supreme Court promulgated A.M. No. 18-03-16-SC, allowing plea bargaining in drug cases under strict frameworks (e.g., an accused charged with illegal possession of dangerous drugs under Sec. 11 may plea bargain to possession of drug equipment under Sec. 12).

15. When Plea of Guilty to a Lesser Offense May Be Made

  1. At Arraignment: Before entering a plea to the original charge.
  2. After Arraignment but Before Trial: By withdrawing the prior plea of "not guilty".
  3. During Trial / Post-Prosecution Rest (Daan v. Sandiganbayan; Estipona v. Lobrigo):
    • Plea bargaining is allowed during arraignment, pre-trial, or even up to the point when the prosecution has rested its case.
    • Requirement After Prosecution Rests: If the motion to plead to a lesser offense is made after the prosecution rests, it can only be allowed if the evidence on record shows that the prosecution lacks sufficient evidence to establish guilt for the grave crime charged.

16. Plea of Guilty to a Capital Offense (Bar 1993, 1995, 1996)

Section 3, Rule 116: "When the accused pleads guilty to a capital offense, the court shall conduct a searching inquiry into the voluntariness and full comprehension of the consequences of his plea and shall require the prosecution to prove his guilt and the precise degree of his culpability. The accused may present evidence in his behalf."

17. Meaning of "Searching Inquiry"

Guidelines Under Jurisprudence (People v. Pastor, 379 SCRA 181; People v. Talusan, 592 SCRA 709):

The trial judge must strictly perform the following during a searching inquiry:

1
CUSTODY CONDITIONS
Check detention background & whether coercion occurred
2
DEFENSE COUNSEL
Verify counsel conferred & explained consequences
3
PERSONALITY PROFILE
Check age, education, and capacity to comprehend
4
EXACT PENALTY
Warn of mandatory imprisonment & civil liabilities
  1. Ascertain Custody and Interrogation Conditions: Inquire how the accused was brought into custody, whether he had competent counsel during custodial investigation, and if he suffered threats or coercion.
  2. Question Defense Counsel: Verify whether counsel conferred with and fully explained the consequences of a guilty plea to the accused.
  3. Elicit Personality Profile: Check the age, education, and socio-economic background of the accused to gauge his capacity for an informed plea.
  4. Explain Exact Penalty & Imprisonment: Inform the accused of the exact length of imprisonment and that a guilty plea carries mandatory conviction and civil liability.
  5. Explain Elements of Offense: Ensure the accused understands the specific legal elements of the crime charged.
  6. Inquire Into Re-enactment/Details: Require the accused to narrate the tragedy or missing details to verify actual guilt.

18. Plea of Guilty to a Non-Capital Offense (Bar 1993, 1995)

Section 4, Rule 116: "When the accused pleads guilty to a non-capital offense, the court may receive evidence from the parties to determine the penalty to be imposed."
  • Directory Nature: Unlike Section 3 (which is mandatory), receiving evidence under Section 4 is discretionary ("may").
  • No Searching Inquiry Required: The court is not required to conduct the rigid searching inquiry mandated for capital offenses (People v. Madraga, 344 SCRA 628).

19. Where No Plea Bargaining or Plea of Guilty Takes Place

Under the Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial (A.M. No. 15-06-10-SC):

  • If the accused does not enter a plea of guilty (whether to a lesser offense or to the offense charged), the court shall immediately proceed with the arraignment and the pre-trial.

20. Improvident Plea of Guilty

Section 5, Rule 116: "At any time before the judgment of conviction becomes final, the court may permit an improvident plea of guilty to be withdrawn and substituted by a plea of not guilty."
  • Definition: An improvident plea is a plea of guilty entered without full comprehension of its meaning, legal nature, and consequences.
  • Effect of Improvident Plea (People v. Gumimba, 517 SCRA 25; People v. Solamillo):
    • A conviction based solely on an improvident plea of guilty to a capital offense will be set aside on appeal.
    • Independent Evidence Exception: If the trial court received independent, credible evidence establishing the guilt of the accused beyond reasonable doubt, the conviction will be sustained despite the improvident plea, because the conviction rests on independent evidence rather than the guilty plea.

21. Production or Inspection of Material Evidence (Bar 2009)

Section 10, Rule 116: "Upon motion of the accused showing good cause and with notice to the parties, the court, in order to prevent surprise, suppression, or alteration, may order the prosecution to produce and permit the inspection and copying or photographing of any written statements given by the complainant and other witnesses in any investigation of the offense conducted by the prosecution or other investigating officers, as well as any designated documents, papers, books, accounts, letters, photographs, objects, or tangible things not otherwise privileged, which constitute or contain evidence material to any matter involved in the case and which are in the possession or under the control of the prosecution, police, or other law investigating agencies."
  • Mode of Discovery: Section 10 acts as a specific mode of discovery in criminal cases.
  • Purpose: To prevent surprise, suppression, or alteration of material evidence in the possession or control of the prosecution or police.
  • Requisites: (1) Motion filed by accused; (2) Showing of good cause; (3) Notice to adverse parties; and (4) Items are non-privileged and material to the case.

Before entering a plea during arraignment, an accused has several procedural remedies and options available under Rule 116 and related provisions of the Rules of Court:

1
BILL OF PARTICULARS
Clarify vague information (Sec. 9)
2
SUSPEND ARRAIGNMENT
Mental / Prejudicial / DOJ Review (Sec. 11)
3
QUASH INFORMATION
Statutory grounds under Rule 117
4
DISCOVERY INSPECTION
Inspect material evidence (Sec. 10)

1. Motion for a Bill of Particulars (Rule 116, Section 9)

  • Purpose: To enable the accused to properly plead and prepare for trial when the complaint or information is vague, indefinite, or lacks sufficient factual details.
  • Requirements: The motion must specifically set forth:
    1. The alleged defects of the complaint or information; and
    2. The specific details desired.
  • Effect of Failure/Waiver: Failure to file a motion for a bill of particulars prior to entering a plea constitutes a waiver of any defect or detail that could have been clarified.
  • Continuous Trial Note: A motion for a bill of particulars that fails to strictly conform to Section 9 of Rule 116 is classified as a prohibited motion under the Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial.

2. Motion to Suspend Arraignment (Rule 116, Section 11)

Arraignment may be suspended upon proper motion only on the following exclusive statutory grounds:

  1. Unsound Mental Condition (Sec. 11[a]): The accused appears to be suffering from an unsound mental condition that renders him unable to fully understand the charge or enter an intelligent plea. The court must order a mental examination and, if warranted, the accused's commitment to a hospital or asylum.
  2. Prejudicial Question (Sec. 11[b]): A valid prejudicial question exists in a previously instituted civil action. Under the Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial, a petition to suspend on this ground is prohibited if no civil case has actually been filed in court.
  3. Pending Petition for Review (Sec. 11[c]): A petition for review of the prosecutor's resolution is pending before either the Department of Justice (DOJ) or the Office of the President.
    • 60-Day Limitation: The period of suspension shall not exceed sixty (60) days counted from the filing of the petition with the reviewing office.
    • Waiver Rule: If the accused submits to arraignment while a petition for review is pending, the petition is deemed waived or abandoned, and the Secretary of Justice can no longer entertain the appeal.

3. Motion to Quash (Rule 117, Section 3)

  • Purpose: At any time before entering a plea, the accused may move to quash the complaint or information to challenge its legal validity or the court's authority.
  • Grounds: Governed by Section 3 of Rule 117 (e.g., facts charged do not constitute an offense, lack of jurisdiction over the offense, officer filing had no authority, double jeopardy, or extinction of criminal liability).

4. Challenge to Illegal Arrest, Warrant, or Absence of Preliminary Investigation (Rule 114, Section 26)

  • Objection to Arrest / Personal Jurisdiction: Any objection regarding the legality of the arrest or the acquisition of jurisdiction over the person of the accused must be raised at or before arraignment.
  • Waiver of Defects: Entering a plea during arraignment without objection operates as a complete waiver of any defect in the arrest and cures any procedural defect in acquiring jurisdiction over the person.
  • Waiver of Preliminary Investigation: Submitting to arraignment without challenging the lack of a preliminary investigation constitutes a waiver of the right to a preliminary investigation or reinvestigation, which is legally tantamount to agreeing to a finding of probable cause.

5. Motion for Production or Inspection of Material Evidence (Rule 116, Section 10)

  • Purpose: Serves as a specific mode of discovery in criminal proceedings to prevent surprise, suppression, or alteration of evidence.
  • Scope: Upon motion of the accused showing good cause and with notice to all parties, the court may order the prosecution to produce and permit the inspection, copying, or photographing of non-privileged written statements, documents, books, papers, photographs, or tangible objects material to the case in the possession or control of the prosecution or law enforcement agencies.

Plea bargaining is the procedural process whereby the accused and the prosecution work out a mutually satisfactory disposition of a criminal case, subject to court approval. It typically involves the accused pleading guilty to a lesser offense (or to fewer counts in a multi-count indictment) in exchange for a lighter sentence or penalty than that imposed for the graver offense charged.

1. Requisites for a Valid Plea Bargaining (Rule 116, Section 2)

Under Section 2, Rule 116 of the Rules of Court, for an accused to validly plead guilty to a lesser offense, all of the following requisites must concur:

⚖️
1. Included Offense
Lesser offense necessarily included in charged offense
👤
2. Offended Party
Express consent (or arresting officer in victimless crimes)
🖋️
3. Trial Prosecutor
Express consent of prosecuting officer
🏛️
4. Court Approval
Sound judicial discretion (not demandable as right)
  1. Lesser Included Offense: The lesser offense to which the accused intends to plead guilty must be necessarily included in the offense charged in the complaint or information.
    • Meaning of "Necessarily Included": An offense is necessarily included when its essential elements form part of or constitute the essential elements of the crime actually charged.
    • Example: A person charged with Robbery or Theft cannot plead guilty to Estafa, because the essential elements of Estafa are not included in Robbery or Theft.
  2. Consent of the Offended Party: Express consent of the private offended party.
    • Exception: The consent of the private offended party is not required if that party fails to appear at the arraignment despite due notice (Sec. 1[f], Rule 116).
    • Victimless Crimes: Under the Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial (A.M. No. 15-06-10-SC), in victimless crimes where there is no private offended party, the consent of the arresting officer is required, with the conformity of the public prosecutor.
  3. Consent of the Trial Prosecutor: Express consent of the prosecuting officer.
  4. Approval of the Court: Approval by the trial court in the exercise of its sound judicial discretion. Acceptance of a plea bargain is not demandable as a matter of right by the accused.

2. When Plea Bargaining May Be Made

  • At Arraignment: Before entering a plea to the original charge sheet.
  • After Arraignment but Before Trial: By withdrawing the prior plea of "not guilty".
  • During Trial / After Prosecution Rests: Plea bargaining may be considered during pre-trial or even after the prosecution has rested its case (Daan v. Sandiganbayan; Estipona v. Lobrigo).
    • Condition After Prosecution Rests: If the motion is made after the prosecution rests, it can only be granted if the evidence on record demonstrates that the prosecution lacks sufficient evidence to establish guilt for the graver offense charged.

3. Plea Bargaining in Drug Cases (Estipona v. Lobrigo)

  • Statutory Prohibition: Section 23 of Republic Act No. 9165 (Comprehensive Dangerous Drugs Act of 2002) originally prohibited plea bargaining in all drug cases regardless of penalty.
  • Unconstitutionality (Estipona v. Lobrigo, G.R. No. 226679, Aug. 15, 2017): The Supreme Court En Banc declared Section 23 of R.A. 9165 unconstitutional for encroaching upon the Supreme Court's exclusive rule-making authority over pleading, practice, and procedure under Section 5(5), Article VIII of the 1987 Constitution.
  • Current Framework: Following Estipona, the Supreme Court promulgated A.M. No. 18-03-16-SC (Adoption of the Plea Bargaining Framework in Drug Cases), explicitly allowing plea bargaining in specific drug violations under strict parameters.
