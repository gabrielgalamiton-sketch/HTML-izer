  • Governing Law (Art. 1058): The appointment, status, rights, and duties of executors and administrators of estates of deceased persons are governed by the Rules of Court (while Art. 1059 applies preference of credits for insufficient estate assets and Art. 1060 allows trust corporations to serve as estate representatives).

  • Prohibition Against Purchase by Executor or Administrator (Art. 1491, No. 3):
    1. The executor or administrator is strictly prohibited from acquiring by purchase, even at a public or judicial auction, either in person or through the mediation of another, the property of the estate under his administration.
    2. Nature of Nullity: Such a sale is void, and not merely voidable (Rubias v. Batiller, 51 SCRA 120).
    3. Cessation of Prohibition: The prohibition no longer applies after the final settlement of the estate. The void transaction may then be ratified by a new contract, which becomes valid only from its execution and does not retroact to the date of the first contract.

I. General Concepts, Meanings, Purposes, and Requisites of Collation (Article 1061)

Article 1061: The Duty of Compulsory Heirs to Collate

Article 1061. Every compulsory heir, who succeeds with other compulsory heirs, must bring into the mass of the estate any property or right which he may have received from the decedent, during the lifetime of the latter, by way of donation, or any other gratuitous title, in order that it may be computed in the determination of the legitime of each heir, and in the account of the partition. (1035a)

A. Big Picture & Intuitive Foundation

Whenever a parent or ascendant transfers property gratuitously during life to a compulsory heir, the law establishes an equitable presumption: the lifetime gift was not intended as favoritism, but as an advance payment on that heir's eventual inheritance. Collation is the statutory accounting device that fictitiously returns these advances to the hereditary mass so that every compulsory heir finishes with their equal, lawful share.

Simple Illustration (Unified across Paras, Jurado, and Rabuya): Suppose a father has ₱1,000,000 in total wealth and two children, X and Y. During his life, he donates ₱100,000 to X and dies intestate leaving ₱900,000 cash. Without collation, splitting ₱900,000 gives X ₱550,000 total (₱100k gift + ₱450k inheritance) and Y only ₱450,000. Under collation, we fictitiously add ₱100,000 back to ₱900,000 to establish a ₱1,000,000 estate base (₱500,000 equal share each). Because X already received ₱100,000 in advance, X takes only ₱400,000 from the remaining cash while Y takes ₱500,000—both ending with exactly ₱500,000.

B. Definition and the Technical Meanings of "Collation"

Classical Definition (7 Manresa 541): Jurado Collation is the act of returning or restoring to the common mass of the hereditary estate, either actually or fictitiously, any property or right which a person may have received from the decedent during the latter's lifetime, but which is understood for legal purposes as an advance from the inheritance.

Sense / Acceptation Statutory Basis Operational Meaning & Primary Purpose
1. Mathematical Computation Jurado Rabuya Arts. 908 & 909 Purely fictitious arithmetic addition of all donations inter vivos (whether to compulsory heirs or to strangers) to the net value of the hereditary estate to determine total legitimes and the disposable free portion.
2. Imputation / Charging Paras Jurado Rabuya Arts. 1061–1077 The governing sense of Section 5 (Arellano v. Pascual, 638 SCRA 826). Adding the donation to the net estate plus charging (imputing) it against the donee-heir's legitime (or against the free portion if dispensed, repudiated, or given to a stranger). Immediate purpose: to compute each heir's legitime and account of partition; ultimate purpose: equality among compulsory heirs.
3. Actual Restitution / Reduction Jurado Rabuya Arts. 911–912 in rel. to Art. 1076 Actual physical return to the estate of that part of a lifetime donation found to be inofficious because it exceeds the disposable free portion and impairs legitimes.
  • Paras's Operational Formulation of "Collation" & "Not Collationable" Paras:
    1. First Meaning of Collation: Computing/adding values to the estate and charging them to the LEGITIME (Arts. 1061–1064) — applies to ordinary donations inter vivos to compulsory heirs.
    2. Second Meaning of Collation: Computing/adding values to the estate and charging them to the FREE PORTION (Arts. 1062–1063) — applies when collation is dispensed with, when the donee repudiates, or when donated to strangers.
    3. First Nuance of "Not Collationable": Computed and added to the estate, but charged to the free portion rather than the legitime.
    4. Second Nuance of "Not Collationable": NOT even computed or added to the estate at all, because it forms no part of the hereditary estate (e.g., Art. 1067 support and medical expenses).
  • Successional Scope: Collation operates in both testamentary succession (as compulsory heirs) and legal/intestate succession (as legal heirs) Paras Rabuya.
  • "Collation in Value" vs. "Collation in Kind" Paras:
    1. General Rule (Collation in Value): Under Art. 1071, the physical things donated are not returned; only their monetary value at the time of donation is brought to collation.
    2. Exception (Returning in Kind): Occurs strictly when the donation must be totally reduced or revoked as completely inofficious and the donee either has no money or does not wish to reimburse the estate in cash.

D. Covered Parties, Exclusions, and Collatable Transfers

  • Strangers vs. Compulsory Heirs: Donations to strangers are still collated in the first sense (computation under Arts. 908–909) to determine if legitimes are impaired, but are imputed against the disposable free portion rather than a legitime (Lucerna Vda. de Tupas v. RTC, G.R. No. 65800) Paras Jurado Rabuya.
  • Why the Surviving Spouse is Excluded from Art. 1061 Paras Rabuya:
    1. Although a compulsory heir, donations or grants of gratuitous advantage between spouses during the marriage are expressly null and void under former Art. 133 of the Civil Code (now Art. 87 of the Family Code); ownership never left the donor's estate.
    2. Moderate festive gifts (e.g., birthday or anniversary gifts on occasions of family rejoicing) are valid but not subject to collation.
    3. Donations Propter Nuptias: Gifts given to a future spouse prior to marriage are treated as donations to strangers (imputed to the free portion), because the recipient was not yet a compulsory heir at the time of donation Paras.
  • Direct and Indirect Transfers Subject to Collation:
    1. Ordinary deeds of donation inter vivos Paras Jurado Rabuya.
    2. Donations propter nuptias (subject to Art. 1070 / stranger rules) Jurado.
    3. Condonation, release, or remission of an heir's debt Paras Jurado Rabuya.
    4. Renunciation of an inheritance in favor of a determinate compulsory heir Paras Rabuya.
    5. Sums paid by a parent to satisfy a child's debts, election campaign outlays, and fines (Art. 1069) Paras.
    6. Repairs and improvements made by the decedent on tenements belonging to an heir Rabuya.
    7. Purchase of immovables by the decedent directly in the name of an heir Rabuya.
  • Life Insurance & Mutual Benefit Exclusion: Proceeds of a life insurance policy taken out by the decedent on his own life in favor of a compulsory heir are NOT collatable, because they arise from contract rather than a donation from the estate (Southern Luzon Employees Ass'n v. Gulpeo, L-6114 Paras; Del Val v. Del Val, 29 Phil. 534 Rabuya). Mutual benefit contracts follow the same rule.
Worked Problem 1.1: Testamentary Disposition and Lifetime Advance Paras

Facts: Father A has two legitimate children, B and C. During life, A donated ₱100,000 to B. In his will, A left his remaining physical estate of ₱900,000 as follows: ₱150,000 to B, ₱250,000 to C, and ₱500,000 to a friend, F. Upon A's death, B complains that his legitime has been impaired. Is B correct?

  1. Collation (Estate Base): Remaining estate (₱900,000) + lifetime gift to B (₱100,000) = ₱1,000,000 net hereditary estate.
  2. Legitime Computation: Legitime of legitimate children = 1/2 of ₱1,000,000 = ₱500,000 → ₱250,000 each for B and C. Disposable free portion = ₱500,000.
  3. Imputation: B's ₱100,000 lifetime gift is an advance on his legitime. Added to his ₱150,000 testamentary share, B receives ₱250,000 total, fully satisfying his legitime. C gets ₱250,000, and F's ₱500,000 legacy fits squarely within the free portion.
  4. Ruling: No. B has no ground to complain.

E. Controlling Jurisprudence Under Article 1061

  • Buhay De Roma v. CA (L-46903, July 23, 1987) Paras: A clause stating that a donation is "irrevocable" does NOT exempt it from collation. An express prohibition against collation is required to dispense with it.
  • Sanchez v. CA (87 SCAD 463, 1997) Paras: Duly notarized deeds of absolute sale enjoy the presumption of validity; Art. 1061 covers only gratuitous transfers and does not apply to bona fide onerous sales.
  • Zaragoza v. CA (G.R. No. 106401, Sept. 29, 2000) Paras: An inter vivos partition by a parent is valid unless it impairs legitimes; impairment can only be determined after collation by all compulsory heirs, making all co-heirs indispensable parties.
  • Natividad P. Nazareno v. CA (G.R. No. 138842, Oct. 18, 2000) Paras: Where an implied trust exists over properties transferred to an heir, collation is proper to determine shares, but innocent purchasers for value relying on Torrens titles are protected.
  • Guinguing v. Abuton (48 Phil. 144) Paras: Disputes regarding the obligation to collate are properly resolved in the course of estate administration proceedings.

II. Dispensation, Repudiation, and Property Left by Will (Articles 1062 & 1063)

Article 1062: Dispensation with Collation and Repudiation

Article 1062. Collation shall not take place among compulsory heirs if the donor should have so expressly provided, or if the donee should repudiate the inheritance, unless the donation should be reduced as inofficious. (1036)
  • Technical Inaccuracy of "Collation Shall Not Take Place" Jurado Rabuya: This phrase does NOT mean the gift escapes the mathematical computation of Art. 908. Its value must still be added to the net estate to compute legitimes and test for inofficiousness. It strictly means the gift is NOT imputed to the donee's legitime, but is charged against the disposable free portion.
  • Two Grounds Shifting Imputation to the Free Portion:
    1. Express Dispensation by the Donor ("No Collation" Clause): Explicitly stated in the deed of donation or in a will. Purpose: to confer a preference (favoritism) over and above the heir's legitime.
    2. Repudiation of the Inheritance by the Donee: By renouncing, the donee waives his legitime, ceases to be an heir, and is treated as a stranger; the donation is charged entirely to the free portion.
  • Absolute Boundary of Inofficiousness: Under both dispensation and repudiation, if the donation exceeds the disposable free portion and impairs the legitimes of accepting compulsory heirs, the excess must be reduced as inofficious.
Worked Problem 2.1: Express Dispensation Within the Free Portion Paras

Facts: D has two sons, A and B. He donates ₱100,000 to A, expressly stating it is "not collationable." D dies intestate leaving ₱900,000.

Solution: The ₱100,000 is charged to the free portion (which is ₱500,000 out of the ₱1,000,000 base), so it is not inofficious. The remaining ₱900,000 is divided equally: ₱450,000 each. A totals ₱550,000 (₱100k gift + ₱450k inheritance), securing a ₱100k preference over B (₱450,000). Had D stayed silent, A would have received only ₱400,000 cash for exact parity at ₱500,000 each.

Worked Problem 2.2: Express Dispensation Impairing Legitimes Paras

Facts: D has two sons, A and B. He gives A a ₱800,000 donation with an express "no collation" clause. D dies intestate leaving ₱200,000.

Solution: Total estate base = ₱200,000 + ₱800,000 = ₱1,000,000. Legitime (1/2) = ₱500,000 → B's legitime is ₱250,000. Since only ₱200,000 remains, B's legitime is impaired by ₱50,000. Thus, B gets the entire remaining ₱200,000 plus ₱50,000 returned from A's donation. A retains ₱750,000.

Worked Problem 2.3: Acceptance vs. Repudiation with Three Children Paras

Facts: D has three legitimate children (A, B, and C). D donated ₱600,000 to A and dies intestate leaving ₱300,000.

  1. Scenario A (All Accept): Estate base = ₱300,000 + ₱600,000 = ₱900,000. Total legitime (1/2) = ₱450,000 → ₱150,000 each. From the remaining ₱300,000 cash, B and C each get ₱150,000, completely satisfying their legitimes. A's donation is not reduced.
  2. Scenario B (A Repudiates; B and C Accept): Only two compulsory heirs remain. Estate base = ₱900,000. Combined legitime of B and C (1/2) = ₱450,000 (₱225,000 each). Free portion = ₱450,000. Treated as a stranger, A's ₱600,000 donation exceeds the ₱450,000 free portion and must be reduced by ₱150,000.
Worked Problem 2.4: Silent Deed vs. Express Dispensation (Four Sons) Jurado

Facts: In 1960, X donated land to eldest son A worth ₱40,000 at donation (worth ₱200,000 at death). In 1980, X died intestate survived by four sons (A, B, C, D) leaving a net estate of ₱280,000.

  1. Case 1 (Silent Deed — Default Rule): Estate base = ₱280,000 + ₱40,000 (value at donation, not ₱200k) = ₱320,000. Legitime (1/2) = ₱160,000 (₱40,000 each); Free portion = ₱160,000. A's ₱40,000 donation satisfies his full legitime. In intestacy, the remaining free portion (₱160,000) is split equally (₱40,000 each). Final cash distribution of ₱280,000: A gets ₱40,000 (plus ₱40k land = ₱80k total); B, C, and D get ₱80,000 each.
  2. Case 2 (Express Dispensation): The ₱40,000 gift is charged to the ₱160,000 free portion (not inofficious). The remaining ₱280,000 net estate is divided equally among the four sons: ₱70,000 each.

Article 1063: Property Left by Will (Legacies and Devises)

Article 1063. Property left by will is not deemed subject to collation, if the testator has not otherwise provided, but the legitime shall in any case remain unimpaired. (1037)
  • Misleading Codal Phrasing Paras Jurado Rabuya: Legacies and devises consist of assets that never left the estate during life, so nothing is brought back. "Not deemed subject to collation" strictly means they are presumed imputed against the disposable free portion, and NOT charged as an advance on the heir's legitime.
  • Paras's Golden Axiom (Dichotomy of Intent) Paras:
    Type of DispositionGeneral Rule (Presumed Intent)Exception (Express Override)
    Inter Vivos Donations (Art. 1061 / 1062)EQUALITY (charged to legitime)PREFERENCE (if donor expressly dispenses)
    Mortis Causa Legacies/Devises (Art. 1063)PREFERENCE (charged to free portion)EQUALITY (if testator expressly orders collation)
  • Inviolability of Legitimes: Whether charged to the free portion or legitime, a legacy/devise can never impair co-heirs' legitimes; otherwise it is reduced under Art. 911.
  • Inapplicability: Under Marina Dizon-Rivera v. Estela Dizon (L-24561, June 30, 1970) Paras, where a testatrix partitions her entire estate by will without prior lifetime donations, Arts. 1061–1063 do not apply.
Worked Problem 2.5: Legacy to One Child vs. Donation to One, Legacy to Another Paras
  1. Case A (Legacy Only): T has two legitimate children (A and B) and a ₱1,000,000 estate. In his will, T gives A a ₱100,000 legacy with no other provisions. Result: The ₱100,000 legacy comes from the free portion. The remaining ₱900,000 is split equally (₱450,000 each). A ends up with ₱550,000; B gets ₱450,000.
  2. Case B (Donation to A vs. Legacy to B): T had ₱1,000,000. He gave A an inter vivos donation of ₱100,000 and gave B a ₱100,000 legacy in his will, leaving ₱800,000 remaining.
    • Add A's ₱100,000 donation to the ₱800,000 cash = ₱900,000 theoretical estate (÷ 2 = ₱450,000 each).
    • A receives: ₱450,000 − ₱100,000 advance = ₱350,000 cash (Grand total: ₱450,000).
    • B receives: ₱450,000 cash (his legacy is NOT an advance; Grand total: ₱550,000).

III. Collation in Representation, Ascendants, and Spouses (Articles 1064 to 1066)

Article 1064: The Two-Fold Collation of Grandchildren

Article 1064. When grandchildren, who survive with their uncles, aunts, or cousins, inherit from their grandparents in representation of their father or mother, they shall bring to collation all that their parents, if alive, would have been obliged to bring, even though such grandchildren have not inherited the property.

They shall also bring to collation all that they may have received from the decedent during his lifetime, unless the testator has provided otherwise, in which case his wishes must be respected, if the legitime of the co-heirs is not prejudiced. (1038)
  • The Double Collation Burden: When grandchildren inherit by right of representation alongside uncles, aunts, or cousins, they must collate TWO separate classes of gifts:
    1. Paragraph 1 (Parent's Advances): Everything their predeceased, incapacitated, or disinherited parent would have collated if alive. This applies even if the grandchildren never inherited or received the property (e.g., if the parent sold, donated, consumed, or lost it, or if the grandchildren renounced their own parent's inheritance Rabuya). This is an explicit statutory exception to the donee-only rule Paras.
    2. Paragraph 2 (Personal Advances): Everything the grandchildren personally received from the grandparent during life (imputed to their legitime), unless expressly dispensed with by the testator (subject to non-impairment of legitimes).
  • When Inheriting in Their Own Right (Crucial Exception) Paras Rabuya: Art. 1064 applies strictly to representation. If grandchildren inherit in their own right (e.g., when all children repudiate), they collate only what they personally received, NOT what their parents received.
  • Paras Concrete Examples Paras: Grandparent A had children B, C, and D. A gave B a house during life; B later donated it to stranger L and predeceased A, leaving son E. Upon A's death, E inherits with C and D by representation: (1) E must collate the house B gave to L; and (2) if A also donated a second house directly to E, E must collate that second house too unless A expressly dispensed with it.
Worked Problem 3.1: Double Collation by Representation with Stranger's Donation Jurado

Facts: A died intestate in 1970 survived by son B and grandson D (son of predeceased son C). Lifetime donations: (1) 1950 to stranger X = ₱40,000; (2) 1955 to son C = ₱10,000; (3) 1960 to grandson D = ₱10,000. Net estate at death = ₱20,000.

  1. Estate Base: ₱20,000 + ₱40,000 + ₱10,000 + ₱10,000 = ₱80,000.
  2. Legitime & Free Portion: Total legitime (1/2) = ₱40,000 (B = ₱20,000; D by representation = ₱20,000). Free portion (1/2) = ₱40,000.
  3. Imputation: Stranger X's ₱40,000 exhausts the ₱40,000 free portion (not inofficious). Under Art. 1064, D collates both C's ₱10,000 and his own ₱10,000 = ₱20,000, completely satisfying D's legitime in advance.
  4. Cash Distribution: The entire remaining ₱20,000 cash goes exclusively to son B.

Article 1065: Donations to Grandchildren When Parent Inherits in Own Right

Article 1065. Parents are not obliged to bring to collation in the inheritance of their ascendants any property which may have been donated by the latter to their children. (1039)
  • The Rule: If a grandparent donates ₱100,000 to grandchild C, and upon the grandparent's death the child A (C's father) is alive and inherits in his own right, A is NOT obliged to collate what C received (no identity of heir and beneficiary).
  • Does Grandchild C Collate? Paras Jurado:
    1. In the first sense (Art. 908 computation): YES — donations to strangers are added to check if legitimes are impaired.
    2. In the second sense (imputation to legitime): NO — C is excluded by his living father A and is a stranger to the succession; the gift is charged against the disposable free portion (Art. 909).

Article 1066: Donations to the Spouse of a Child

Article 1066. Neither shall donations to the spouse of the child be brought to collation; but if they have been given by the parent to the spouses jointly, the child shall be obliged to bring to collation one-half of the thing donated. (1040)
  • Exclusively to Child's Spouse (Son/Daughter-in-Law): A child-in-law is a stranger to the estate. The child does not collate it; it is charged against the disposable free portion.
  • Jointly to Child and Spouse: Presumed held in equal undivided shares. The child must collate one-half (1/2) of the property (imputed to the child's legitime), while the spouse's 1/2 is charged against the free portion.

IV. Expenses: Exempt, Career/Vocational, Debts/Fines, and Wedding Gifts (Articles 1067 to 1070)

Article 1067: Expenses Mandatorily Exempt from Collation

Article 1067. Expenses for support, education, medical attendance, even in extraordinary illness, apprenticeship, ordinary equipment, or customary gifts are not subject to collation. (1041)
  • Literal & Absolute Exclusion: Their values are NOT added to the estate at all, nor charged to either the legitime or the free portion Paras Jurado. These fulfill legal, moral, and social obligations, and calculating routine living outlays is practically impossible (7 Manresa 626).
  • Prohibitive Nature: Parents cannot validly order or provide that Art. 1067 expenses be collated Jurado Rabuya.
  • Scope of "Education": Covers schooling only up to elementary and high school; college/professional studies fall under Art. 1068 Paras Rabuya.
  • Extraordinary Medical Expenses: Even substantial medical bills (e.g., ₱50,000 spent treating psychoneurosis for two years) are completely exempt Paras.
  • Post-Mortem Allowances Excluded: Support allowances given during judicial estate settlement after death are advances on the inheritance and do not fall under Art. 1067 (Lesaca v. Lesaca, L-3605) Paras.

Article 1068: Expenses for a Professional or Vocational Career

Article 1068. Expenses incurred by the parents in giving their children a professional, vocational or other career shall not be brought to collation unless the parents so provide, or unless they impair the legitime; but when their collation is required, the sum which the child would have spent if he had lived in the house and company of his parents shall be deducted therefrom. (1042a)
  • Scope & Default Rule: Covers college, vocational, graduate, and artistic training (tuition, books, matriculation, examination fees, locally or abroad). Presumed NOT an advance on the legitime (charged to the free portion), unless (1) parents so provide, or (2) they impair the legitime.
  • Mandatory "Living-at-Home" Deduction: Whenever collation is required, the estate must deduct what the child would have spent living at home (7 Manresa 630). Under Adan v. Casili and Adan (76 Phil. 279) Paras Rabuya, absent contrary proof, one-half (50%) of the educational expenses must be deducted as ordinary home support.
  • Expenses Incurred AFTER Completion of Career (7 Manresa 621 / 578–579) Paras Jurado: Art. 1068 applies strictly to expenses in acquiring the career. Outlays made after graduation to establish a practice or business—buying an hacienda, law library, office furniture, medical instruments, pharmacy stock, or maritime vessel—are ordinary donations inter vivos collatable under Art. 1061.

Article 1069: Payment of Debts, Election Expenses, and Fines

Article 1069. Any sums paid by a parent in satisfaction of the debts of his children, election expenses, fines, and similar expenses shall be brought to collation. (1043a)
  • Nature & Enforceability: Paying a child's personal civil debts, election campaign outlays, or criminal fines out of liberality confers a direct benefit and is charged to the child's legitime. The debt must be valid, enforceable, and demandable; prescribed or unenforceable debts confer no legal advantage and are not collated (7 Manresa 635) Paras Rabuya.
  • Crucial Distinction: Donee vs. Debtor (7 Manresa 635–636 / 582–583) Paras Jurado:
    1. Parent as Guarantor / Loan with Expectation of Repayment: Creates a debtor-creditor relationship, NOT collation. If the child repudiates the inheritance, he remains strictly bound to pay the estate the debt.
    2. Parent Paying Out of Pure Liberality / Pity: Constitutes a collatable donation under Art. 1069. If the child repudiates the inheritance, he does NOT have to repay the estate (treated as a stranger under Art. 1062), except to the extent it is inofficious.

Article 1070: Wedding Gifts by Parents and Ascendants

Article 1070. Wedding gifts by parents and ascendants consisting of jewelry, clothing, and outfit, shall not be reduced as inofficious except insofar as they may exceed one-tenth of the sum which is disposable by will. (1044)
  • The 1/10 Free Portion Ceiling: Wedding gifts of jewelry, clothing, and outfit (trousseau) are charged against the disposable free portion up to one-tenth (1/10) of the disposable free portion per child. Only the excess over 1/10 is collated and charged as an advance against the child's legitime (7 Manresa 639).
  • Doctrinal Split on Subject Matter Paras:
    1. Justice Paras's View: By analogy, cash, money, or real property may be included within Art. 1070.
    2. Contrary View (Justices J.B.L. Reyes & Ricardo C. Puno): Money and real property are excluded from the 1/10 privilege of Art. 1070 (and are governed by ordinary collation under Art. 1061).
Worked Problem 4.1: Two Children with Wedding Gifts Paras

Facts: A had two legitimate children, B and C. On their marriages, A gave B a ₱10,000 pin and C a ₱20,000 trousseau. A died intestate leaving ₱190,000.

  1. Estate Base & Free Portion: ₱190,000 + ₱10,000 + ₱20,000 = ₱220,000. Legitime (1/2) = ₱110,000; Free portion (1/2) = ₱110,000.
  2. 1/10 Ceiling: 1/10 of ₱110,000 = ₱11,000. B's ₱10,000 pin is within the ceiling (no reduction). C's ₱20,000 trousseau exceeds ₱11,000 by ₱9,000 → ₱9,000 is charged as an advance against C's legitime.
  3. Partition of ₱190,000: Add ₱9,000 advance to ₱190,000 = ₱199,000 (÷ 2 = ₱99,500 theoretical share). B receives ₱99,500; C receives ₱90,500 (₱99,500 − ₱9,000). Total cash distributed = ₱190,000.
Worked Problem 4.2: Three Daughters — Jewelry Exceeding 1/10 Ceiling Jurado

Facts: Father X gave daughter C ₱40,000 jewelry as a wedding gift in 1975. X died intestate in 1980 survived by three daughters (A, B, C) leaving ₱200,000 net estate.

  1. Estate Base & Free Portion: ₱200,000 + ₱40,000 = ₱240,000. Legitime (1/2) = ₱120,000 (₱40,000 each); Free portion (1/2) = ₱120,000.
  2. 1/10 Ceiling: 1/10 of ₱120,000 = ₱12,000 (charged to free portion). Excess = ₱40,000 − ₱12,000 = ₱28,000 (imputed as advance on C's legitime).

  4. Final Cash Distribution (₱200,000): A = ₱76,000; B = ₱76,000; C = ₱48,000.

V. Valuation, Risk of Loss, Joint Parental Donations, and Equalization (Articles 1071 to 1074)

Article 1071: Valuation at Time of Donation and Risk of Loss

Article 1071. The same things donated are not to be brought to collation and partition, but only their value at the time of the donation, even though their just value may not then have been assessed.

Their subsequent increase or deterioration and even their total loss or destruction, be it accidental or culpable, shall be for the benefit or account and risk of the donee. (1045a)
  • Valuation at Perfection: Donees do not return the physical object; only its monetary value at the time of the donation controls (Guerrero v. De la Cuesta, 59 Phil. 464) Paras. Market inflation or appreciation before death does not increase the collated figure.
  • Deed Recitals Not Conclusive: Under Tordilla v. Tordilla (60 Phil. 162) Paras, the value recited in the deed is not conclusive (it may be inflated by vanity or understated for taxes, 7 Manresa 643–644). True market value is a question of fact proved by evidence (deed recitals, cadastral surveys, tax assessments, or expert appraisals Jurado).
  • Risk of Loss (Res Perit Domino): Ownership vests in the donee upon perfection. Hence, even if the donated property is totally destroyed by a fortuitous event before the donor dies, the donee must still collate its full value at donation. Conversely, all increases and improvements inure solely to the donee.

Article 1072: Donations Made by Both Parents

Article 1072. In the collation of a donation made by both parents, one-half shall be brought to the inheritance of the father, and the other half, to that of the mother. That given by one alone shall be brought to collation in his or her inheritance. (1046a)
  • Joint Conjugal Donation: Presumed given 1/2 by the father and 1/2 by the mother; each half is collated only when that respective parent's succession opens.
  • Exclusive Property Donation: Given by one parent alone → collated solely in that parent's estate.
  • Key Distinction Paras: Art. 1066 governs donations given to spouses; Art. 1072 governs donations given by spouses.

Articles 1073 & 1074: Equalization in Kind and Impracticability Rules

Article 1073. The donee's share of the estate shall be reduced by an amount equal to that already received by him; and his co-heirs shall receive an equivalent, as much as possible, in property of the same nature, class and quality. (1047)

Article 1074. Should the provisions of the preceding article be impracticable, if the property donated was immovable, the co-heirs shall be entitled to receive its equivalent in cash or securities, at the rate of quotation; and should there be neither cash nor marketable securities in the estate, so much of the other property as may be necessary shall be sold at public auction.

If the property donated was movable, the co-heirs shall only have a right to select an equivalent of other personal property of the inheritance at its just price. (1048)
Property Donated 1st Priority (Art. 1073) 2nd Priority (Art. 1074) 3rd Priority / Limit (Art. 1074)

VI. Fruits, Reimbursement for Improvements, and Settlement of Disputes (Articles 1075 to 1077)

Article 1075: Accrual of Fruits and Interests

Article 1075. The fruits and interest of the property subject to collation shall not pertain to the estate except from the day on which the succession is opened.

For the purpose of ascertaining their amount, the fruits and interest of the property of the estate of the same kind and quality as that subject to collation shall be made the standard of assessment. (1049)
  • Accrual Strictly from Death: Prior to death, fruits belong exclusively to the donee as owner. From the day succession opens (moment of death, Art. 777 & Art. 781), all fruits and interests accrue to the common hereditary estate.
  • Standard of Assessment: Yield of estate property of the same kind and quality.
  • Retention of Possession: The donee is not deprived of physical possession of the property pending partition (Guinguing v. Abuton, 48 Phil. 144) Paras.

Article 1076: Reimbursement for Expenses and Improvements

Article 1076. The co-heirs are bound to reimburse to the donee the necessary expenses which he has incurred for the preservation of the property donated to him, though they may not have augmented its value.

The donee who collates in kind an immovable, which has been given to him, must be reimbursed by his co-heirs for the improvements which have increased the value of the property, and which exist at the time the partition is effected.

As to works made on the estate for the mere pleasure of the donee, no reimbursement is due him for them; he has, however, the right to remove them, if he can do so without injuring the estate. (n)
  • Three Classes of Expenses:
    1. Necessary Expenses (Preservation): Must be reimbursed by co-heirs even if they did not increase market value.
    2. Useful Improvements: Must be reimbursed if they increased value and still exist at partition.
    3. Ornamental / Mere Pleasure: No reimbursement; donee has the right to remove them if no injury is caused to the estate.
  • Justice J.B.L. Reyes's Doctrinal Critique Paras Jurado: Because Art. 1071 abolishes collation in kind (only value is collated), physical return of an immovable occurs strictly as a "returning in kind" when a donation is revoked in its entirety as inofficious under Art. 912 and the donee lacks cash. Hence, Art. 1076 properly belongs in the chapter on legitimes (reduction of inofficious donations), not collation.
Worked Problem 6.1: Appreciation of a Totally Inofficious Donation Paras

Facts: D donated land worth ₱1,000,000 to friend F. At D's death, D leaves a legitimate child (legitime 1/2), surviving spouse (1/4), and acknowledged natural child (1/4) → free portion = ₱0 (donation totally inofficious). At death, the land is worth ₱4,000,000. How much must F return?

Solution: The entire land is NOT returned. What was donated was ₱1,000,000 out of a ₱4,000,000 present value. Therefore, only 1/4 of the present land (or its value) must be returned to the estate. The remaining 3/4 inures to F as owner since perfection. Fruits on that 1/4 accruing since death belong to the estate.

Article 1077: Disputes Do Not Suspend Distribution

Article 1077. Should any question arise among the co-heirs upon the obligation to bring to collation or as to the things which are subject to collation, the distribution of the estate shall not be interrupted for this reason, provided adequate security is given. (1050)
  • Continuity with Adequate Security: Collation controversies do not halt distribution, provided adequate security (such as a court-approved surety bond) is posted to protect the claimants.
  • Controlling Procedural & Case Doctrines Paras:
    1. Proper Forum: Resolved during estate administration proceedings (Guinguing v. Abuton, 48 Phil. 144).
    2. Prescription: Passing of more than 30 years since the donation does NOT bar collation, because prescription does not run while the donor is alive (Ignacio v. Ignacio [CA], 5465-R).
    3. Onerous & Remuneratory Donations: Subject to collation only insofar as they exceed the value of the service rendered or charge imposed (Hernaez v. Hernaez, 1 Phil. 718).
    4. Premature Raising: When estate proceedings have not yet reached liquidation and partition, collation issues are prematurely raised (Vda. de Rodriguez v. CA, L-39532, July 20, 1979).
    5. Res Judicata: Final judgments on collation bind the claimant and all heirs under Rule 90, Sec. 2 of the Rules of Court.

B. Collation Practical Problem-Solving Exercises (ALAC Method)

ALAC Exercise 1: Collation with Fortuitous Loss (Art. 1071) Paras

Facts: Father F donated a vintage sports car worth ₱200,000 to son S in 2020. In 2022, an accidental fire totally destroyed the car without fault or insurance. F died intestate in 2024 leaving ₱800,000 cash, survived by sons S and D. S argues he has nothing to collate. Decide.

  • Answer: S's argument is untenable. S must collate ₱200,000; S receives ₱300,000 and D receives ₱500,000 from the remaining cash.
  • Law: Under Art. 1071, only the value at the time of donation is collated, and subsequent accidental or culpable loss is for the account and risk of the donee.
  • Application: Ownership transferred to S upon perfection (res perit domino). Adding ₱200,000 to ₱800,000 yields a ₱1,000,000 estate base (₱500,000 each). Charged with his ₱200,000 advance, S takes ₱300,000 cash while D takes ₱500,000.
  • Conclusion: Fortuitous destruction does not extinguish collation; S gets ₱300,000 and D gets ₱500,000.
ALAC Exercise 2: Career Expenses & Adan v. Casili Deduction (Art. 1068) Paras

Facts: P spent ₱100,000 for daughter D's pilot training and directed in his will that it be collated. The executor seeks to charge the entire ₱100,000 against D's legitime. Rule.

  • Answer: The executor cannot charge the full ₱100,000; 50% must be deducted under Adan v. Casili, charging only the net balance of ₱50,000.
  • Law: Under Art. 1068, when collation of career expenses is required, the sum the child would have spent living at home must be deducted—presumptively 50% (Adan v. Casili, 76 Phil. 279).
  • Application: Although P expressly ordered collation, the statutory deduction is mandatory to protect the child from being charged for basic support. Deducting 50% (₱50,000) leaves ₱50,000 net.
  • Conclusion: Only ₱50,000 may be collated against D's legitime.
ALAC Exercise 3: Collation by Representation (Art. 1064) Jurado

Facts: T died intestate leaving ₱60,000 cash, survived by son S and grandson G (child of predeceased son P). During life, T donated ₱20,000 to P and ₱10,000 to G. How is the estate distributed?

  • Answer: Son S receives ₱45,000 and grandson G receives ₱15,000.
  • Law: Under Art. 1064, grandchildren inheriting by representation must collate both what their parent would have collated and what they personally received.
  • Application: Estate base = ₱60,000 + ₱20,000 + ₱10,000 = ₱90,000 (÷ 2 branches = ₱45,000 each). G collates ₱20,000 + ₱10,000 = ₱30,000 advances, leaving G ₱15,000 cash (₱45k − ₱30k), while S receives his full ₱45,000 cash.
  • Conclusion: S gets ₱45,000 and G gets ₱15,000.
ALAC Exercise 4: Career Tuition vs. Post-Graduation Law Library (Art. 1068 vs. Art. 1061) Jurado

Facts: P spent ₱300,000 on son L's law school tuition, and after L passed the bar, spent ₱200,000 buying him annotated law books and office furniture. Siblings demand collation of both. Rule.

  • Answer: The ₱300,000 tuition is exempt, but the ₱200,000 for law books and furniture must be collated.
  • Law: Under Art. 1068, career acquisition expenses are exempt unless parents provide otherwise or legitimes are impaired. However, per Manresa and Jurado, post-graduation practice setup costs are ordinary donations under Art. 1061.
  • Application: The ₱300,000 tuition was spent acquiring the profession (exempt absent contrary proof). The ₱200,000 books/furniture was spent after graduation and is an advance on L's legitime.
  • Conclusion: Demand denied as to ₱300,000 tuition; granted as to ₱200,000 law library.

D. Collation Active Recall & Recitation Questions (Tap to Reveal Model Answers)

1. What are the three legal meanings of "collation" (Jurado/Rabuya), which meaning governs Arts. 1061–1077, and how does Paras frame "not collationable"?
The three meanings are (1) Mathematical Computation (Art. 908), (2) Imputation/Charging (Arts. 1061–1077 — the governing sense here), and (3) Actual Restitution/Reduction (Arts. 911–912). Paras frames "not collationable" in two nuances: either added to the estate but charged to the free portion (Art. 1062/1063), or not even added to the estate at all (Art. 1067).
2. If a deed of donation specifies that the gift is "irrevocable," does this dispense with collation under Art. 1061?
No. Under Buhay De Roma v. CA (L-46903), "irrevocable" merely reflects the nature of an inter vivos donation and does not constitute an express prohibition against collation.
3. When a donor expressly dispenses with collation or a donee repudiates under Art. 1062, does the gift escape Art. 908 computation? Where is it charged?
No. It is still mathematically added to the net estate under Art. 908 to verify legitimes, but it is imputed against the disposable free portion (and reduced if inofficious).
4. Under Art. 1064, why must a grandchild collate a house given to his predeceased parent even if the parent sold it to a stranger before dying?
Because a representative steps into the exact place, rights, and obligations of the person represented. Excusing collation would allow the representative branch to receive more than the parent could have claimed, destroying equality among branches.
5. Under what circumstances are career/college expenses collated under Art. 1068, and what deduction applies under Adan v. Casili?
They are collated only if (1) the parents so provide, or (2) they impair the legitime. When collated, the sum the child would have spent living at home is deducted—presumptively 50% under Adan v. Casili (76 Phil. 279).
6. Why does a son whose debt was paid by his father as guarantor remain liable even if he repudiates the inheritance, unlike a son whose debt was paid out of parental pity (Art. 1069)?
When the father pays as guarantor (or lends expecting repayment), a debtor-creditor relationship exists, which survives repudiation. When paid out of pure liberality/pity, it is a collatable donation; upon repudiation under Art. 1062, the son is treated as a stranger and owes nothing unless the gift is inofficious.
7. What is the exact formula for collating wedding gifts under Art. 1070?
Compute the total estate base (net estate + donations), determine the disposable free portion, take 1/10 of that free portion (exempt ceiling per child), and collate only the excess over 1/10 as an advance against the child's legitime.
8. If land donated at ₱500,000 in 2010 is worth ₱5,000,000 at death in 2024—or if a donated ₱500,000 car is totally destroyed in a typhoon—what amount is collated under Art. 1071?
₱500,000 in both cases. Under Art. 1071, only the value at the time of donation is collated; subsequent appreciation and fortuitous loss alike fall on the donee (res perit domino).
9. Why did Justice J.B.L. Reyes criticize the text and placement of Art. 1076 regarding reimbursement for improvements?
Because Art. 1076 speaks of a donee who "collates in kind an immovable," whereas Art. 1071 abolished collation in kind in Philippine law. Physical return occurs only when a donation is revoked as completely inofficious under Art. 912, so Art. 1076 properly belongs in the chapter on legitimes (reduction of inofficious donations).

I. General Concepts, Classifications, and Mechanics of Partition (Articles 1078 to 1082)

Article 1078: Community Property / Co-Ownership Prior to Partition

Article 1078. Where there are two or more heirs, the whole estate of the decedent is, before its partition, owned in common by such heirs, subject to the payment of debts of the deceased. (n)
A. Big Picture & Intuitive Foundation

While successional rights are transmitted and vest at the precise moment of death (Art. 777), physical delivery and individual ownership of specific items cannot take place immediately. Outstanding debts, taxes, and administration expenses must first be settled. Article 1078 defines the legal condition of the estate during this interim interval: a community of co-ownership among all co-heirs over the collective hereditary mass.

Simple Illustration: If a parent dies leaving three children and two parcels of land, Child A cannot immediately fence off Lot 1, nor can Child B claim exclusive title to Lot 2. Each child owns an undivided one-third (1/3) ideal interest across both lots and every asset of the estate until debts are paid and partition is executed.

B. Operative Doctrines & Jurisprudence Under Article 1078
  • Immediate & Retroactive Vesting (Ibarle v. Po; Hacbang v. Alo; Mendoza v. CA) Paras Selected:
    1. Co-heirs become co-owners from the exact moment of death without need of a prior judicial declaration of heirship. Partition merely converts ideal shares into concrete physical portions, and its effects retroact to the date of death.
    2. In Ibarle v. Po, a widow's unauthorized sale of her minor children's inherited half to X was held null and void because title had already vested in the children upon the father's death.
    3. In Mendoza v. CA, co-ownership is extinguished only when the portions are concretely determined and technically described.
  • Subordination to Debts & Administration (Lat v. CA) Paras Jurado: The co-ownership is subordinate to the payment of decedent's debts. An administrator is an officer of the court (not a mere alter ego of the heirs); distribution awaits payment of debts, expenses, and taxes, and prior to liquidation, heirs generally cannot interfere with administration.
  • Sole Heir vs. Plural Heirs (Rule 74, Sec. 1; Sanchez v. CA) Paras Jurado:
    1. Sole Heir: Adjudicates the entire estate via an Affidavit of Self-Adjudication filed with the Register of Deeds.
    2. Two or More Heirs (Extrajudicial Settlement): Valid when (a) decedent died intestate, (b) left no debts or all debts are paid, (c) all heirs are of legal age or minors are represented by judicial/legal guardians, and (d) executed in a public instrument or affidavit filed with the Register of Deeds.
  • Sale of Undivided Share Before Liquidation (Mondonido v. De Roda; Saturnino v. Paulino) Paras: An heir may validly sell his undivided hereditary share even pending liquidation because the decedent is already dead (not a prohibited sale of future inheritance under Art. 1347). A co-heir need not wait for termination of administration proceedings to assert rights or redeem.
  • No Prescription While Recognizing Co-Ownership (Sebial v. Sebial) Paras: Consistent with Art. 494, a co-heir cannot acquire co-heirs' shares by prescription so long as he expressly or impliedly recognizes the co-heirship.

Article 1079: Definition, Classifications, and Procedural Modes of Partition

Article 1079. Partition, in general, is the separation, division and assignment of a thing held in common among those to whom it may belong. The thing itself may be divided, or its value. (n)
  • Core Purpose (Villamor v. CA; Confesor v. Pelayo) Selected: Severs individual interests in common property, vesting each co-owner with a sole estate in specific property (or dividing its monetary proceeds).
  • Manresa's Classifications of Partition Jurado Paras:
    CriterionClassifications & Meaning
    As to ExtentTotal (all estate properties divided) vs. Partial (some divided, rest left in co-ownership).
    As to Duration / FinalityProvisional / Temporary (conditional, e.g. under Art. 1084) vs. Definite / Permanent (absolute and final).
    As to Form / MethodExtrajudicial (by decedent under Art. 1080, mandatary under Art. 1081, or heirs under Rule 74, Sec. 1) vs. Judicial (court-supervised under Rule 69 or Rule 90).
  • Four Procedural Methods & Actors Jurado:
    1. Procedural Methods (Rules of Court): (a) Extrajudicial settlement (Rule 74, Sec. 1); (b) Ordinary action for partition (Rule 74, Sec. 1; Rule 69); (c) Judicial summary settlement of estates of small value (Rule 74, Sec. 2); and (d) Administration proceedings (Rule 90 / Rules 79–91).
    2. Who May Effect Partition: (a) The decedent himself (Art. 1080); (b) A third-person mandatary (Art. 1081); (c) The heirs themselves (Arts. 1083–1084); or (d) A competent probate court.
  • Judicial Partition Rules Paras:
    1. Probate Prerequisite (Ralla v. Untalan): No valid partition under a will before probate, if covering properties embraced in the will.
    2. Project of Partition & Advance Distribution (Recto Dia v. Castillo; Gatmaitan v. Medina): Distribution awaits court approval of the project of partition, though advance/partial distribution may be permitted if heirs post a bond or deposit to safeguard creditors (Rule 90, Sec. 1).
    3. Non-Parties Not Bound (Evangelista v. Bonilla): A judicial partition does not bind excluded heirs, who may file an accion reivindicatoria within the prescriptive period.
  • Extrajudicial Partition Rules Paras Jurado:
    1. Oral / Parol Partition Valid (Belen v. Belen; Hernandez v. Andal): Valid and enforceable if freely entered into; partition is a confirmation of ownership (not a conveyance), and the Statute of Frauds does not apply to completed or partially executed acts.
    2. Effect of Non-Registration (Ladisla v. Pestano; Heirs of Joaquin Teves v. CA): Binds the heirs intrinsically even if unregistered when no creditors are prejudiced, but registration is needed to bind third parties.
    3. Excluded Properties Void Ab Initio (Bautista v. CA, G.R. No. 79958, Oct. 28, 1988): Extrajudicial partition dividing property belonging exclusively to the surviving husband as part of the deceased wife's estate is void ab initio; only estate property transmitted by succession can be partitioned.
    4. Future Inheritance Bar (Art. 1347, par. 2): Heirs cannot partition property during the lifetime of its owner (except by the owner himself under Art. 1080).

Article 1080: Partition by Testator or Decedent & Preserving Family Enterprises

Article 1080. Should a person make partition of his estate by an act inter vivos, or by will, such partition shall be respected, insofar as it does not prejudice the legitime of the compulsory heirs.

A parent who, in the interest of his or her family, desires to keep any agricultural, industrial, or manufacturing enterprise intact, may avail himself of the right granted him in this article, by ordering that the legitime of the other children to whom the property is not assigned, be paid in cash. (1056a)
  • Abrogation of Legasto v. Verzosa (54 Phil. 776) Jurado Paras Selected:
    1. Old Civil Code (Art. 1056): Used the word "testator", leading Legasto v. Verzosa, Alsua-Betts v. CA (92 SCRA 332), and Dimayuga v. CA (129 SCRA 110) to require a prior valid will for an inter vivos partition to stand.
    2. New Civil Code (Art. 1080): Deliberately replaced "testator" with "person". Under controlling modern jurisprudence (Romero v. Villamor; Dizon-Rivera v. Dizon; Chavez v. IAC; Zaragoza v. CA; J.L.T. Agro, Inc. v. Balansag), a person may make a valid partition inter vivos without need of a supporting will.
  • Nature, Formalities & Justice Caguioa's Doctrinal Limits Paras Selected:
    1. Statutory Exception to Art. 1347: An inter vivos partition by the owner is an express statutory exception to the ban on contracts over future inheritance. It is an instrument sui generis, revocable during life, taking effect only upon death (requiring neither will nor donation formalities; valid in a public instrument, private writing, or even orally).
    2. No Institution of Strangers (Justice Caguioa's Synthesis): Partition determines physical allocation, not disposition. A person cannot institute a stranger via an inter vivos partition without a will. Without a will, it is valid only if confined to legal/compulsory heirs following intestacy rules without impairing legitimes.
  • Preserving Family Enterprises (Art. 1080, par. 2):
    1. Exception to Arts. 872 & 904: Although legitimes cannot generally be burdened, a parent wishing to keep an agricultural, industrial, or manufacturing enterprise (or by extension, a professional office/library) intact may assign the entire enterprise to one child and order the other children's legitimes to be paid in cash.
    2. Mandatory Assignment Requirement (Bahanay, Jr. v. Martinez, 64 SCRA 452) Paras: Ordering cash payment of legitimes without assigning the ownership or management of the enterprise to one or more heirs is void for violating Art. 1080.

Articles 1081 & 1082: Third-Person Mandatary & Constructive Partitions

Article 1081. A person may, by an act inter vivos or mortis causa, intrust the mere power to make the partition after his death to any person who is not one of the co-heirs.

The provisions of this and of the preceding article shall be observed even should there be among the co-heirs a minor or a person subject to guardianship; but the mandatary, in such case, shall make an inventory of the property of the estate, after notifying the co-heirs, the creditors, and the legatees or devisees. (1057a)

Article 1082. Every act which is intended to put an end to indivision among co-heirs and legatees or devisees is deemed to be a partition, although it should purport to be a sale, and exchange, a compromise, or any other transaction. (n)
  • Mandatary to Partition (Art. 1081):
    1. Strict Disqualification: The mandatary must NOT be one of the co-heirs (Del Rosario v. Del Rosario), ensuring impartiality.
    2. Distinction from Will-Making (Art. 784): Only the physical apportionment ("mere power to make the partition") may be delegated; the mandatary cannot alter testamentary shares or dispose of property.
    3. Mandatory Inventory for Minors/Wards: When a minor or person under guardianship is a co-heir, the mandatary must prepare an inventory after notifying co-heirs, creditors, and legatees/devisees.
  • Constructive Partitions (Art. 1082): Substance controls over nomenclature. Any agreement intended to terminate co-ownership—whether labeled a sale, exchange, waiver, or compromise—is legally deemed a partition (Gabila v. Perez: sale by all heirs ends co-ownership; Favor v. CA: "Compromise Agreement" operated as a valid deed of partition).

Article 1083: Right to Demand Partition & Lawful Prohibitions

Article 1083. Every co-heir has a right to demand the division of the estate unless the testator should have expressly forbidden its partition, in which case the period of indivision shall not exceed twenty years as provided in article 494. This power of the testator to prohibit division applies to the legitime.

Even though forbidden by the testator, the co-ownership terminates when any of the causes for which partnership is dissolved takes place, or when the court finds for compelling reasons that division should be ordered, upon petition of one of the co-heirs. (1051a)
  • Who May Demand Partition Jurado: (1) Any compulsory heir; (2) any voluntary heir; (3) any legatee or devisee; and (4) any person who has acquired an interest in the estate. Articles 1084 to 1087: Conditional Heirs, Equality, Indivisibility & Accounting

Article 1084. Voluntary heirs upon whom some condition has been imposed cannot demand a partition until the condition has been fulfilled; but the other co-heirs may demand it by giving sufficient security for the rights which the former may have in case the condition should be complied with, and until it is known that the condition has not been fulfilled or can never be complied with, the partition shall be understood to be provisional. (1054a)

Article 1085. In the partition of the estate, equality shall be observed as far as possible, dividing the property into lots, or assigning to each of the co-heirs things of the same nature, quality and kind. (1061)

Article 1086. Should a thing be indivisible, or would be much impaired by its being divided, it may be adjudicated to one of the heirs, provided he shall pay the others the excess in cash.

Nevertheless, if any of the heirs should demand that the thing be sold at public auction and that strangers be allowed to bid, this must be done. (1062)

Article 1087. In the partition the co-heirs shall reimburse one another for the income and fruits which each one of them may have received from any property of the estate, for any useful and necessary expenses made upon such property, and for any damage thereto through malice or neglect. (1063)
  • Conditional Heirs (Art. 1084): Voluntary heirs under a suspensive condition cannot demand partition until fulfillment. Unconditional co-heirs may demand partition by posting sufficient security; pending resolution, the partition is provisional.
  • Equality in Kind (Art. 1085): Assign things of the same nature, quality, and kind. Forcing heirs into a drawing of lots based on arbitrary valuation without a hearing violates due process (Cabaluna, Jr. v. Cordova Paras).
  • Indivisible Property & Mandatory Public Auction (Art. 1086): May be adjudicated to one heir who pays cash difference; however, if even one co-heir demands a public auction with strangers bidding, the auction MUST be held.
  • Mutual Accounting (Art. 1087): Co-heirs reimburse each other for (1) income and fruits received, (2) useful and necessary expenses incurred, and (3) damages caused by malice or neglect.

Article 1088: Redemption of Hereditary Rights & The Written Notice Rule

Article 1088. Should any of the heirs sell his hereditary rights to a stranger before the partition, any or all of the co-heirs may be subrogated to the rights of the purchaser by reimbursing him for the price of the sale, provided they do so within the period of one month from the time they were notified in writing of the sale by the vendor. ARTICLE 1088 LEGAL REDEMPTION WORKFLOW
Five Cumulative Triggers (Jurado Paras Selected)
↓
1. Plurality of Heirs + Sale of Undivided Hereditary Rights
Heir sells ideal/undivided hereditary interest (not a post-partition determinate parcel).
↓
2. Sale Is to a "Stranger" & Executed BEFORE Partition
Stranger = anyone not a co-heir in that succession (De Jesus v. Manglapus). After partition, Art. 1620 governs (Castro v. Castro).
↓
3. Exercised Within 1 Month (30 Days) from WRITTEN NOTICE BY THE VENDOR + Price Reimbursement
Notice by buyer or mere registration does NOT start the 30-day clock (Butte; Garcia v. Calaliman; Francisco v. Boiser).
  • Who Is a "Stranger" (De Jesus v. Manglapus) Paras: Anyone who is not a co-heir in that specific succession—including creditors, legatees, devisees, and incapacitated/non-succeeding heirs. Sales between co-heirs trigger no redemption.
  • Before Partition & Court-Ordered Sales (Castro v. Castro; Plan v. IAC) Paras:
    1. If the sale occurs after partition while property remains held in common, Art. 1088 does not apply; ordinary co-ownership redemption under Art. 1620 governs.
    2. Art. 1088 does not apply to sales ordered by a probate court to pay estate debts (Plan v. IAC).
  • Mandatory Written Notice Served BY THE VENDOR (Butte v. Manuel Uy & Sons, Inc., 4 SCRA 526; Garcia v. Calaliman; Francisco v. Boiser, 332 SCRA 792):
    1. Actual knowledge, verbal notice, notice served by the buyer, or Registry of Deeds registration does NOT start the 30-day period. Written notice must come from the selling co-heir (vendor) so there is no doubt that a definitive alienation occurred.
    2. Form (Conejero v. CA): No rigid wording is required—furnishing a copy of the deed of sale by the vendor suffices.
    3. Narrow Equity Exception (Alonzo v. IAC, 150 SCRA 259): Where co-heirs lived inside the same inherited compound alongside the buyer for over a decade, equity treated indisputable long-standing actual knowledge as notice (strictly exceptional; Francisco v. Boiser reaffirmed Butte).
  • Reimbursement Price on Resale (Hernaez v. Hernaez) Paras: If Heir A sells to Stranger X for ₱100,000 and X resells to Y for ₱200,000, Co-Heir B redeems from Y by paying only the original ₱100,000 price.

IV. Delivery of Title Deeds (Articles 1089 & 1090)

Article 1089. The titles of acquisition or ownership of each property shall be delivered to the co-heir to whom said property has been adjudicated. (1065a)

Article 1090. When the title comprises two or more pieces of land which have been assigned to two or more co-heirs, or when it covers one piece of land which has been divided between two or more co-heirs, the title shall be delivered to the one having the largest interest, and authentic copies of the title shall be furnished to the other co-heirs at the expense of the estate. If the interest of each co-heir should be the same, the oldest shall have the title. (1066a)
  • Individual Holdings (Art. 1089): Delivered to the co-heir to whom that specific property was adjudicated.
  • Shared Titles (Art. 1090): (1) Delivered to the co-heir with the largest interest; (2) if equal, to the oldest co-heir; and (3) authentic copies furnished to the others at the expense of the estate.

V. Effects of Partition and Reciprocal Warranties (Articles 1091 to 1096)

Article 1091. A partition legally made confers upon each heir the exclusive ownership of the property adjudicated to him. (1068)

Article 1092. After the partition has been made, the co-heirs shall be reciprocally bound to warrant the title to, and the quality of, each property adjudicated. (1069a)

Article 1093. The reciprocal obligation of warranty referred to in the preceding article shall be proportionate to the respective hereditary shares of the co-heirs, but if any one of them should be insolvent, the other co-heirs shall be liable for his part in the same proportion, deducting the part corresponding to the one who should be indemnified.

Those who pay for the insolvent heir shall have a right of action against him for reimbursement, should his financial condition improve. (1071)

Article 1094. An action to enforce the warranty among heirs must be brought within ten years from the date the right of action accrues. (n)

Article 1095. If a credit should be assigned as collectible, the co-heirs shall not be liable for the subsequent insolvency of the debtor of the estate, but only for his insolvency at the time the partition is made.

The warranty of the solvency of the debtor can only be enforced during the five years following the partition.

Co-heirs do not warrant bad debts, if so known to, and accepted by, the distributee. But if such debts are not assigned to a co-heir, and should be collected, in whole or in part, the amount collected shall be distributed proportionately among the heirs. (1072a)

Article 1096. The obligation of warranty among co-heirs shall cease in the following cases:
(1) When the testator himself has made the partition, unless it appears, or it may be reasonably presumed, that his intention was otherwise, but the legitime shall always remain unimpaired;
(2) When it has been so expressly stipulated in the agreement of partition, unless there has been bad faith;
(3) When the eviction is due to a cause subsequent to the partition, or has been caused by the fault of the distributee of the property. (1070a)
Warranty Aspect Operative Rule & Article Prescription / Key Exceptions
Exclusive Ownership Art. 1091 — Ends co-ownership; confers exclusive title over adjudicated property. Once carried out, the estate is finally settled.
Title & Quality Arts. 1092–1094 — Reciprocal warranty against eviction and hidden defects, borne proportionately (insolvent co-heir's part shared by others, with reimbursement right if solvency improves). 10 years from accrual of the right of action (Art. 1094).
Assigned Credits Art. 1095 — Warrants debtor's solvency strictly at the time of partition (NOT subsequent insolvency). 5 years from partition. Known & accepted bad debts carry no warranty; unassigned bad debts collected later are split proportionately.
Cessation of Warranty Art. 1096 — Ceases if: (1) testator made partition (unless contrary intent / legitime impaired); (2) express waiver without bad faith; (3) post-partition cause or distributee's fault (e.g., letting third party acquire by prescription Paras). Legitime remains inviolable under all exceptions.

VI. Rescission and Nullity of Partition (Articles 1097 to 1105)

Article 1097: Contract Grounds — Nullity vs. Rescission

Article 1097. A partition may be rescinded or annulled for the same causes as contracts. (1073a)
  • Nullity vs. Rescission (Justices J.B.L. Reyes & Ricardo C. Puno) Selected Paras:
    1. Nullity / Annulment: Arises from lack of essential requisites or vices of consent (fraud, intimidation, mistake). A deed signed on behalf of an absent co-heir without a written special power of attorney is not binding absent ratification (Agolto v. CA). Fraud annulment prescribes in 4 years from discovery, reckoned from Register of Deeds registration as constructive notice (Gerona v. De Guzman).
    2. Rescission: A subsidiary remedy presupposing a valid partition causing economic prejudice (lesion), often curable by cash indemnity.

Articles 1098 to 1102: Rescission on Account of Lesion (1/4 Rule)

Article 1098. A partition, judicial or extra-judicial, may also be rescinded on account of lesion, when any one of the co-heirs received things whose value is less, by at least one-fourth, than the share to which he is entitled, considering the value of the things at the time they were adjudicated. (1074a)

Article 1099. The partition made by the testator cannot be impugned on the ground of lesion, except when the legitime of the compulsory heirs is thereby prejudiced, or when it appears or may reasonably be presumed, that the intention of the testator was otherwise. (1075)

Article 1100. The action for rescission on account of lesion shall prescribe after four years from the time the partition was made. (1076)

Article 1101. The heir who is sued shall have the option of indemnifying the plaintiff for the loss, or consenting to a new partition.

Indemnity may be made by payment in cash or by the delivery of a thing of the same kind and quality as that awarded to the plaintiff.

If a new partition is made, it shall affect neither those who have not been prejudiced nor those have not received more than their just share. (1077a)

Article 1102. An heir who has alienated the whole or a considerable part of the real property adjudicated to him cannot maintain an action for rescission on the ground of lesion, but he shall have a right to be indemnified in cash. (1078a)
  • The One-Fourth (1/4) Lesion Threshold (Art. 1098): Market value at adjudication controls. Rescission requires damage of at least 1/4 (25%); if less than 1/4, the remedy is damages Paras.
  • Testator's Partition Exempt (Art. 1099): Cannot be impugned for lesion unless (1) legitimes are impaired, or (2) testator intended otherwise.
  • Prescription (Art. 1100): Prescribes in 4 years from partition (or court approval in judicial partition).
  • Defendant's Option (Art. 1101): Sued heir chooses to indemnify (cash or same kind/quality) or consent to a new partition (affecting only prejudiced or over-allotted heirs).
  • Bar by Alienation (Art. 1102): An heir who alienated all or a considerable part of his realty cannot sue for rescission (mutual restitution impossible); restricted to cash indemnity.

Articles 1103 to 1105: The Omission, Preterition & Non-Heir Triad

Article 1103. The omission of one or more objects or securities of the inheritance shall not cause the rescission of the partition on the ground of lesion, but the partition shall be completed by the distribution of the objects or securities which have been omitted. (1079a)

Article 1104. A partition made with preterition of any of the compulsory heirs shall not be rescinded, unless it be proved that there was bad faith or fraud on the part of the other persons interested; but the latter shall be proportionately obliged to pay to the person omitted the share which belongs to him. (1080)

Article 1105. A partition which includes a person believed to be an heir, but who is not, shall be void only with respect to such person. (1081a)

B. Partition Practical Problem-Solving Exercises (ALAC Method)

ALAC Exercise 1: Preterition in a Will vs. Preterition in a Partition (Art. 854 vs. Art. 1104) Jurado Paras

Facts: Testator T executed a will instituting sons A and B, completely omitting his legitimate daughter C. Later, A and B extrajudicially partitioned the estate without C. No fraud or bad faith was proved. C sued to annul the partition. Decide.

  • Answer: The institution of heirs in the will is annulled under Art. 854, but the physical partition itself is not rescinded; A and B must pay C her one-third share under Art. 1104.
  • Law: Under Art. 854, preterition of a compulsory heir in the direct line annuls the institution of heirs. Under Art. 1104, a partition made with preterition of a compulsory heir shall not be rescinded unless bad faith or fraud is proved; the other heirs are proportionately obliged to pay the omitted heir the share belonging to him.
  • Application: Omitting C from the will opens the estate to total intestacy. However, as to the physical partition, Art. 1104 controls to preserve stability. Since fraud was not proved, the partition stands, and A and B must each contribute proportionally from their shares to satisfy C's 1/3 intestate share.
  • Conclusion: The partition is not rescinded; A and B must proportionately pay C her share.
ALAC Exercise 2: Preterition of OFW Sister in Extrajudicial Partition (Art. 1104 Computation) Selected

Facts: Three legitimate brothers, A, B, and C, executed an extrajudicial partition dividing an estate worth ₱900,000 equally among themselves (₱300,000 each). They omitted their legitimate sister, D, who was working overseas, mistakenly believing without fraud or bad faith that D had already received her share via an earlier donation. D files an action to annul the entire partition. Rule.

  • Answer: The action to annul the entire partition will not prosper. D is entitled to receive her ₱225,000 share, paid proportionally by A, B, and C.
  • Law: Under Art. 1104, a partition made with preterition of a compulsory heir is not rescinded absent bad faith or fraud; the other heirs are proportionally obliged to pay the omitted heir her share.
  • Application: In intestacy among four children, the ₱900,000 estate gives each child ₱225,000. Absent bad faith or fraud, the partition stands, and A, B, and C must each contribute ₱75,000 from their ₱300,000 allotment to pay D her ₱225,000 share.
  • Conclusion: The partition is not rescinded; A, B, and C must proportionally pay D ₱225,000 (₱75,000 each).
ALAC Exercise 3: Written Notice Served by Buyer vs. Vendor in Legal Redemption (Art. 1088) Jurado Paras Selected

Facts: Prior to partition, heir X sold his undivided hereditary share to stranger S. Buyer S personally served a notarized copy of the deed of sale upon co-heirs Y and Z. Forty days later, Y filed an action for legal redemption under Art. 1088, tendering the purchase price. S opposed on the ground that the 30-day period had expired. Decide.

  • Answer: Y's action for legal redemption is timely and valid.
  • Law: Under Art. 1088, co-heirs may redeem within one month from written notification of the sale by the vendor. Under Butte v. Manuel Uy & Sons, Inc., Garcia v. Calaliman, and Francisco v. Boiser, written notice must come from the selling co-heir (vendor), and notice from the buyer or actual knowledge does not start the 30-day period.
  • Application: Notice was served exclusively by S (the purchaser), not X (the vendor). Because the vendor never served written notice, the statutory 30-day period never commenced to run against Y.
  • Conclusion: Y is legally entitled to redeem the hereditary share.

D. Partition Active Recall & Recitation Questions (Tap to Reveal Model Answers)

1. Why is an oral/parol partition among heirs valid, and why does the Statute of Frauds not apply to it (Hernandez v. Andal)?
Because partition is not a conveyance transferring title from one person to another, but a mere confirmation and physical determination of pre-existing ownership (Art. 777); moreover, the Statute of Frauds does not apply to completed or partially executed agreements.
2. How did the replacement of "testator" with "person" in Art. 1080 abrogate Legasto v. Verzosa, and what are Justice Caguioa's limits on non-will inter vivos partitions?
Under the old Code's "testator," Legasto v. Verzosa required a prior valid will. Changing it to "person" allows a valid partition inter vivos without a supporting will (Chavez v. IAC; J.L.T. Agro v. Balansag). Per Justice Caguioa, however, without a will it cannot institute a stranger and must be confined to legal/compulsory heirs following intestacy rules without impairing legitimes.
3. Under Art. 1083, what is the maximum period a testator may prohibit partition, does it bind the legitime, and what other laws impose indivision?
Up to 20 years, and it validly binds even the legitime (terminating earlier only upon partnership dissolution causes under Arts. 1830–1831 or compelling court reasons). Heirs may also agree up to 10 years (Art. 494), and a family home continues for 10 years or while a minor beneficiary resides (Art. 159 FC).
4. If an indivisible estate property is assigned to one heir who offers to pay the cash difference, can another co-heir compel a public auction with outside bidders?
Yes. Under Art. 1086, par. 2, if any of the heirs demands that the indivisible thing be sold at public auction and that strangers be allowed to bid, the public auction must be done.
5. What is the decisive difference between legal redemption under Art. 1088 and Art. 1620, and why does buyer notice or actual knowledge fail to start the 30-day clock?
Art. 1088 governs sales of hereditary rights before partition (co-heirs); Art. 1620 governs sales of co-owned property after partition (Castro v. Castro). Under Butte, Garcia v. Calaliman, and Francisco v. Boiser, only written notice served by the vendor eliminates doubt over a definitive sale and starts the 1-month (30-day) clock (save the rare decade-long compound exception in Alonzo v. IAC).
6. What is the decisive difference between preterition in a will (Art. 854) and preterition in a partition (Art. 1104)?
Preterition in a will (Art. 854) annuls the institution of heirs. Preterition in a partition (Art. 1104) does NOT rescind the partition unless bad faith or fraud is proved; the included heirs are simply obliged to pay the omitted heir his rightful share proportionally.
7. What is the legal effect if an extrajudicial partition (a) omits two parcels of land (Art. 1103), or (b) mistakenly includes an intruder who is not an heir (Art. 1105)?
(a) Omission of assets does not rescind the partition; it is simply completed by distributing the omitted parcels (Art. 1103). (b) Inclusion of a non-heir makes the partition void only with respect to the intruder (unless a sole heir partitioned with intruders, which voids it totally per Manresa) (Art. 1105).
