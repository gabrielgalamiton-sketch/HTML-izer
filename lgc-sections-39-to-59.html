CHAPTER 1: QUALIFICATIONS AND ELECTION (SECTIONS 39–43)

SECTION 39. QUALIFICATIONS

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 39, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 39. Qualifications. — (a) An elective local official must be a citizen of the Philippines; a registered voter in the barangay, municipality, city, or province or, in the case of a member of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, Sangguniang Panlungsod, or Sangguniang Bayan, the district where he intends to be elected; a resident therein for at least one (1) year immediately preceding the day of the election; and able to read and write Filipino or any other local language or dialect. (b) Candidates for the position of governor, vice governor, or member of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, or mayor, vice mayor, or member of the Sangguniang Panlungsod of highly urbanized cities must be at least twenty-three (23) years of age on election day. (c) Candidates for the position of mayor or vice mayor of independent component cities, component cities, or municipalities must be at least twenty-one (21) years of age on election day. (d) Candidates for the position of member of the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan must be at least eighteen (18) years of age on election day. (e) Candidates for the position of punong barangay or member of the Sangguniang Barangay must be at least eighteen (18) years of age on election day. (f) Candidates for the Sangguniang Kabataan must be at least fifteen (15) years of age but less than eighteen (18) years of age on election day. (NOTE: Expressly superseded/amended by R.A. No. 10742 and R.A. No. 11768 — see Statutory Update below).

II. Core Doctrines, Elements & Requisites

  • Nature of Section 39 (Positive Qualifications):
    • The qualifications set forth in Section 39 constitute the positive qualifications (roughly half of the requirements for election to local public offices).
    • The other half is contained in Section 40, which lays down the negative qualifications (circumstances that disqualify local candidates).
    • Continuing Requirements: Qualifications prescribed by law are continuing requirements and must be possessed not only on the date of election/assumption of office, but for the entire duration of the official's incumbency.
  • General Common Qualifications of All Elective Local Officials:
    1. Citizenship: Must be a citizen of the Philippines (either natural-born OR naturalized);
      • Handwritten Marginal Note Distinction: Unlike members of Congress (Senators and District/Party-List Representatives), who are constitutionally required under Art. VI, Secs. 3 & 6 of the 1987 Constitution to be natural-born citizens of the Philippines, local elective candidates need not be citizens by birth. Naturalized Filipinos may validly qualify for local elective positions.
    2. Voter Registration: Must be a registered voter in the:
      • Barangay, municipality, city, or province where he or she intends to be elected (for Governor, Vice Governor, City/Municipal Mayor, City/Municipal Vice Mayor, Punong Barangay, and Sangguniang Barangay members); OR
      • Legislative/Sanggunian District where he or she intends to be elected (for regular members of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, Sangguniang Panlungsod, or Sangguniang Bayan); OR
      • Katipunan ng Kabataan (KK) of the barangay (for Sangguniang Kabataan candidates under Sec. 10, R.A. No. 10742).
    3. Residency (Domicile): Must be a resident therein for at least one (1) year immediately preceding the day of the election:
      • In the province, city, municipality, or barangay where seeking election (for local chief executives, vice-executives, and barangay officials); OR
      • In the specific Sanggunian district where seeking election (for Sangguniang Panlalawigan, Sangguniang Panlungsod, and Sangguniang Bayan members).
    4. Literacy: Must be able to read and write Filipino or any other local language or dialect:
      • Legislative History Note: This literacy requirement was almost deleted during the Senate deliberations upon the suggestion of Senator Teofisto Guingona, who argued that a literacy requirement was elitist, but Congress ultimately retained it in Section 39(a).
    5. Age Requirement: Must possess the minimum statutory age on election day (not on the day of filing the Certificate of Candidacy, and not on the day of assumption of office).
    6. Absence of Property Qualification:
      • There is no property qualification to run for local elective office.

III. Age Requirements & Statutory Updates

  • Reckoning Point of Age ("On Election Day"):
    • The statutory phrase "on election day" was deliberately inserted in Section 39 to preclude any ambiguity as to when the age qualification must be satisfied.
    • Contrast with the Classic Benigno "Ninoy" Aquino Jr. Underage Case:
      • Under the 1935 Constitution (and the 1987 Constitution), the age requirement for a Senator is 35 years. The late Senator Benigno Aquino Jr. was less than 35 years old when he filed his Certificate of Candidacy and on election day, but had turned 35 by the day the winning senators assumed office. He was allowed to sit because the Constitutional phrase "on the day of the election" had not yet been strictly applied to bar assumption if the candidate reached the qualifying age upon assuming office (note that under the 1987 Constitution, Art. VI, Secs. 3 & 6, the phrase "on the day of the election" is now explicitly mandated for Congress as well).
      • Rule for Local Candidates: Under Section 39 of the LGC, a candidate who is still underage on election day is disqualified, even if he or she will reach the required age before the June 30 assumption of office.
  • STATUTORY UPDATE: Sangguniang Kabataan (SK) Qualifications under R.A. No. 10742 and R.A. No. 11768:
    • Outdated Provision in the Text: Page 1 of the material cites Section 39(f) as requiring SK candidates to be "at least fifteen (15) years of age but less than eighteen (18) years of age on election day" (and Page 3 notes "15 years of age, at least, but not more than 21 years old" under earlier amendments such as R.A. No. 9164).
    • Current Controlling Law (Sec. 10, R.A. No. 10742, as amended by R.A. No. 11768):
      • Katipunan ng Kabataan (KK) Membership Age: 15 to 30 years of age (Sec. 4, R.A. No. 10742).
      • Elective SK Official (SK Chairperson & SK Kagawad) Age: Must be at least eighteen (18) years but not more than twenty-four (24) years of age on the day of the elections (so that SK officials have reached the age of majority and possess full legal capacity to enter into contracts and be held accountable for public funds).
      • Appointive SK Official (SK Secretary & SK Treasurer) Age: Under R.A. No. 11768 (amending Sec. 10 of R.A. No. 10742), an appointed SK Secretary or SK Treasurer must be at least eighteen (18) years but not more than thirty (30) years of age on the day of appointment.
      • Additional SK Qualifications (Sec. 10, R.A. No. 10742):
        • Must be a citizen of the Philippines;
        • Must be a qualified and registered member of the Katipunan ng Kabataan (KK);
        • Must be a resident of the barangay for not less than one (1) year immediately preceding the day of the elections;
        • Must be able to read and write Filipino, English, or the local dialect;
        • Anti-Political Dynasty Qualification: Must not be related within the second (2nd) civil degree of consanguinity or affinity to any incumbent elected national official or to any incumbent elected regional, provincial, city, municipal, or barangay official, in the locality where he or she seeks to be elected; and
        • Must not have been convicted by final judgment of any crime involving moral turpitude.

IV. Jurisprudential Doctrines on Residency, Domicile & Property Qualifications

  • 1. Residence is Synonymous with Domicile (Animus Revertendi):
    • Co v. Electoral Tribunal of the House of Representatives (199 SCRA 692 [1991]), quoting Ong Huan Tin v. Republic (19 SCRA 966 [1967]) and Ujano v. Republic (17 SCRA 147 [1966]):
      • Under both the previous Constitutions and the 1987 Constitution (as well as election laws), the term "residence" is synonymous with "domicile."
      • Definition of Domicile: It denotes a fixed permanent residence to which, when absent for business or pleasure, one intends to return.
      • Continuity Despite Absence: A person's absence from his permanent residence, no matter how long, does not interrupt the continuity of his residence at that place, which continues to be his domicile so long as it is characterized by animus revertendi (intent to return).
  • 2. No Loss of Residence Due to Transfer for Professional, Business, or Educational Reasons:
    • Abella v. Commission on Elections (201 SCRA 253 [1991]), citing Faypon v. Quirino (96 Phil. 294 [1954]):
      • General Doctrine: "Mere absence from one's residence or origin — domicile — to pursue studies, engage in business, or practice his avocation, is not sufficient to constitute abandonment or loss of such residence."
      • Determination of Intention & Burden of Proof: The determination of a person's legal residence or domicile largely depends upon intention, which may be inferred from his acts, activities, and utterances. The party who claims that a person has abandoned or left his residence of origin (domicile of origin) must show and prove preponderantly such abandonment or loss.
      • Effect of Voter Registration in Place of Work (Faypon v. Quirino): A citizen who leaves his birthplace to look for "greener pastures" (studying, practicing his avocation, or engaging in business) may not want to absent himself from the place of his professional or business activities during elections and may register as a voter there so as not to lose the opportunity to choose national officials. Despite such registration, if animus revertendi to his home/domicile of origin has not forsaken him, he does not lose his domicile of origin.
      • Application in Abella v. COMELEC (When Abandonment IS Proven): Where candidate Adelina Y. Larrazabal claimed she merely temporarily left her residence in Kananga, Leyte in 1975, the Supreme Court held that she had actually established her permanent residence in Ormoc City with her husband and considered herself a resident therein. The fact that she occasionally visited Kananga, Leyte through the years did not signify an intention to continue her residence there: "It is common among us Filipinos to often visit places where we (had) formerly resided (in) specially so when we have left friends and relatives therein although for intents and purposes we have already transferred our residences to other places." Hence, she lacked animus revertendi to Kananga and was disqualified.
  • 3. Three (3) Requisites to Successfully Effect a Change of Domicile:
    • Dumpit-Michelena v. Boado (475 SCRA 290 [2005]), citing Romualdez-Marcos v. COMELEC (248 SCRA 300 [1995]), Aquino v. COMELEC (248 SCRA 400 [1995]), and Domino v. COMELEC (310 SCRA 546 [1999]):
      • Rule: Domicile of origin is not easily lost. Without clear and positive proof of the concurrence of the following three (3) requirements, the domicile of origin continues:
        1. An actual removal or an actual change of domicile (the residence at the place chosen for the new domicile must be actual);
        2. A bona fide intention of abandoning the former place of residence and establishing a new one (there must be animus manendi [intent to remain in the new domicile for an indefinite period of time] coupled with animus non revertendi [intent not to return to the former domicile], and the change must be voluntary); AND
        3. Acts which correspond with the purpose.
  • 4. No Property Qualification Required to Establish Residency:
    • Co v. Electoral Tribunal of the House of Representatives (199 SCRA 692 [1991]), citing De los Reyes v. Solidum (61 Phil. 893 [1935]) and Maquera v. Borra (122 Phil. 412 [1965]):
      • Rejecting the contention that respondent Jose Ong Jr.'s lack of property in Laoang, Northern Samar equated to lack of residency, the Supreme Court held: "It is not required that a person should have a house in order to establish his residence and domicile. It is enough that he should live in the municipality or in a rented house or in that of a friend or relative."
      • Constitutional Rationale: Requiring a candidate to own real property in order to be eligible to run for public office would be tantamount to a property qualification, which is unconstitutional because the Constitution only requires age, citizenship, voting, and residence qualifications.

SECTION 40. DISQUALIFICATIONS

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 40, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 40. Disqualifications. — The following persons are disqualified from running for any elective local position: (a) Those sentenced by final judgment for an offense involving moral turpitude or for an offense punishable by one (1) year or more of imprisonment, within two (2) years after serving sentence; (b) Those removed from office as a result of an administrative case; (c) Those convicted by final judgment for violating the oath of allegiance to the Republic; (d) Those with dual citizenship; (e) Fugitives from justice in criminal or nonpolitical cases here or abroad; (f) Permanent residents in a foreign country or those who have acquired the right to reside abroad and continue to avail of the same right after the effectivity of this Code; and (g) The insane or feebleminded.

II. Exhaustive Breakdown of the Seven (7) Grounds for Disqualification

  • General Rules on Section 40:
    • Section 40 enumerates seven (7) distinct grounds for disqualifying any candidate for a local elective position.
    • Any one (1) ground is sufficient to disqualify a candidate.
    • Prospective Application of Section 40(b) (Grego v. COMELEC, 274 SCRA 481 [1997]): Section 40(b) applies only to those administratively removed from office on or after January 1, 1992 (the date of effectivity of the Local Government Code). There is no provision in the Code indicating that it operates retroactively to removals prior to January 1, 1992.
  • Ground (a): Conviction by Final Judgment for an Offense Involving Moral Turpitude OR an Offense Punishable by One (1) Year or More of Imprisonment:
    • Requisites for Disqualification under Section 40(a):
      1. There must be a sentence by final judgment; AND
      2. The conviction must be either:
        • For an offense involving moral turpitude (regardless of the duration of the penalty); OR
        • For any offense punishable by one (1) year or more of imprisonment (whether or not it involves moral turpitude);
      3. Duration of Disqualification: The candidate is disqualified during the service of sentence and within two (2) years after serving sentence.
        • Note on Omnibus Election Code (B.P. Blg. 881, Sec. 12) vs. LGC Sec. 40(a): In Jalosjos v. COMELEC (G.R. No. 205033, June 18, 2013) and Risos-Vidal v. COMELEC (G.R. No. 206666, January 21, 2015), the Supreme Court clarified that while Sec. 40(a) lifts the disqualification after 2 years from service of the principal sentence, if the convict was sentenced to a penalty carrying the accessory penalty of perpetual special disqualification from the right of suffrage (e.g., prision mayor or reclusion temporal under Arts. 41–42 of the Revised Penal Code), that perpetual accessory penalty bars the convict unless expressly remitted by an absolute pardon or extinguished by amnesty.
    • Definition of "Moral Turpitude" (In re: Gutierrez, 5 SCRA 661 [1962]; Court Administrator v. San Andres, 197 SCRA 704 [1991]; Teves v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 180363 [2009]):
      • Simply means "everything which is done contrary to justice, honesty, modesty, or good morals."
      • Covers any "act of baseness, vileness, or depravity in the private and social duties which a man owes his fellow men, to society in general, contrary to the accepted and customary rule of the right and duty between man and woman or conduct contrary to justice, honesty, modesty, or good morals."
      • Test (Mala in Se vs. Mala Prohibita): It implies something immoral in itself, regardless of whether it is punishable by law or not. It must not merely be mala prohibita (an act unlawful solely because it is prohibited by statute); the act itself must be inherently immoral.
    • Effect of Probation — Exemption from the 2-Year Disqualification (Moreno v. Commission on Elections, G.R. No. 168550, August 10, 2006):
      • Rule: The two (2)-year period of ineligibility under Section 40(a) applies both to those sentenced for an offense involving moral turpitude and to those sentenced for an offense punishable by 1 year or more of imprisonment.
      • Why Probationers Are NOT Disqualified: The phrase "service of sentence" in Section 40(a) applies only to a convict who has been confined in a penal facility for some time, and does NOT apply to a probationer whose principal and accessory penalties were suspended upon the grant of probation under Section 4 of the Probation Law (P.D. No. 968).
      • Two Effects of Probation on Eligibility:
        1. During the Period of Probation: The probationer does not "serve sentence" but merely complies with the conditions of the probation order. Because the accessory penalty of suspension from public office is put on hold during probation and the 2-year ineligibility period under Sec. 40(a) does not even begin to run, a probationer is not disqualified from running for a local elective office even while on probation.
        2. Upon Final Discharge from Probation: The trial court's order finally discharging the probationer restores him to all civil rights lost or suspended as a result of his conviction, including the right to run for public office.
  • Ground (b): Removal from Office as a Result of an Administrative Case:
    • The penalty of removal (dismissal) from local elective office as a result of an administrative case bars the official concerned from qualifying as a candidate (reiterated in Section 66[c] of the LGC).
    • Requisites & Doctrinal Updates:
      • The administrative removal must have occurred on or after January 1, 1992 (Grego v. COMELEC, 274 SCRA 481 [1997]).
      • The administrative decision imposing removal must have attained finality prior to the election (Lingating v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 153478, November 13, 2002).
      • STATUTORY / JURISPRUDENTIAL UPDATE (Abandonment of the Condonation / Aguinaldo Doctrine): In Carpio-Morales v. Court of Appeals and Jejomar Erwin S. Binay, Jr. (G.R. Nos. 217126–27, November 10, 2015, prospective from April 12, 2016), the Supreme Court abandoned the Condonation Doctrine (which previously held that reelection condones administrative misconduct committed during a prior term), ruling it incompatible with the 1987 Constitution's mandate that public office is a public trust. Furthermore, an administrative penalty of dismissal/removal from public service carries the accessory penalty of perpetual disqualification from holding any public office (whether elective or appointive).
  • Ground (c): Conviction by Final Judgment for Violating the Oath of Allegiance to the Republic:
    • Requires a final judgment of conviction by a competent court for an offense involving violation of allegiance to the Republic of the Philippines (e.g., treason, rebellion, sedition, or espionage).
  • Ground (d): Dual Citizenship (Construed as "Dual Allegiance") & R.A. No. 9225 Updates:
    • Constitutional Context (Art. IV, Sec. 5, 1987 Constitution): "Dual allegiance of citizens is inimical to the national interest and shall be dealt with by law."
    • Landmark Distinction: Dual Citizenship vs. Dual Allegiance (Mercado v. Manzano, 307 SCRA 630 [1999]; Valles v. COMELEC, 337 SCRA 543 [2000]):
      • The Supreme Court ruled that the phrase "dual citizenship" in Section 40(d) of R.A. No. 7160 must be understood as referring to "dual allegiance".
      • Consequently, persons with mere dual citizenship (involuntary dual citizens by birth) do NOT fall under this disqualification.

  • STATUTORY & JURISPRUDENTIAL UPDATE: R.A. No. 9225 (Citizenship Retention and Re-acquisition Act of 2003, Approved August 29, 2003):
    • Coverage (Sec. 3, R.A. No. 9225): Natural-born citizens of the Philippines who have lost their Philippine citizenship by reason of their naturalization as citizens of a foreign country may re-acquire (or retain, if naturalized after the effectivity of the Act) their Philippine citizenship upon taking the Oath of Allegiance to the Republic of the Philippines.
    • Mandatory Twin Requirements to Run for Elective Public Office (Sec. 5(2), R.A. No. 9225): Those seeking elective public office in the Philippines must:
      1. Meet all qualifications for holding such public office as required by the Constitution and existing laws (and take the Oath of Allegiance under Sec. 3); AND
      2. Make a personal and sworn renunciation of any and all foreign citizenship before any public officer authorized to administer an oath at the time of filing the Certificate of Candidacy (Jacot v. Dal, G.R. No. 179848, November 27, 2008; Cordora v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 176947, February 19, 2009).
    • Strict Prohibitions under Sec. 5(5)(a) & (b), R.A. No. 9225: The right to vote or be elected/appointed to any public office in the Philippines cannot be exercised by, nor granted to, those who:
      1. Are candidates for or are occupying any public office in the country of which they are naturalized citizens; AND/OR
      2. Are in active service as commissioned or non-commissioned officers in the armed forces of the country of which they are naturalized citizens.
    • Subsequent Use of Foreign Passport Recants the Renunciation (Maquiling v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 195649, April 16, 2013; Arnado v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 210164, August 18, 2015): Even if a candidate executes a valid Affidavit of Renunciation of foreign citizenship under Sec. 5(2) of R.A. No. 9225, if he or she subsequently uses a foreign passport to travel, such positive act effectively recants and nullifies the Oath of Renunciation, reverting the candidate to dual-citizenship/dual-allegiance status and disqualifying him or her under Section 40(d) of the LGC.
  • Ground (e): Fugitives from Justice in Criminal or Nonpolitical Cases Here or Abroad:
    • Legislative History ("Camaraderie" Provision):
      • This disqualification was an innovation proposed by the House of Representatives to protect a southern Tagalog congressman who intended to run for governor against an incumbent who had reportedly fled from U.S. justice.
      • Although the Senate panel in the Conference Committee allowed the provision on the understanding that a fugitive must be one convicted by final judgment, and Article 73 of the LGC Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR) attempted to limit "fugitive from justice" only to a person "who has been convicted by final judgment," the Supreme Court struck down Article 73 of the IRR.
    • Controlling Judicial Definition (Marquez, Jr. v. Commission on Elections, 243 SCRA 538 [1995]; Rodriguez v. Commission on Elections, 259 SCRA 296 [1996]):
      • The Supreme Court invalidated Article 73 of the IRR as an "inordinate and undue circumscription of the law."
      • Definition: A "fugitive from justice" includes NOT ONLY those who flee after conviction to avoid punishment, BUT LIKEWISE those who, after being charged, flee to avoid prosecution.
      • Indispensable Element — Intent to Evade: Intent to evade on the part of a candidate must be established by proof that there has already been a conviction or, at least, a criminal charge has already been filed, at the time of flight (Rodriguez v. COMELEC, where Gov. Eduardo Rodriguez left the U.S. five months before a criminal complaint was filed against him in Los Angeles, and thus lacked intent to evade at the time of flight).
  • Ground (f): Permanent Residents in a Foreign Country ("Green Card" Holders):
    • Scope: Disqualifies permanent residents in a foreign country or those who have acquired the right to reside abroad and continue to avail of the same right after January 1, 1992 (the date of effectivity of the Code).
    • Landmark Doctrine on Waiver of Permanent Resident Status (Caasi v. Court of Appeals, 191 SCRA 229 [1990]):
      • In disqualifying Bolinao, Pangasinan Mayor-elect Merito Miguel for being a U.S. "green card" holder, the Supreme Court held that to be qualified to run for elective office in the Philippines, a green card holder must have waived his status as a permanent resident or immigrant of a foreign country.
      • Mere Filing of COC is NOT a Waiver: The candidate's act of filing a Certificate of Candidacy (COC) for elective office in the Philippines does NOT of itself constitute a waiver of his status as a permanent resident/immigrant of the United States.
      • Requisites of a Valid Waiver: The waiver of his green card must be manifested by some act or acts independent of and done PRIOR to the filing of his candidacy for elective office in this country (e.g., formally surrendering the green card to the U.S. Embassy/immigration authorities before filing the COC). Without such prior independent waiver, the candidate is disqualified.
      • Rationale for Disqualification (Caasi v. Court of Appeals): Congress laid down a clear policy of excluding from elective public office those Philippine citizens who possess dual loyalties and allegiance. The law reserves the privilege of holding elective office for citizens who have cast their lot with our country "without mental reservations or purpose of evasion." Resident aliens of a foreign country are incapable of entire devotion to the interest and welfare of their homeland because "with one eye on their public duties here, they must keep another eye on their duties under the laws of the foreign country of their choice in order to preserve their status as permanent residents thereof."
      • Practical Evidentiary Note in the Text: The author notes the evidentiary difficulty in disqualifying green card holders due to the reluctance of U.S. embassy/immigration authorities to issue formal written certifications in time for election cases, suggesting that Philippine immigration authorities require Filipino green card holders to register upon entry.
  • Ground (g): The Insane or Feebleminded:
    • Handwritten Marginal Note on Page 6: Requires a competent court declaration ("- the Court - to declare a person insane or feebleminded"). A person is presumed to be of sound mind unless judicially declared insane or incompetent.

I. Codal Provisions (Secs. 42–43, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 43. II. Fundamental Doctrines on Term of Office, Tenure & Synchronization

  • Constitutional Basis (Art. X, Sec. 8, 1987 Constitution):
    • "The term of office of elective local officials, except barangay officials, which shall be determined by law, shall be three years and no such official shall serve for more than three consecutive terms. Voluntary renunciation of the office for any length of time shall not be considered as an interruption in the continuity of his service for the full term for which he was elected."
    • Why a Constitutional Amendment is Required for Provincial/City/Municipal Officials: Because the 3-year term of provincial, city, and municipal elective officials is fixed directly by Article X, Section 8 of the 1987 Constitution, Congress cannot lengthen their term to 4 or 5 years by ordinary legislation; doing so requires a constitutional amendment. By contrast, the Constitution explicitly exempted barangay officials ("except barangay officials, which shall be determined by law"), allowing Congress to fix or modify the term of Barangay and SK officials by statute.
  • Term Cannot Be Extended by Hold-Over Where Fixed by the Constitution (Osmeña v. Commission on Elections, 199 SCRA 750 [1991]):
    • Rule: Where the Constitution has in effect or by clear implication prescribed the term of office and fixed the day on which the official term shall begin (noon of June 30), the legislature cannot extend the term of provincial, city, or municipal officers by providing that they shall "hold over" until their successors are elected and qualified, even if their successors fail to qualify within the time.
    • Synchronization of Elections Reaffirmed (Osmeña v. COMELEC): The 1987 Constitution (Art. XVIII, Secs. 1, 2, & 5) mandates the synchronization of national and local elections on the second Monday of May every three years.
  • Distinction Between "Term" and "Tenure" (Dimaporo v. Mitra, Jr., 202 SCRA 779 [1991], citing Topacio Nueno v. Angeles, 76 Phil. 12 [1946]):
Aspect Term of Office Tenure of Office
Definition The fixed and definite period of time prescribed by the Constitution or by law during which an officer may claim to hold the office as of right. The actual period during which the incumbent actually holds the office and exercises its functions.
Susceptibility to Change When prescribed by the Constitution, it may NOT be extended or shortened by the legislature; it is fixed and unchanging. May be affected by circumstances within or beyond the power of the officer (e.g., resignation, death, removal, forfeiture by filing a COC for another office). Tenure may be shorter than the term, or it may not exist at all.
  • Security of Tenure of Barangay Officials (De Leon v. Esguerra, 153 SCRA 602 [1987]):
    • Elective barangay officials have a fixed statutory term of office that cannot be shortened arbitrarily (for example, by summary ouster or replacement by an Officer-in-Charge) without valid legal cause and due process.

  • The Two (2) Concurring Requisites of the Three-Term Limit Rule (Borja, Jr. v. Commission on Elections, 295 SCRA 157 [1998]; Ong v. Alegre, 479 SCRA 473 [2006]):
    • It is not enough that an individual has served three consecutive terms in an elective local office; he must also have been elected to the same position for the same number of times before the disqualification can apply.
    • For the three-term limit disqualification to apply, TWO (2) CONDITIONS OR REQUISITES MUST CONCUR:
      1. The official concerned has been ELECTED for three (3) consecutive terms to the same local government post; AND
      2. He or she has FULLY SERVED three (3) consecutive terms.
  • Rules on Voluntary vs. Involuntary Interruption of Service:
    • Voluntary Renunciation = NO Interruption: Under Section 43(b), voluntary renunciation of the office for any length of time (e.g., resigning a few months before the end of one's third term, or filing a COC for a higher office during one's term) shall NOT be considered as an interruption in the continuity of service for the full term for which the official was elected.
    • Succession by Operation of Law = NOT an Election: When a Vice Mayor succeeds to the office of Mayor by operation of law upon the Mayor's death or permanent vacancy and serves the remainder of the unexpired term, he was not elected to the office of Mayor for that term; hence, that service is NOT counted as one term for purposes of the three-term limit as Mayor (Borja, Jr. v. COMELEC).
    • Involuntary Interruption / Severance = BREAKS Continuity of Service (Lonzanida v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 135150, July 28, 1999; Dizon v. COMELEC & Morales, G.R. No. 182088, January 30, 2009):
      • There is no full service of term when there is an involuntary interruption or break in the continuity of service, such as when the official is involuntarily severed from office by legal process BEFORE the expiration of the term (resulting in an actual loss of title to the office).
      • Case Illustration — Dizon v. COMELEC & Morales (2009):
        • Mabalacat Mayor Marino Morales occupied the office of mayor during: (1) 1995–1998, (2) 1998–2001, (3) 2001–2004, and (4) July 1, 2004 to May 16, 2007.
        • In the consolidated Rivera v. COMELEC cases (promulgated May 9, 2007), the Supreme Court disqualified Morales for the 2004–2007 term and cancelled his COC. Morales vacated his post on May 16, 2007 — before the June 30, 2007 expiration of the term — and the Vice Mayor assumed office from May 17, 2007 to June 30, 2007.
        • Ruling: Morales could not be deemed to have served the full 2004–2007 term because he was ordered to vacate his post before the expiration of the term. The period from May 17, 2007 to June 30, 2007 served as an involuntary gap/interruption for purposes of the three-term limit rule. Thus, his subsequent 2007–2010 term was effectively his first term anew!
        • Furthermore, quoting Lonzanida v. COMELEC, the Court stressed that lengthy litigation and delays in resolving election cases "not shown to have been intentionally sought by the petitioner to prolong his stay in office cannot serve as basis to bar his right to be elected."
      • Contrast — Election Protest Decided AFTER Expiration of the Term (Ong v. Alegre, 479 SCRA 473 [2006]):
        • Where a duly proclaimed Mayor assumed office and exercised its functions from start to finish of the full term (1998–2001), and was only declared the loser in an election protest after the term of the contested office had already long expired, there was no actual interruption of service during the term. He is deemed to have fully served the term in the contemplation of the three-term rule, and is barred from running for a fourth consecutive term.

SECTION 44. PERMANENT VACANCIES IN THE OFFICES OF THE GOVERNOR, VICE GOVERNOR, MAYOR, AND VICE MAYOR

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 44, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 44. Permanent Vacancies in the Offices of the Governor, Vice Governor, Mayor, and Vice Mayor. — (a) If a permanent vacancy occurs in the office of the governor or mayor, the vice governor or vice mayor concerned shall become the governor or mayor. If a permanent vacancy occurs in the offices of the governor, vice governor, mayor, or vice mayor, the highest-ranking sanggunian member or, in case of his permanent inability, the second highest-ranking sanggunian member, shall become the governor, vice governor, mayor, or vice mayor, as the case may be. Subsequent vacancies in the said office shall be filled automatically by the other sanggunian members according to their ranking as defined herein.

(b) If a permanent vacancy occurs in the office of the punong barangay, the highest-ranking sangguniang barangay member or, in case of his permanent inability, the second highest-ranking sanggunian member, shall become the punong barangay.

(c) A tie between or among the highest-ranking sanggunian members shall be resolved by the drawing of lots.

(d) The successors as defined herein shall serve only the unexpired terms of their predecessors. For purposes of this Chapter, a permanent vacancy arises when an elective local official:

  1. Fills a higher vacant office;
  2. Refuses to assume office;
  3. Fails to qualify;
  4. Dies;
  5. Is removed from office;
  6. Voluntarily resigns; or
  7. Is otherwise permanently incapacitated to discharge the functions of his office.

For purposes of succession as provided in this Chapter, ranking in the sanggunian shall be determined on the basis of the proportion of votes obtained by each winning candidate to the total number of registered voters in each district in the immediately preceding local election.

II. Definition & Causes of Permanent Vacancy

  • Judicial Definition of "Vacancy" (Menzon v. Petilla, 197 SCRA 251 [1991]):
    • "There is no vacancy whenever the office is occupied by a legally qualified incumbent. A sensu contrario, there is a vacancy when there is no person lawfully authorized to assume and exercise at present the duties of the office."
  • Seven (7) Statutory Causes of Permanent Vacancy (Sec. 44(d), R.A. No. 7160):
    1. Fills a higher vacant office (e.g., Vice Mayor succeeds as Mayor, creating a permanent vacancy in the Office of the Vice Mayor);
    2. Refuses to assume office;
    3. Fails to qualify (e.g., cancelled Certificate of Candidacy or voided proclamation);
    4. Dies (Unda v. COMELEC, 190 SCRA 827 [1990]);
    5. Is removed from office (by final administrative decision or judicial conviction);
    6. Voluntarily resigns (Author's Note: Included in "voluntarily resigns" under the original Omnibus Election Code was the act of local officials filing Certificates of Candidacy for offices other than the ones they were occupying or for President/Vice President — NOTE: Under Section 14 of R.A. No. 9006 [Fair Election Act of 2001] and Fariñas v. Executive Secretary [G.R. No. 147387, Dec. 10, 2003], Section 67 of B.P. Blg. 881 was repealed, so elective officials are no longer deemed ipso facto resigned upon filing a COC for another office during regular elections); OR
    7. Is otherwise permanently incapacitated to discharge the functions of his office.

      1. 1st Highest-Ranking Sangguniang Barangay Member (or in case of permanent inability, the 2nd Highest-Ranking Member, and so on down the line) automatically becomes Punong Barangay;
      2. The resulting vacancy at the bottom of the Sangguniang Barangay is filled by appointment by the City or Municipal Mayor upon recommendation of the Sangguniang Barangay concerned under Section 45(a)(3).
    • STATUTORY UPDATE — Permanent Vacancy in the Office of the Sangguniang Kabataan (SK) Chairperson (Sec. 19, R.A. No. 10742 as amended by R.A. No. 11768):
      • In case an SK Chairperson refuses to assume office, fails to qualify, is convicted of a felony, voluntarily resigns, dies, is permanently incapacitated, or is removed from office, the SK Member (Kagawad) who obtained the highest number of votes in the election immediately preceding shall assume the office of SK Chairperson for the unexpired portion of his or her term. In case the said member refuses or fails to qualify, the next highest-ranking SK member shall assume the position.
  • Formula for Determining "Highest-Ranking" Sanggunian Member (Sec. 44(d)):
    • Because regular Sanggunian members in provinces, cities, and Metro Manila municipalities are elected by district (which have varying voter populations), ranking is NOT based on raw vote totals alone.
    • Statutory Formula:
      Ranking Percentage = Votes Obtained by the Winning Candidate Total Number of Registered Voters in That Candidate's District
    • Textbook Case Example: This formula enabled Quezon City Councilor Alice Herrera to assume the Vice Mayorship of Quezon City in 1992 when Vice Mayor Vicente Sotto III vacated the post by filing his Certificate of Candidacy for the Senate.
    • Ex-Officio / Appointive Members Excluded from Executive Succession: Only duly elected regular Sanggunian members (who obtained votes from the electorate) are included in the line of automatic succession to Governor, Vice Governor, Mayor, or Vice Mayor (Victoria v. COMELEC, G.R. No. 109005, January 10, 1994).
    • Resolution of Ties (Sec. 44(c)): A tie between or among the highest-ranking Sanggunian members is resolved by the drawing of lots.
    • Historical Context Note in the Text (1986 Freedom Constitution & OICs):
      • Senator Pimentel recounts the historical exception during the early months of President Corazon C. Aquino's revolutionary government under the 1986 Freedom Constitution, when automatic succession was not recognized. When Makati Mayor Nemesio Yabut died, Vice Mayor Wilson sought to succeed him and was sworn in by Vice President Salvador Laurel, but then-Minister of Local Government Aquilino Pimentel Jr. appointed Jejomar Binay as Officer-in-Charge (OIC) Mayor of Makati by authority of the President. The Supreme Court upheld the authority to appoint OICs under the Freedom Constitution in Governors of the Philippines v. Pimentel (G.R. No. 73823), The Municipal Mayors of the Philippines v. Pimentel (G.R. No. 73940), Topacio Jr. v. Pimentel (G.R. No. 73811), and Solis v. Pimentel (G.R. No. 73970).

SECTION 45. PERMANENT VACANCIES IN THE SANGGUNIAN

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 45, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 45. Permanent Vacancies in the Sanggunian. — (a) Permanent vacancies in the sanggunian where automatic successions provided above do not apply shall be filled by appointment in the following manner:

  1. The President, through the Executive Secretary, in the case of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan and the Sangguniang Panlungsod of highly urbanized cities and independent component cities;
  2. The governor, in the case of the Sangguniang Panlungsod of component cities and the Sangguniang Bayan;
  3. The city or municipal mayor, in the case of Sangguniang Barangay, upon recommendation of the Sangguniang Barangay concerned.

(b) Except for the Sangguniang Barangay, only the nominee of the political party under which the sanggunian member concerned had been elected and whose elevation to the position next higher in rank created the last vacancy in the sanggunian shall be appointed in the manner hereinabove provided. The appointee shall come from the same political party as that of the sanggunian member who caused the vacancy and shall serve the unexpired term of the vacant office. In the appointment herein mentioned, a nomination and a certificate of membership of the appointee from the highest official of the political party concerned are conditions sine qua non, and any appointment without such nomination and certification shall be null and void ab initio and shall be a ground for administrative action against the official responsible therefor.

(c) In case the permanent vacancy is caused by a sanggunian member who does not belong to any political party, the local chief executive shall, upon recommendation of the sanggunian concerned, appoint a qualified person to fill the vacancy.

(d) In case of vacancy in the representation of the youth and the barangay in the sanggunian, said vacancy shall be filled automatically by the official next in rank of the organization concerned.

II. Rules, Requisites & Doctrines on Filling Sanggunian Vacancies

  • 1. When Section 45 Applies:
    • Section 45 governs permanent vacancies in the various Sanggunians where automatic succession under Section 44 does not apply — such as when:
      • The lowest-ranking Sanggunian seat is left vacant after the other councilors/board members have moved up the ladder to fill a higher vacancy; OR
      • A Sanggunian member himself dies, resigns, is removed, or is permanently incapacitated (and the remaining members move up in rank, leaving the last/bottom rank vacant).
  • 2. The "Last Vacancy" & Political Party Nomination Rule (Sec. 45(b); Navarro v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 141307, March 28, 2001):
    • Rationale: To maintain the political party representation in the Sanggunian as willed by the electorate in the preceding election.
    • Whose Party Nominates the Replacement?
      • Because all lower-ranking Sanggunian members move up one notch under Section 44(a) when a higher vacancy occurs, the "last vacancy" (at the bottom of the Sanggunian) is caused by the lowest-ranking Sanggunian member whose elevation to the position next higher in rank left the bottom seat empty (or, as clarified by the Supreme Court in Navarro v. Court of Appeals [355 SCRA 672 (2001)], in order to preserve party representation inside the Sanggunian, the appointee must belong to the political party of the Sanggunian member whose elevation/departure actually reduced that party's representation in the Sanggunian).
    • Two (2) Mandatory Certificates (Conditions Sine Qua Non):
      1. Certificate of Nomination of the appointee signed by the highest official of the political party concerned; AND
      2. Certificate of Membership of the appointee signed by the highest official of the political party concerned.
    • Effect of Non-Compliance: Any appointment issued without both certificates is null and void ab initio (void from the very beginning) and constitutes a ground for administrative action against the appointing official.
  • 3. Four (4) Instances When Political Party Certificates Are NOT Required:
    1. When the Sanggunian member who caused the permanent vacancy does NOT belong to any political party (Independent candidate — governed by Sec. 45[c]);
    2. When the permanent vacancy occurs in the Sangguniang Barangay (because Barangay officials are strictly non-partisan by law — governed by Sec. 45[a][3]);
    3. When the permanent vacancy refers to the Ex-Officio Youth (Pederasyon ng mga SK) Representative in the Sanggunian (governed by Sec. 45[d]); OR
    4. When the permanent vacancy refers to the Ex-Officio Barangay (Liga ng mga Barangay) Representative in the Sanggunian (governed by Sec. 45[d]).
  • 4. Landmark Doctrine on Independent Members & Section 45(c) (Fariñas v. Barba, 256 SCRA 396 [1996]):
    • In Fariñas v. Barba, a permanent vacancy in the Sangguniang Bayan of San Nicolas, Ilocos Norte was caused by a member who did not belong to any political party. The Supreme Court resolved two critical textual ambiguities in Section 45(c):
      1. Meaning of "Local Chief Executive" in Section 45(c):
        • The phrase "local chief executive" in Section 45(c) is a stylistic misnomer and must be read in harmony with Section 45(a) and Section 63 (on Preventive Suspension), which vest executive power in descending order in the President, the Governor, and the Mayor.
        • Thus, "local chief executive" in Section 45(c) refers to the appointing authority specified in Section 45(a):
          • The President (through the Executive Secretary): For vacancies in the Sangguniang Panlalawigan and the Sangguniang Panlungsod of HUCs and ICCs;
          • The Provincial Governor: For vacancies in the Sangguniang Panlungsod of Component Cities and the Sangguniang Bayan of Municipalities; and
          • The City or Municipal Mayor: For vacancies in the Sangguniang Barangay.
      2. Meaning of "Sanggunian Concerned" in Section 45(c):
        • Refers to the Sanggunian in which the vacancy is created (consistent with Section 45[a][3]).
      3. Mandatory Nature of the Sanggunian's Recommendation:
        • Where there is no political party to make a nomination (either because the vacating member was an independent under Sec. 45[c] or because the vacancy is in the non-partisan Sangguniang Barangay under Sec. 45[a][3]), the recommendation of the Sanggunian where the vacancy occurs takes the place of party nomination and is likewise a condition sine qua non for the validity of the appointment.
        • Scope of Discretion: While the appointing authority (e.g., the Governor) is not bound to appoint a specific person recommended by the Sanggunian (since appointment is a discretionary power and he may ask for another recommendation), he cannot disregard the Sanggunian and appoint someone who was NOT recommended by the Sanggunian concerned.
  • 5. STATUTORY UPDATE: Filling Permanent Vacancies in the Sangguniang Kabataan (SK) Membership (R.A. No. 12326 [2026]):
    • Under Section 4-A of R.A. No. 12232, as inserted by R.A. No. 12326 (signed September 24, 2026), a permanent vacancy in the SK membership (SK Kagawad) shall be filled through appointment by the City or Municipal Mayor having jurisdiction over the barangay of the concerned SK, of any person who meets the qualifications provided under Section 10 of R.A. No. 10742 (the SK Reform Act of 2015, as amended by R.A. No. 11768).

SECTION 46. TEMPORARY VACANCY IN THE OFFICE OF THE LOCAL CHIEF EXECUTIVE

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 46, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 46. Temporary Vacancy in the Office of the Local Chief Executive. — (a) When the governor, city or municipal mayor, or punong barangay is temporarily incapacitated to perform his duties for physical or legal reasons such as, but not limited to, leave of absence, travel abroad, and suspension from office, the vice governor, city or municipal vice mayor, or the highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member shall automatically exercise the powers and perform the duties and functions of the local chief executive concerned, except the power to appoint, suspend, or dismiss employees which can only be exercised if the period of temporary incapacity exceeds thirty (30) working days.

(b) Said temporary incapacity shall terminate upon submission to the appropriate sanggunian of a written declaration by the local chief executive concerned that he has reported back to office. In cases where the temporary incapacity is due to legal causes, the local chief executive concerned shall also submit necessary documents showing that said legal causes no longer exist.

(c) When the incumbent local chief executive is traveling within the country but outside his territorial jurisdiction for a period not exceeding three (3) consecutive days, he may designate in writing the officer-in-charge of the said office. Such authorization shall specify the powers and functions that the local official concerned shall exercise in the absence of the local chief executive except the power to appoint, suspend, or dismiss employees.

(d) In the event, however, that the local chief executive concerned fails or refuses to issue such authorization, the vice governor, the city or municipal vice mayor, or the highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member, as the case may be, shall have the right to assume the powers, duties, and functions of the said office on the fourth (4th) day of absence of the said local chief executive, subject to the limitations provided in subsection (c) hereof.

(e) Except as provided above, the local chief executive shall in no case authorize any local official to assume the powers, duties, and functions of the office, other than the vice governor, the city or municipal vice mayor, or the highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member, as the case may be.

II. Rules, Procedures & Limitations on Temporary Vacancies

  • 1. Causes of Temporary Vacancy in the Office of the LCE (Sec. 46(a)):
    • Physical Reasons: Illness, physical injury, leave of absence, or travel abroad (for any duration), or domestic travel outside territorial jurisdiction exceeding 3 consecutive days.
    • Legal Reasons: Preventive or administrative suspension from office, arrest/detention, or temporary restraining order.
    • Automatic Assumption (No Appointment Required): The Vice Governor, City/Municipal Vice Mayor, or Highest-Ranking Sangguniang Barangay Member automatically exercises the powers and performs the duties of the LCE without needing any designation or appointment from anyone.
    • Jurisprudential Note on Acting LCE vs. Presiding Officer (Gamboa, Jr. v. Aguirre, Jr., G.R. No. 134213, July 20, 1999): When the Vice Governor or Vice Mayor assumes the powers and duties of the Governor or Mayor as Acting Governor / Acting Mayor during a temporary vacancy under Section 46(a), he cannot simultaneously continue to preside over the sessions of the Sanggunian because doing so would violate the separation of executive and legislative powers by uniting both executive and legislative leadership in one person; the Sanggunian must elect a temporary presiding officer under Section 49(b).
    • Jurisprudential Note on Temporary Vacancy in the Office of the Vice Governor (Menzon v. Petilla, 197 SCRA 251 [1991]): Although the Code is silent on the mode of succession in the event of a temporary vacancy in the Office of the Vice Governor (e.g., when the Vice Governor acts as Governor), the Supreme Court held in Menzon that to prevent a hiatus in public service, the mode of succession provided for permanent vacancies (or temporary designation by the President/DILG Secretary under the old Code) may be observed by analogy.
  • 2. Powers Withheld from the Acting LCE (The 30-Working-Day Rule):
    • General Rule: An Acting Governor, Acting Mayor, or Acting Punong Barangay CANNOT exercise the power to APPOINT, SUSPEND, or DISMISS employees.
    • Exception: He or she MAY exercise the power to appoint, suspend, or dismiss employees ONLY if the period of temporary incapacity EXCEEDS thirty (30) working days.
  • 3. Domestic Travel Outside Territorial Jurisdiction (The 3-Consecutive-Day Rule) (Secs. 46(c)–(e)):
    • When an LCE travels within the Philippines but outside his territorial jurisdiction for three (3) consecutive days or less, he is NOT considered physically absent, and there is no vacancy (not even a temporary one) in his office.
    • Strict Prohibition Against Arbitrary OICs (Sec. 46(e)):
      • In the past, Governors and Mayors routinely bypassed their elected Vice Governors or Vice Mayors by designating their secretaries, subordinate officials, or even prosecutors as "Officers-in-Charge" (OICs) whenever they traveled to Manila.
      • General Rule: Under Section 46(e), the LCE shall in NO case authorize any local official other than the Vice Governor, Vice Mayor, or highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member to assume the powers of the office.
      • Sole Exception: ONLY when the LCE is traveling within the country (outside his jurisdiction) for three (3) consecutive days or less under Section 46(c) may he designate in writing an Officer-in-Charge (OIC), specifying the powers to be exercised except the power to appoint, suspend, or dismiss employees.
      • Effect of Failure/Refusal or Travel Beyond 3 Days (Sec. 46(d)): If the LCE fails or refuses to issue a written authorization, or if the domestic travel exceeds three (3) days, the Vice Governor, Vice Mayor, or highest-ranking Sangguniang Barangay member automatically assumes the office on the fourth (4th) day of absence (still subject to the ban on appointing, suspending, or dismissing employees unless the absence exceeds 30 working days).
    • Travel Permission Required for Subordinate LCEs:
      • Mayors of component cities and municipalities need permission from their respective Provincial Governors to travel outside their jurisdictions.
      • Punong Barangays need permission from their respective City or Municipal Mayors to travel outside their jurisdictions.
  • 4. How Temporary Incapacity is Terminated (Sec. 46(b)):
    • If Caused by Physical Reasons (e.g., illness, leave, travel): Terminated upon submission to the appropriate Sanggunian of a written declaration by the LCE that he has reported back to office.
    • If Caused by Legal Reasons (e.g., suspension, arrest): The LCE must submit:
      1. A written declaration to the appropriate Sanggunian that he has reported back to office; AND
      2. Necessary documents showing that said legal causes no longer exist (e.g., expiration of suspension period or court order lifting suspension/detention).

SECTION 47. APPROVAL OF LEAVES OF ABSENCE

SECTION 48. LOCAL LEGISLATIVE POWER

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 48, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 48. Local Legislative Power. — Local legislative power shall be exercised by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan for the province; the Sangguniang Panlungsod for the city; the Sangguniang Bayan for the municipality; and the Sangguniang Barangay for the barangay.

II. Fundamental Principles, Requisites & Doctrines on Local Legislation

  • 1. The Four (4) Local Legislative Bodies (Sanggunians) & Their Statutory Powers:
    1. Sangguniang Panlalawigan (Province) — Powers enumerated in Section 468 of the LGC;
    2. Sangguniang Panlungsod (City) — Powers enumerated in Section 458 of the LGC;
    3. Sangguniang Bayan (Municipality) — Powers enumerated in Section 447 of the LGC; and
    4. Sangguniang Barangay (Barangay) — Powers enumerated in Section 391 of the LGC.
  • 2. Nature of Local Legislative Power: Subordinate Legislation (Solicitor General v. Metropolitan Manila Authority, 204 SCRA 837 [1991]):
    • General Rule (Delegated Power): Local political subdivisions are able to legislate only by virtue of a valid delegation of legislative power from the national legislature (Congress). They are mere agents vested with the power of subordinate legislation. As delegates of Congress, a local government unit cannot contravene, modify, or violate, but must obey at all times, the will of its principal (Congress).
    • Constitutional Exception (Direct Grant of Local Taxing & Revenue-Raising Power): The ONLY exception is the power of LGUs to create their own sources of revenue and to levy taxes, fees, and charges, which is conferred directly by the Constitution itself under Article X, Section 5 of the 1987 Constitution (subject to such guidelines and limitations as the Congress may provide, consistent with the basic policy of local autonomy).
  • 3. Contempt Powers Are NOT Vested in Local Sanggunians (Negros Oriental II Electric Cooperative, Inc. (NORECO II) v. Sangguniang Panlungsod of Dumaguete, 155 SCRA 421 [1987]):
    • Doctrine: Unlike the Senate and the House of Representatives (which possess inherent constitutional power to issue compulsory process and punish non-members for contempt in aid of legislation), local legislative bodies (Sanggunians) do NOT have an inherent or implied power to issue compulsory process (subpoena) or to cite and punish non-members for legislative contempt.
    • Because there is no provision in the Local Government Code explicitly granting local legislative bodies the power to issue compulsory process or punish for contempt, the Sangguniang Panlungsod of Dumaguete's subpoena and show-cause contempt order against NORECO II officials during a legislative investigation were declared null and void for being ultra vires.
  • 4. Presumption of Constitutionality and Validity of Ordinances (Tano v. Socrates, 278 SCRA 154 [1997]):
    • Ordinances enacted in the exercise of the powers under the Local Government Code (particularly the General Welfare Clause / police power under Sec. 16 and environmental protection powers) enjoy the presumption of constitutionality and validity.
    • Quantum Required to Overthrow Presumption: To overthrow this presumption, there must be a clear and unequivocal breach of the Constitution, not merely a doubtful or argumentative contradiction.
  • 5. Requisites for a Valid Ordinance (Lagcao v. Labra, 440 SCRA 279 [2004]):
    • A. Formal / Procedural Requisites:
      1. It must be within the corporate powers of the province, city, municipality, or barangay to enact; AND
      2. It must be passed according to the procedure prescribed by law.
    • B. Six (6) Substantive Requisites of a Valid Ordinance (Memorize for Bar Exams!):
      1. It must NOT contravene the Constitution or any statute (because municipal ordinances are inferior in status and subordinate to the laws of the state);
      2. It must NOT be unfair or oppressive;
      3. It must NOT be partial or discriminatory;
      4. It must NOT prohibit, but may regulate, trade (unless the trade or business is inherently unlawful or a nuisance per se);
      5. It must be general and consistent with public policy; AND
      6. It must NOT be unreasonable.

SECTION 49. PRESIDING OFFICER

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 49, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 49. Presiding Officer. — (a) The vice governor shall be the presiding officer of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan; the city vice mayor, of the Sangguniang Panlungsod; the municipal vice mayor, of the Sangguniang Bayan; and the punong barangay, of the Sangguniang Barangay. The presiding officer shall vote only to break a tie.

(b) In the event of the inability of the regular presiding officer to preside at a sanggunian session, the members present and constituting a quorum shall elect from among themselves a temporary presiding officer. He shall certify within ten (10) days from the passage of ordinances enacted and resolutions adopted by the sanggunian in the session over which he temporarily presided.

II. Key Rules & Doctrines on the Presiding Officer

  • 1. Regular Presiding Officers Across the Four (4) LGUs:
    • Province (Sangguniang Panlalawigan): Vice Governor
    • City (Sangguniang Panlungsod): City Vice Mayor
    • Municipality (Sangguniang Bayan): Municipal Vice Mayor
    • Barangay (Sangguniang Barangay): Punong Barangay
    • Rationale: In provinces, cities, and municipalities, the Code removes the Governor and Mayor from the Sanggunian and makes the Vice Governor and Vice Mayor the Presiding Officers to distribute powers among elective local officials so that the legislative (Sanggunian) can properly check the executive (Governor/Mayor) and vice versa without undue interference.
  • 2. Voting Rule of the Presiding Officer:
    • The Presiding Officer shall vote ONLY to break a tie (Sec. 49(a)).
  • 3. Rule on Temporary Inability to Preside (No Automatic Succession to the Chair) (Sec. 49(b)):
    • Whenever the regular presiding officer is unable to preside at a Sanggunian session, there is NO automatic succession (even by the highest-ranking Sanggunian member or President Pro-Tempore) to the position of presiding officer for that session.
    • Instead, the members present and constituting a quorum shall elect from among themselves a Temporary Presiding Officer.
    • Mandatory Duty of the Temporary Presiding Officer: He or she must certify within ten (10) days from passage the ordinances enacted and resolutions adopted by the Sanggunian in the session over which he or she temporarily presided.

SECTION 50. INTERNAL RULES OF PROCEDURE

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 50, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 50. Internal Rules of Procedure. — (a) On the first regular session following the election of its members and within ninety (90) days thereafter, the sanggunian concerned shall adopt or update its existing rules of procedure.

(b) The rules of procedure shall provide for the following:

  1. The organization of the sanggunian and the election of its officers as well as the creation of standing committees which shall include, but shall not be limited to, the committees on appropriations, women and family, human rights, youth and sports development, environmental protection, and cooperatives; the general jurisdiction of each committee; and the election of the chairman and members of each committee;
  2. The order and calendar of business for each session;
  3. The legislative process;
  4. The parliamentary procedures which include the conduct of members during sessions;
  5. The discipline of members for disorderly behavior and absences without justifiable cause for four (4) consecutive sessions, for which they may be censured, reprimanded, or excluded from the session, suspended for not more than sixty (60) days, or expelled: Provided, That the penalty of suspension or expulsion shall require the concurrence of at least two-thirds (2/3) vote of all the sanggunian members: Provided, further, That a member convicted by final judgment to imprisonment of at least one (1) year for any crime involving moral turpitude shall be automatically expelled from the sanggunian; and
  6. Such other rules as the sanggunian may adopt.

II. Key Rules, Doctrines & Disciplinary Matrix Under Section 50

  • 1. Adoption/Updating of Internal Rules & The "Three Readings in One Day" Doctrine (Malonzo v. Zamora, 323 SCRA 875 [2000]):
    • Timing: Must be taken up on the first regular session following the election of its members and completed within ninety (90) days thereafter.
    • Landmark Rulings in Malonzo v. Zamora (323 SCRA 875 [2000]):
      1. Updating of Internal Rules NOT a Precondition to Enacting Ordinances: The Local Government Code does NOT require the completion of the updating or adoption of the internal rules of procedure before the Sanggunian can act on any other matter like the enactment of an ordinance. It simply requires that the matter of adopting or updating the internal rules of procedure be taken up during the first day of session.

SECTION 51. FULL DISCLOSURE OF FINANCIAL AND BUSINESS INTERESTS OF SANGGUNIAN MEMBERS

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 51, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 51. Full Disclosure of Financial and Business Interests of Sanggunian Members. — (a) Every sanggunian member shall, upon assumption to office, make a full disclosure of his business and financial interests. He shall also disclose any business, financial, or professional relationship or any relation by affinity or consanguinity within the fourth civil degree, which he may have with any person, firm, or entity affected by any ordinance or resolution under consideration by the sanggunian of which he is a member, which relationship may result in conflict of interest. Such relationship shall include:

  1. Ownership of stock or capital, or investment, in the entity or firm to which the ordinance or resolution may apply; and
  2. Contracts or agreements with any person or entity which the ordinance or resolution under consideration may affect.

In the absence of a specific constitutional or statutory provision applicable to this situation, "conflict of interest" refers in general to one where it may be reasonably deduced that a member of a sanggunian may not act in the public interest due to some private, pecuniary, or other personal considerations that may tend to affect his judgment to the prejudice of the service or the public.

(b) The disclosure required under this Act shall be made in writing and submitted to the secretary of the sanggunian or the secretary of the committee of which he is a member. The disclosure shall, in all cases, form part of the record of the proceedings and shall be made in the following manner:

  1.   2. Disclosure shall be made when a member takes a position or makes a privilege speech on a matter that may affect the business interest, financial connection, or professional relationship described herein.

SECTION 52. SESSIONS

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 52, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 52. Sessions. — (a) On the first day of the session immediately following the election of its members, the sanggunian shall, by resolution, fix the day, time, and place of its regular sessions. The minimum number of regular sessions shall be once a week for the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, Sangguniang Panlungsod, and Sangguniang Bayan, and twice a month for the Sangguniang Barangay.

(b) When public interest so demands, special sessions may be called by the local chief executive or by a majority of the members of the sanggunian.

(c) All sanggunian sessions shall be open to the public unless a closed-door session is ordered by an affirmative vote of a majority of the members present, there being a quorum, in the public interest or for reasons of security, decency, or morality. (e) Each sanggunian shall keep a journal and record of its proceedings which may be published upon resolution of the sanggunian concerned.

SECTION 53. QUORUM

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 53, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 53. Quorum. — (a) A majority of all the members of the sanggunian who have been elected and qualified shall constitute a quorum to transact official business. Should a question of quorum be raised during a session, the presiding officer shall immediately proceed to call the roll of the members and thereafter announce the results.

(b) Where there is no quorum, the presiding officer may declare a recess until such time as a quorum is constituted, or a majority of the members present may adjourn from day to day and may compel the immediate attendance of any member absent without justifiable cause by designating a member of the sanggunian, to be assisted by a member or members of the police force assigned in the territorial jurisdiction of the local government unit concerned, to arrest the absent member and present him at the session.

(c) If there is still no quorum despite the enforcement of the immediately preceding subsection, no business shall be transacted. The presiding officer, upon proper motion duly approved by the members present, shall then declare the session adjourned for lack of quorum.

II. Rules, Computations & Procedures on Quorum

  • 1. Definition & Computation of Quorum (Sec. 53(a)):
    • Definition: A quorum is the fixed number of members present at a session which is considered legally sufficient to transact the business of the Sanggunian even if other members are absent.
    • Formula: More than half (commonly expressed as one-half plus one for even numbers) of all the members of the Sanggunian who have been ELECTED AND QUALIFIED.
    • Significance of the Phrase "Elected and Qualified" (Page 35–36 Commentary):
      • To be "elected and qualified" means the member has been proclaimed AND has assumed office (taken his oath and entered upon his duties).
      • Textbook Illustration: Suppose a Sanggunian has 10 regular seats, but only 5 members have been proclaimed and assumed office (elected and qualified), while the other 5 seats are tied up in pre-proclamation or electoral protests and those candidates have not yet qualified. The denominator for computing the quorum is 5 (the number of elected and qualified members), NOT 10! Thus, 3 out of the 5 elected and qualified members constitute a valid quorum to meet and transact official business.
    • Special Quorum for Veto Override (Page 35 Commentary):
      • When a session is intended to override a veto of the Local Chief Executive (which requires a 2/3 vote of all the members of the Sanggunian under Secs. 54[a] and 55[c]), the working quorum present at the session must not be less than two-thirds (2/3) of all the members of the Sanggunian.
    • Jurisprudential Note on Inclusion of the Presiding Officer & Ex-Officio Members in the Denominator (La Carlota City v. Rojo, G.R. No. 181367, April 24, 2012; Zamora v. Caballero, G.R. No. 147767, January 14, 2004): "All the members of the Sanggunian" includes the regular Sanggunian members, the ex-officio members (Liga and SK Federation presidents, plus PCL president in the SP), any sectoral representatives, and the Presiding Officer (Vice Governor / Vice Mayor) as a constituent member of the Sanggunian.
  • 2. Effect of Lack of Quorum: Void Ab Initio:
    • No Quorum, No Valid Session: Without a quorum, there can be no valid session of the Sanggunian. Any transaction or business conducted during a session without a quorum is VOID AB INITIO.
  • 3. Step-by-Step Procedure When Quorum is Questioned or Lacking (Secs. 53(a)–(c)):
    1. Question of Quorum Raised: The Presiding Officer must immediately call the roll of the members and announce the results.
    2. Remedies When There is No Quorum (Sec. 53(b)):
      • Option A (By the Presiding Officer): Declare a recess until such time as a quorum is constituted; OR
      • Option B (By a Majority of the Members Present):
        • Adjourn from day to day; AND
        • Compel the immediate attendance of any member absent without justifiable cause by designating a Sanggunian member, assisted by a member or members of the local police force assigned in the LGU, to ARREST the absent member and present him at the session.
        • Crucial Distinction on Purpose of Arrest: The arrest is NOT for the purpose of jailing or imprisoning the absent member, but solely to compel his physical attendance and presentation at the session.
    3. If Still No Quorum Despite Enforcement (Sec. 53(c)):
      • No business shall be transacted; and
      • The Presiding Officer, upon proper motion duly approved by the members present, shall declare the session adjourned for lack of quorum.

SECTION 54. APPROVAL OF ORDINANCES

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 54, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 54. Approval of Ordinances. — (a) Every ordinance enacted by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, Sangguniang Panlungsod, or Sangguniang Bayan shall be presented to the provincial governor or city or municipal mayor, as the case may be. If the local chief executive concerned approves the same, he shall affix his signature on each and every page thereof; otherwise, he shall veto it and return the same with his objections to the sanggunian, which may proceed to reconsider the same. The sanggunian concerned may override the veto of the local chief executive by two-thirds (2/3) vote of all its members, thereby making the ordinance or resolution effective for all legal intents and purposes.

(b) The veto shall be communicated by the local chief executive concerned to the sanggunian within fifteen (15) days in the case of a province, and ten (10) days in the case of a city or a municipality; otherwise, the ordinance shall be deemed approved as if he had signed it.

(c) Ordinances enacted by the Sangguniang Barangay shall, upon approval by the majority of all its members, be signed by the punong barangay.

II. Key Doctrines, Distinctions & Procedures on Approval of Ordinances

  • 1. Ordinance vs. Resolution Distinguished (Art. 107[a] & [c], LGC IRR; Municipality of Parañaque v. V.M. Realty Corp., 292 SCRA 678 [1998]):
Point of Distinction Ordinance Resolution
Legal Nature A LAW (legislative act of a general and permanent character) possessing the force and effect of a statute within the LGU. Merely a declaration of the sentiment or opinion of a lawmaking body on a specific matter.
Permanence & Subject Matter General and permanent character (e.g., tax codes, zoning, police power regulations, appropriations, and exercising eminent domain under Sec. 19 of the LGC). Temporary character, or matters relating to proprietary functions and private concerns (Art. 107[a], IRR).
Requirement of Third (3rd) Reading MANDATORY — must undergo three (3) readings before final enactment. NOT required — enacted in the same manner as an ordinance, except that it need NOT go through a third (3rd) reading for final consideration unless decided otherwise by a majority of all the Sanggunian members (Art. 107[c], IRR).
Sufficiency for Eminent Domain (Parañaque v. V.M. Realty) Valid basis to authorize the LCE to initiate expropriation proceedings under Section 19 of the LGC. INVALID basis for expropriation under the 1991 LGC (unlike under the old LGC / B.P. Blg. 337 studied in Moday v. CA, Sec. 19 of R.A. No. 7160 strictly requires an ordinance)!
  • 2. Approval by Governor/Mayor is Discretionary, NOT Ministerial (De los Reyes v. Sandiganbayan, Third Division, 281 SCRA 631 [1997]):
    • Doctrine: The approval of a Sanggunian ordinance or resolution by the Governor or Mayor is NOT a ministerial act.
    • The grant of the veto power confers authority beyond the simple mechanical act of signing an ordinance or resolution; it accords the Local Chief Executive the discretion to sustain a resolution or ordinance in the first instance or to veto it and return it with his objections. Concurrence requires "not only a flourish of the pen, but the application of judgment after meticulous analysis and intelligence as well."
    • Contrast with the Punong Barangay (Sec. 54(c)):
      • In the case of Barangay Ordinances, once approved by a majority of all the members of the Sangguniang Barangay, the approval of the Punong Barangay is NOT required to make the ordinance effective, because the Punong Barangay is himself the Presiding Officer and a member of the Sangguniang Barangay and has no veto power. Hence, the action of the majority binds him, and signing and implementing a duly enacted Barangay Ordinance is MINISTERIAL on the part of the Punong Barangay.
  • 3. Three (3) Modes by Which a Provincial, City, or Municipal Ordinance Becomes Approved (Secs. 54(a)–(b)):
    1. Express Approval: The Governor or City/Municipal Mayor affixes his signature on each and every page of the ordinance.
    2. Approval by Executive Inaction (Lapse of Period): The LCE fails to communicate a veto to the Sanggunian within:
      • Fifteen (15) days from date of receipt (for a Provincial Governor); or
      • Ten (10) days from date of receipt (for a City or Municipal Mayor).
      • Effect: The ordinance is deemed approved as if he had signed it.
    3. Legislative Override of Veto: When the LCE timely vetoes the ordinance and returns it with written objections, and the Sanggunian overrides the veto by a two-thirds (2/3) vote of ALL its members.
  • 4. Mandatory Judicial Notice of Municipal Ordinances (Gallego v. People of the Philippines, 8 SCRA 813 [1963]):
    • Trial courts (e.g., Municipal Trial Courts / Metropolitan Trial Courts) must take mandatory judicial notice of municipal ordinances in force within their respective territorial jurisdictions. Thus, the enactment and provisions of such ordinances need not be proven by evidence in proceedings before the trial courts of that municipality/city.

SECTION 55. VETO POWER OF THE LOCAL CHIEF EXECUTIVE

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 55, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 55. Veto Power of the Local Chief Executive. — (a) The local chief executive may veto any ordinance of the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, Sangguniang Panlungsod, or Sangguniang Bayan on the ground that it is ultra vires or prejudicial to the public welfare, stating his reasons therefor in writing.

(b) The local chief executive, except the punong barangay, shall have the power to veto any particular item or items of an appropriations ordinance, an ordinance or resolution adopting a local development plan and public investment program, or an ordinance directing the payment of money or creating liability. In such a case, the veto shall not affect the item or items which are not objected to. The vetoed item or items shall not take effect unless the sanggunian overrides the veto in the manner herein provided; otherwise, the item or items in the appropriations ordinance of the previous year corresponding to those vetoed, if any, shall be deemed reenacted.

(c) The local chief executive may veto an ordinance or resolution only once. The sanggunian may override the veto of the local chief executive concerned by two-thirds (2/3) vote of all its members, thereby making the ordinance effective even without the approval of the local chief executive concerned.

II. Complete Breakdown of the Veto Power

  • 1. Who Possesses Veto Power?:
    • Vested In: Provincial Governor, City Mayor, and Municipal Mayor.
    • Excluded: The Punong Barangay has NO VETO POWER over ordinances of the Sangguniang Barangay (Sec. 54(c) & Sec. 55(b)).
  • 2. Two (2) Statutory Grounds for Exercise of Veto (Sec. 55(a)):
    1. Ultra Vires: The ordinance or part thereof is beyond the power of the Sanggunian to enact;
      • Case Example — Villacorta v. Bernardo (143 SCRA 480 [1986]): When a Sanggunian enacts an ordinance (Dagupan City Ordinance No. 22) that amends and violates a national law (Act No. 496 / Land Registration Act) under the guise of implementing it by imposing additional subdivision certification requirements, fees, and penalties not found in the national statute, that ordinance is void for being ultra vires.
    2. Prejudicial to the Public Welfare: The ordinance or part thereof is inimical or harmful to the general welfare of the LGU's constituents.
  • 3. Formal & Procedural Requisites of a Valid Veto:
    1. Must be based on ultra vires or prejudicial to the public welfare;
    2. Must state the LCE's reasons/objections in writing;
    3. Must be communicated to the Sanggunian within:
      • Fifteen (15) days from receipt (for a Province); or
      • Ten (10) days from receipt (for a City or Municipality);
    4. One-Veto Limit (Sec. 55(c)): The LCE may veto an ordinance or resolution ONLY ONCE. Once overridden by the Sanggunian, the veto cannot be reimposed on the same ordinance or subject matter.
  • 4. General Veto vs. Item (Line-Item) Veto (Secs. 55(a)–(b)):

  • 5. Vote Required for Veto Override (Sec. 54(a) & Sec. 55(c)):
    • Requires a two-thirds (2/3) vote of ALL the members of the Sanggunian (meaning at least 2/3 of the entire Sanggunian membership, not merely 2/3 of those present).
    • Effect of Override: Makes the ordinance or resolution effective for all legal intents and purposes even without the approval/signature of the LCE.

SECTIONS 56, 57 & 58. REVIEW OF ORDINANCES AND ENFORCEMENT OF DISAPPROVED MEASURES

I. Codal Provisions (Secs. 56–58, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 56. Review of Component City and Municipal Ordinances or Resolutions by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan. — (a) Within three (3) days after approval, the secretary to the sangguniang panlungsod or sangguniang bayan shall forward to the Sangguniang Panlalawigan for review, copies of approved ordinances and the resolutions approving the local development plans and public investment programs formulated by the local development councils.

(b) Within thirty (30) days after receipt of copies of such ordinances and resolutions, the Sangguniang Panlalawigan shall examine the documents or transmit them to the provincial attorney, or if there be none, to the provincial prosecutor for prompt examination. The provincial attorney or provincial prosecutor shall, within a period of ten (10) days from receipt of the documents, inform the Sangguniang Panlalawigan in writing of his comments or recommendations, which may be considered by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan in making its decision.

(c) If the Sangguniang Panlalawigan finds that such an ordinance or resolution is beyond the power conferred upon the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan concerned, it shall declare such ordinance or resolution invalid in whole or in part. The Sangguniang Panlalawigan shall enter its action in the minutes and shall advise the corresponding city or municipal authorities of the action it has taken.

(d) If no action has been taken by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan within thirty (30) days after submission of such an ordinance or resolution, the same shall be presumed consistent with law and therefore valid.

Section 57. Review of Barangay Ordinances by the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan. — (a) Within ten (10) days after its enactment, the Sangguniang Barangay shall furnish copies of all barangay ordinances to the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan concerned for review as to whether the ordinance is consistent with law and city or municipal ordinances.

(b) If the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan, as the case may be, fails to take action on barangay ordinances within thirty (30) days from receipt thereof, the same shall be deemed approved.

(c) If the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan, as the case may be, finds the barangay ordinances inconsistent with law or city or municipal ordinances, the sanggunian concerned shall, within thirty (30) days from receipt thereof, return the same with its comments and recommendations to the Sangguniang Barangay concerned for adjustment, amendment, or modification; in which case, the effectivity of the barangay ordinance is suspended until such time as the revision called for is effected.

Section 58. Enforcement of Disapproved Ordinances or Resolutions. — Any attempt to enforce any ordinance or any resolution approving the local development plan and public investment program, after the disapproval thereof, shall be sufficient ground for the suspension or dismissal of the official or employee concerned.

II. Landmark Doctrine & Side-by-Side Comparison of Sections 56, 57 & 58

  • 1. Landmark Doctrine on the Sole Ground for Sangguniang Panlalawigan Invalidation (Moday v. Court of Appeals, 268 SCRA 586 [1997]):
    • Who Exercises Review Power: Under Section 56, the power to review component city and municipal ordinances is vested in the Sangguniang Panlalawigan, NOT the Provincial Governor.
    • Note on HUCs & ICCs: Ordinances and resolutions of Highly Urbanized Cities (HUCs) and Independent Component Cities (ICCs) are NOT subject to review by the Sangguniang Panlalawigan (under Sec. 29 of the LGC, they are independent of the province); only Component Cities and Municipalities are reviewed under Section 56.
    • SOLE Ground to Invalidate Under Section 56(c) (Moday v. Court of Appeals, 268 SCRA 586 [1997]):
      • In Moday, the Municipality of Bunawan passed a legislative measure authorizing the Mayor to initiate expropriation proceedings for a public gymnasium, which the Sangguniang Panlalawigan of Agusan del Sur disapproved.
      • Ruling: The Supreme Court held that the Sangguniang Panlalawigan's disapproval was an infirm action that did not render the municipal measure null and void, because the municipality clearly possessed the statutory power of eminent domain.
      • Golden Rule: "The law grants the Sangguniang Panlalawigan the power to declare a municipal resolution [or ordinance] invalid on the SOLE GROUND that it is beyond the power of the Sangguniang Bayan [or Sangguniang Panlungsod] or the mayor to issue (ultra vires)." Unlike the Mayor's veto power under Section 55(a) (which includes "prejudicial to the public welfare"), the Sangguniang Panlalawigan on review under Section 56(c) cannot invalidate a component city or municipal ordinance merely because it disagrees with its wisdom or policy; it can only check if the measure is within the conferred powers (intra vires vs. ultra vires)!

Ground(s) for Disapproval / Invalidation SOLE GROUND: That the ordinance or resolution is beyond the power conferred upon the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan concerned (ultra vires) (Moday v. CA). That the barangay ordinance is inconsistent with law OR with city or municipal ordinances.
Action Upon Disapproval & Legal Effect Declared invalid in whole or in part, entered in the minutes, and communicated to the city/municipal authorities. Returned with comments and recommendations to the Sangguniang Barangay for adjustment, amendment, or modification; effectivity is SUSPENDED until the revision called for is effected.
  • 3. Section 58: Sanction for Attempting to Enforce Disapproved Ordinances or Resolutions:
    • Prohibited Act: Any attempt to enforce any ordinance or any resolution approving the local development plan and public investment program after the disapproval thereof (under Sec. 56 or Sec. 57).
    • Persons Liable: Both elective AND appointive LGU officials or employees.
    • Penalty: Sufficient ground for suspension or dismissal of the erring official or employee (an additional statutory ground for administrative discipline).

SECTION 59. EFFECTIVITY OF ORDINANCES OR RESOLUTIONS

I. Codal Provision (Sec. 59, R.A. No. 7160)

Section 59. Effectivity of Ordinances or Resolutions. — (a) Unless otherwise stated in the ordinance or the resolution approving the local development plan and public investment program, the same shall take effect after ten (10) days from the date a copy thereof is posted in a bulletin board at the entrance of the provincial capitol or city, municipal, or barangay hall, as the case may be, and in at least two (2) other conspicuous places in the local government unit concerned.

(b) The secretary to the sanggunian concerned shall cause the posting of an ordinance or resolution in the bulletin board at the entrance of the provincial capitol and the city, municipal, or barangay hall in at least two (2) conspicuous places in the local government unit concerned not later than five (5) days after approval thereof. The text of the ordinance or resolution shall be disseminated and posted in Filipino or English and in the language or dialect understood by the majority of the people in the local government unit concerned, and the secretary to the sanggunian shall record such fact in a book kept for the purpose, stating the dates of approval and posting.

(c) The gist of all ordinances with penal sanctions shall be published in a newspaper of general circulation within the province where the local legislative body concerned belongs. In the absence of any newspaper of general circulation within the province, posting of such ordinances shall be made in all municipalities and cities of the province where the sanggunian of origin is situated.

(d) In the case of highly urbanized and independent component cities, the main features of the ordinance or resolution duly enacted or adopted shall, in addition to being posted, be published once in a local newspaper of general circulation within the city: Provided, That in the absence thereof the ordinance or resolution shall be published in any newspaper of general circulation.

II. Step-by-Step Rules on Posting, Publication & Effectivity

  • 1. General Rule on Date of Effectivity (Sec. 59(a)):
    • Primary Rule: An ordinance or a resolution approving the local development plan and public investment program takes effect on the date stated/fixed in the ordinance or resolution itself (provided mandatory posting/publication requirements are complied with).
    • Default Rule (If Not Stated in the Measure): It shall take effect "after ten (10) days" — meaning on the ELEVENTH (11th) DAY — from the date a copy thereof is posted:
      1. In a bulletin board at the entrance of the provincial capitol or city, municipal, or barangay hall, as the case may be; AND
      2. In at least two (2) other conspicuous places in the LGU concerned.
      • Author's Textual Clarification (Page 44): Although the phrase "after ten days" could literally mean any day after the 10th day, the legislative intent is that the ordinance or resolution takes effect on the day immediately following the 10-day posting period (i.e., the 11th day).
  • 2. Mandatory Duties of the Sanggunian Secretary on Posting & Language (Sec. 59(b)):
    • Deadline to Post: Must cause the posting not later than five (5) days after approval of the ordinance or resolution.
    • Dual-Language Requirement: Must be disseminated and posted in:
      1. Filipino OR English; AND
      2. The language or dialect understood by the majority of the people in the LGU concerned.
    • Mandatory Record Book: The Sanggunian Secretary must record the fact of posting in a book kept for the purpose, stating the dates of approval and posting.

  • 4. End-to-End Lifecycle Timeline of a Local Ordinance (Secs. 50, 54, 55, 56, 57 & 59):
    1. Enactment by the Sanggunian (Sec. 50 & IRR Art. 107):
      • Undergoes three (3) readings (which may be held in a single session day per Malonzo v. Zamora unless barred by internal rules) and is passed by the required vote, there being a quorum (Sec. 53).
    2. Presentation to the Local Chief Executive (Sec. 54 & Sec. 55):
      • Province: Governor has 15 days from receipt to sign every page or communicate a written veto; otherwise deemed approved.
      • City / Municipality: Mayor has 10 days from receipt to sign every page or communicate a written veto; otherwise deemed approved.
      • Barangay: Signed ministerially by the Punong Barangay upon approval by a majority of all Sangguniang Barangay members (no veto power).
      • If Vetoed: Sanggunian may override the veto by a 2/3 vote of all its members (LCE may veto only once).
    3. Review by Higher Sanggunian (Sec. 56 & Sec. 57):
      • Component City / Municipal Ordinance (or LDP/PIP Resolution): Forwarded by Sanggunian Secretary to the Sangguniang Panlalawigan within 3 days after approval → SP may refer to the Provincial Attorney / Prosecutor (who has 10 days to comment) → SP has 30 days from receipt to declare it invalid in whole or in part on the sole ground of being ultra vires (Moday v. CA); if no action within 30 days, presumed valid.
      • Barangay Ordinance: Furnished by Sangguniang Barangay to the Sangguniang Panlungsod or Sangguniang Bayan within 10 days after enactment → SPanlungsod/SBayan has 30 days from receipt to review for consistency with law and city/municipal ordinances → if inconsistent, returned for adjustment/amendment and effectivity is suspended; if no action within 30 days, deemed approved.
    4. Posting, Publication & Effectivity (Sec. 59):
      • Sanggunian Secretary causes posting not later than 5 days after approval at the Capitol/Hall entrance bulletin board and at least 2 conspicuous places (in Filipino/English AND the local dialect), plus newspaper publication if it is a penal ordinance (Sec. 59[c]) or an HUC/ICC measure (Sec. 59[d]).
      • Takes effect after 10 days from posting (on the 11th day), unless another date is fixed in the ordinance/resolution itself.
