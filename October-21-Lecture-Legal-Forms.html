I. THE NATURE AND ESSENTIAL ELEMENTS OF A CONTRACT IN LEGAL DRAFTING

A. Statutory Definition and Scope in Legal Forms

Under Article 1305 of the Civil Code, a contract is a meeting of minds between two persons whereby one binds himself, with respect to the other, to give something or to render some service. In the context of Legal Forms and Drafting, the general principles of Obligations and Contracts transition from abstract doctrinal theory into tangible, enforceable written instruments.

  • The Concept of "Meeting of Minds": A contract exists when the offer and acceptance of the contracting parties concur upon the exact terms, conditions, and subject matter of the agreement.
  • Scope of Prestations: While Article 1305 explicitly mentions obligations to give (real obligations) and to render some service (positive personal obligations to do), the prestation of a contract equally encompasses negative personal obligations (obligations not to do), such as non-compete covenants, confidentiality undertakings, or restrictions on subleasing.
  • Oral vs. Written Contracts: Although Philippine civil law generally recognizes the validity of oral or verbal contracts under the principle of consensuality (Article 1315, Civil Code), the discipline of Legal Forms focuses exclusively on reducing agreements into written instruments to ensure enforceability, evidentiary certainty, and registrability.

B. Essential Requisites of a Contract (COC)

Under Article 1318 of the Civil Code, there is no contract unless the following three essential requisites concur: (1) Consent of the contracting parties; (2) Object certain which is the subject matter of the contract; and (3) Cause of the obligation which is established. When drafting any legal instrument, the lawyer must ensure that all three elements are explicitly and unmistakably reflected on the face of the document.

1. Consent of the Contracting Parties

Consent is manifested by the meeting of the offer and the acceptance upon the thing and the cause which are to constitute the contract (Article 1319, Civil Code).

  • Manner of Signifying Consent in Written Contracts:
    • Standard Wet-Ink Signature: The primary and most common method of signifying express consent to a written instrument is by affixing one's customary signature above the printed name in the signature block.
    • Thumbmark or Other Physical Mark: If a contracting party is illiterate, physically unable to write, or otherwise incapable of signing, consent may be signified by affixing a thumbmark (or another finger's print if the party lacks a thumb).
    • Notarial Modifications for Thumbmarks: Under the 2004 Rules on Notarial Practice (A.M. No. 02-8-13-SC), when a party executes a document by thumbmark or other mark in lieu of a handwritten signature, best practice and notarial rules require at least two (2) disinterested instrumental witnesses to attest that the party affixed the thumbmark in their presence. Furthermore, the drafter must modify the language of the Acknowledgment or Jurat to state that the affiant or executing party "affixed his/her thumbmark" rather than "signed" the instrument.

Even if a person is 18 years or older, suffering from severe mental disability or cognitive incapacity vitiates consent.

2. Object Certain (Subject Matter of the Contract)

The object of a contract may be things, rights, or services that are not outside the commerce of men, are not impossible, and are determinate as to their kind (Articles 1347–1349, Civil Code). In contract drafting, the object must be specific and sufficiently definite so that it can be identified without the need for a new agreement between the parties.

  • Distinction Between Generic and Specific Objects: A generic thing is identified only by its specie or class (e.g., "a black Kawasaki motorcycle"), whereas a specific or determinate thing is particularly designated or physically segregated from all others of the same class. A Deed of Sale or Chattel Mortgage covering a movable or immovable must describe a specific thing.
  • Drafting Descriptions for Movable Property (Motor Vehicles): Simply describing the object as "a black Toyota car" or "a Kawasaki motorcycle" is fatally defective for registration and enforcement. The drafter must examine the Certificate of Registration (CR) and Official Receipt (OR) issued by the Land Transportation Office (LTO) and explicitly state the following technical specifications:
    1. Make, Brand, and Series/Model;
    2. Year Model and Body Type;
    3. Motor or Engine Number;
    4. Chassis or Serial Number;
    5. Plate Number and MV File Number;
    6. Color; and
    7. CR Number and OR Number.
  • Drafting Descriptions for Immovable Property (Real Estate): Colloquial or informal landmark descriptions (such as describing land in Ilocano vernacular as being "near the salamagi [tamarind] tree" or using general directional terms like daya [east] and laud [west] without technical metes and bounds) are legally insufficient.
    • Titled Real Property: The contract must specify the Transfer Certificate of Title (TCT) or Original Certificate of Title (OCT) number, the Registry of Deeds where it is registered, the Lot Number, Block Number, Survey/Plan Number, exact location (Barangay, Municipality/City, Province), total area in square meters, and the verbatim technical description (metes and bounds) appearing on the title.
    • Untitled / Unregistered Real Property: Where the land is not yet covered by a Torrens title, the drafter must identify it using the Tax Declaration (TD) Number, Property Index Number (PIN), exact location, approximate area, and the specific adjoining boundary owners on the North, East, South, and West as recorded in the Municipal or Provincial Assessor's Office.

Professor's Illustration: Defective Colloquial Land Descriptions vs. Technical Specificity
If a client asks you to draft a Deed of Sale for a parcel of land in the province and describes its location merely as "my land in Tinagpudtudan or Daldalayap, right beside the big salamagi (tamarind) tree," you cannot put that colloquial description in the deed. What if the tamarind tree is cut down or dies? The object becomes unidentifiable. The lawyer must require the client to produce the Transfer Certificate of Title (TCT) or, if unregistered, the current Tax Declaration, and copy the exact technical description and boundary limits into the instrument.

3. Cause or Consideration of the Obligation

Under Article 1350 of the Civil Code, in onerous contracts the cause is understood to be, for each contracting party, the prestation or promise of a thing or service by the other; in remuneratory ones, the service or benefit which is remunerated; and in contracts of pure beneficence, the mere liberality of the benefactor.

  • Onerous Contracts (e.g., Deed of Sale, Contract of Lease): The cause or consideration for the seller or lessor is the monetary price or rental paid or promised by the buyer or lessee. The drafter must state the exact currency and amount both in words and in figures (e.g., "ONE HUNDRED THOUSAND PESOS (PhP 100,000.00), Philippine Currency") to prevent alteration or ambiguity.
  • Gratuitous Contracts / Contracts of Pure Beneficence (e.g., Deed of Donation): There is no monetary consideration. Instead, the drafter must explicitly recite that the conveyance is made out of the donor's "liberality and generosity" or out of "love and affection" for the donee (such as when parents or grandparents donate a parcel of land to a newlywed child or grandchild to help them start their married life).

1. Identify the Parties Correctly and Verify Legal Capacity

The opening paragraph (Exordium) must accurately state the full legal names of the parties, their capacities, and their authority if acting on behalf of another.

  • Personal Circumstances: Always state whether a natural person is "of legal age", their nationality/citizenship (critical for constitutional restrictions on land ownership under Article XII of the 1987 Constitution), their civil status (single, married, widow/widower, or legally separated), and their residence or postal address.
  • Importance of Civil Status: Under the Family Code (Articles 96 and 124), any disposition or encumbrance of community or conjugal property without the written consent of the other spouse is void. Indicating civil status alerts the drafter to include a marital consent clause or have both spouses execute the deed.
  • Representative Capacity: If the person signing is not the principal owner or obligor, the contract must explicitly disclose the representative relationship in both the exordium and the signature block (e.g., "JUAN DELA CRUZ, of legal age, Filipino, single, and a resident of Baguio City, herein represented by his Attorney-in-Fact, PEDRO PENDUKO, by virtue of a Special Power of Attorney dated...").

2. Be Specific: State Who Must Do What, By When, and For How Much

Ambiguity regarding performance schedules is the leading cause of breach-of-contract litigation. Specificity is especially vital in Deeds of Conditional Sale or Contracts to Sell where the purchase price is paid in installments following an initial down payment.

  • Why Specificity Matters—Determining Delay (Mora): Under Article 1169 of the Civil Code, those obliged to deliver or to do something incur in delay from the time the obligee judicially or extrajudicially demands from them the fulfillment of their obligation.
  • Ordinary Delay vs. Legal Delay (Mora Solvendi):
    • Ordinary Delay is merely the non-performance of an obligation on the stipulated maturity date. By itself, without a demand, it does not place the debtor in legal default.
    • Legal Delay (Default / Mora) arises only when: (1) the obligation is demandable and liquidated; (2) the debtor delays performance; and (3) the creditor makes a judicial or extrajudicial demand, and the debtor fails to comply despite receipt of demand (unless demand is expressly waived in the contract under Art. 1169[1]).

3. Use Clear, Plain Language and Avoid Unnecessary Legal Expressions

A contract is written primarily for the client and the contracting parties, not as a contest of esoteric vocabulary.

  • Eliminate Archaic Legalese and Vague Adverbs: Avoid highfalutin Latinisms, redundant couplets, and vague temporal modifiers such as "promptly," "immediately," "forthwith," or "as soon as possible."
  • Use Measurable Benchmarks: Instead of vague adverbs, specify exact calendar or business days anchored to a verifiable trigger event.
Professor's Illustration: Vague vs. Definite Payment Clauses
Compare two drafting approaches in a service agreement:
  • Defective/Vague Clause: "The Client shall pay the Consultant promptly." This is dangerous because "promptly" is subjective; the Consultant cannot easily establish when the Client's obligation has matured for purposes of making a formal demand.
  • Proper/Definite Clause: "The Client shall pay the amount of PhP 50,000.00 within five (5) calendar days after receiving the completed report and billing invoice." This establishes an unmistakable reckoning point and deadline.

4. Keep Provisions Consistent and Proofread Meticulously

Lawyers frequently commit errors by failing to proofread final drafts before printing and execution.

  • Internal Reconciliation: The drafter must cross-check all dates, monetary amounts (ensuring words match figures), defined terms, party designations, and attached annexes or schedules.
  • Prohibition Against Correction Tape or Fluid ("White-Out / Snowpake"): Unlike the typewriter and carbon-paper era where typists used correction fluid (Snowpake or White-out), modern lawyers must never use correction tape or correction fluid on final contracts. Physical erasures or tape over material terms create a presumption of unauthorized alteration or spoliation, which can render the instrument suspect in court or unacceptable to registries (such as the Registry of Deeds or SEC). If a typographical error is discovered before signing, the entire page or document must be reprinted.

5. Anticipate Problems ("Preventive Lawyering")

Contract drafting is an exercise in preventive lawyering—designing the agreement to prevent litigation before it arises, or to ensure clear remedies if litigation becomes unavoidable. The drafter must think several steps ahead and incorporate protective clauses addressing:

  1. Default and Interest: What happens if the conditional buyer or debtor fails to pay on time? Include penalty clauses and stipulated interest rates.
  2. Warranties vs. "As-Is, Where-Is" Clauses: If the object sold is brand new, include express warranties against hidden or mechanical defects (Article 1561, Civil Code). Conversely, if the object is a secondhand motor vehicle or used equipment, protect the seller by stipulating that the sale is on an "as-is, where-is" basis, waiving implied warranties as to physical condition.
  3. Breach, Termination, and Remedies: In a Contract of Lease, what if the tenant absconds in the middle of the night without paying rent? Specify the grounds for pre-termination, forfeiture of deposits, and extrajudicial remedies.
  4. Fairness and Mutuality: While a lawyer must protect their client's interests, a contract should not be unconscionably one-sided. Under Article 1308 of the Civil Code, the contract must bind both contracting parties; its validity or compliance cannot be left to the will of one of them. A balanced, fair contract is more likely to be respected by the parties and upheld by the courts.

6. Follow the Law (Limits on Contractual Autonomy)

Under Article 1306 of the Civil Code, the contracting parties may establish such stipulations, clauses, terms, and conditions as they may deem convenient, provided they are not contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order, or public policy. Under Article 1409(1), contracts whose cause, object, or purpose is contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order, or public policy are inexistent and void from the beginning.

Professor's Illustration: Void Agreements (Marital Separation & Criminal Contracts)
  1. Contract of Marital Separation: Suppose an estranged husband and wife visit your law office requesting a notarized "Agreement to Separate Until Death Do Us Part," stipulating that they are mutually dissolving their marriage and allowing each other to remarry or cohabit with third persons. Under Article 2035 of the Civil Code and the Family Code, marriage is an inviolable social institution; any extrajudicial contract for personal separation or dissolution of marriage is void ab initio for being contrary to law and public policy.
  2. Contract to Commit a Crime: A client asks you to draft a service contract obligating another party to assassinate a rival in exchange for a stipulated fee. Such an agreement has an illicit object and cause, rendering it utterly void and exposing the parties and the lawyer to criminal prosecution and disbarment. (As noted in public controversies involving anomalous flood control projects, perpetrators of illegal schemes avoid leaving a written "paper trail," whereas unwitting or careless signatories who place their names on irregular documents become the primary "fall guys" when investigators trace documentary liability.)

III. ANATOMY OF A STANDARD CONTRACT AND FORMALITIES OF EXECUTION

To draft systematically, a lawyer must master the standard architecture of a Philippine legal instrument (as illustrated in standard formbooks such as Suarez's Legal Forms, pp. 122 et seq.):

  1. Title or Caption: Placed at the top center in bold capital letters identifying the nature of the instrument (e.g., DEED OF ABSOLUTE SALE OF MOTOR VEHICLE, DEED OF SALE OF UNREGISTERED LAND, DEED OF CONDITIONAL SALE, CONTRACT OF LEASE, or MEMORANDUM OF AGREEMENT).
  2. Announcement / Opening Words: Traditional introductory phrase such as "KNOW ALL MEN BY THESE PRESENTS:" (or modern gender-neutral equivalents).
  3. Exordium / Identification of Parties and Designations: States the names, capacities, civil status, citizenship, and addresses of the parties, together with their defined shorthand designations used throughout the body:
    • Contract of Sale: VENDOR and VENDEE (or SELLER and BUYER).
    • Contract of Lease: LESSOR and LESSEE.
    • Contract of Loan: CREDITOR / LENDER and DEBTOR / BORROWER.
    • Deed of Real Estate or Chattel Mortgage: MORTGAGOR and MORTGAGEE.
    • Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) / Understanding (MOU): Where nominate civil law labels do not neatly apply, parties may be designated by their organizational acronyms or simply as "FIRST PARTY", "SECOND PARTY", and (in a tripartite agreement) "THIRD PARTY".
  4. Recitals (Whereas Clauses) / Subject and Purpose: Sets forth the factual background, ownership of the subject property, and the mutual intent or purpose of entering into the contract.
  5. Consideration, Price, and Payment Terms: Specifies the financial obligation—whether the purchase price in a sale, monthly or annual rental in a lease, or premium in an insurance contract—along with the schedule and mode of payment.
  6. Operative Covenants / Obligations and Duties: Numbered paragraphs detailing the specific rights, duties, warranties, and restrictions binding each party.
  7. Duration / Term of the Contract: Essential in time-bound agreements such as a Contract of Lease (e.g., one year) or a Fixed-Term / Contractual Employment Agreement (e.g., three months, six months, or one year), stating the exact commencement and expiration dates.
  8. Breach and Remedies Clause (Catch-All Provision): Specifies specific penalties for default and typically includes a general catch-all stipulation: "In the event of breach by either party of any of the terms and conditions herein stipulated, the aggrieved or innocent party may avail of any and all remedies provided under existing laws."
  9. Testimonium Clause: The concluding paragraph preceding the signatures, stating the place and date of execution (e.g., "IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the parties have hereunto affixed their signatures this ___ day of __________, 20__ at the City of San Fernando, La Union, Philippines.").
  10. Signature Block and Instrumental Witnesses: Contains the printed names of the parties (with their designations underneath) and lines for at least two instrumental witnesses under the phrase "SIGNED IN THE PRESENCE OF:".
  11. Notarial Certificate (Acknowledgment or Jurat): Complies with the 2004 Rules on Notarial Practice, transforming the bilateral contract (via an Acknowledgment) into a public document.

IV. STATUTORY RULES ON FORM, NOTARIZATION, AND REGISTRATION

A. General Rule on Form vs. Statutory Exceptions

Under Article 1356 of the Civil Code, contracts shall be obligatory, in whatever form they may have been entered into, provided all the essential requisites for their validity are present. However, when the law requires that a contract be in some form in order that it may be valid or enforceable, or that a contract be proved in a certain way, that requirement is absolute and indispensable.

1. Form Required for Enforceability: The Statute of Frauds

Under Article 1403(2) of the Civil Code (Statute of Frauds), certain executory agreements must be in writing (or in some note or memorandum subscribed by the party charged or by his agent) to be enforceable by action in court. These include:

  • An agreement that by its terms is not to be performed within a year from the making thereof;
  • A special promise to answer for the debt, default, or miscarriage of another (guaranty);
  • An agreement made in consideration of marriage, other than a mutual promise to marry;
  • An agreement for the sale of goods, chattels, or things in action at a price not less than PhP 500.00;
  • An agreement for the leasing for a longer period than one year, or for the sale of real property or of an interest therein; and
  • A representation as to the credit of a third person.

Crucial Distinction (Validity vs. Enforceability): An oral contract of sale of land covered by the Statute of Frauds is valid between the consenting parties because all essential elements (COC) are present; however, if it remains purely executory and unwritten, it is unenforceable—meaning neither party can sue in court to compel performance over the timely objection of the other.

2. Form Required for Convenience, Efficacy Against Third Persons, or Specific Remedies

Under Article 1358 of the Civil Code, acts and contracts which have for their object the creation, transmission, modification, or extinguishment of real rights over immovable property (such as a Deed of Sale of Land) must appear in a public document (a notarized instrument).

  • Sale of Real Property: Even if a Deed of Sale of Land is only in a private (unnotarized) writing, or even oral (if partially executed), the sale itself is valid between the buyer and seller. However, without a notarized public instrument, the buyer cannot register the sale with the Registry of Deeds to bind third parties. Under Article 1357, the buyer can compel the seller to execute the necessary public instrument once the contract is perfected and enforceable.
  • Real Estate Mortgage (Art. 2125, Civil Code): For a Real Estate Mortgage to be validly constituted and registered so that the mortgagee can legally foreclose the mortgage upon default, it is indispensable that the document in which it appears be in a public instrument and recorded in the Registry of Property. If the mortgage is not notarized and registered, the creditor cannot avail of foreclosure against third persons, although the principal loan and the personal undertaking between the parties remain binding.

B. Legal Effect of Notarization and Registration

  1. Unnotarized Contracts Are Not Automatically Void: Notarization is not a general requisite for the validity of ordinary contracts. An unnotarized written contract remains a private document that is fully valid, binding, and enforceable between the signatory parties.
  2. Function of Notarization: Notarization converts a private document into a public document, rendering it admissible in evidence in court without the need for further proof of its due execution and authenticity (Rule 132, Section 19 & 30, Revised Rules on Evidence), and making it eligible for registration in public registries.
  3. Notarization Cannot Cure an Illegal Contract: If the underlying contract is void for being contrary to law, morals, or public policy (e.g., an illegal sale of public domain or a contract with an illicit cause), notarizing it does not validate or cure the defect. Furthermore, the Notary Public who knowingly notarizes an unlawful or void instrument faces administrative sanctions, revocation of notarial commission, and suspension or disbarment under the Code of Professional Responsibility and Accountability (CPRA).
  4. Registration as a Separate Post-Execution Step: Drafting and notarizing the contract is only the first phase. To bind the whole world and effect the official transfer of ownership or encumbrance, the notarized instrument must undergo registration with the appropriate government agency:
    • Motor Vehicles: Registered with the Land Transportation Office (LTO).
    • Titled Real Property: Registered with the Registry of Deeds (RD) of the city or province where the land is situated.
    • Untitled / Unregistered Real Property: Recorded with the Municipal or Provincial Assessor's Office (and Register of Deeds under Act No. 3344 / P.D. 1529 for unregistered lands) for the issuance of a new Tax Declaration.

V. AGENCY AND POWERS OF ATTORNEY (GPA VS. SPA)

A. Nature of Agency in Legal Drafting

Under Article 1868 of the Civil Code, by the contract of agency a person binds himself to render some service or to do something in representation or on behalf of another, with the consent or authority of the latter. The written instrument evidencing this authority is a Power of Attorney, wherein the person granting authority is the Principal and the person authorized to act is the Attorney-in-Fact (Agent).

B. General Power of Attorney (GPA) vs. Special Power of Attorney (SPA)

When drafting a Power of Attorney or reviewing an agent's authority before preparing a transaction deed, the lawyer must strictly distinguish between acts of administration and acts of strict dominion:

  • General Power of Attorney (GPA) — Article 1877, Civil Code: An agency couched in general terms comprises only acts of administration, even if the principal should state that he withholds no power or that the agent may execute such acts as he may consider appropriate, or even though the agency should authorize a general and unlimited management.
  • Special Power of Attorney (SPA) — Article 1878, Civil Code: Special powers of attorney are necessary whenever the act to be performed is an act of strict dominion or ownership (such as selling, mortgaging, leasing real property for more than one year, compromising, novating, or waiving an obligation gratuitously).
Feature General Power of Attorney (GPA) Special Power of Attorney (SPA)
Statutory Basis Article 1877, Civil Code Article 1878, Civil Code

Drafting Style Broad, general management clauses without specifying the disposition of specific properties. Explicitly enumerates the exact transaction and specifically describes the property or right involved.

C. Key Doctrinal Rules in Drafting and Interpreting Powers of Attorney

  1. Substance Over Form (Caption Does Not Control): In Philippine jurisprudence, the title or caption of the document is not controlling. To determine whether an instrument is a GPA or an SPA, the lawyer and the court must read the actual powers granted in the body of the text. Even if a document is titled "General Power of Attorney," if its body expressly and specifically authorizes the agent "to sell, transfer, and convey my parcel of land covered by TCT No. 12345," it is legally operative as a Special Power of Attorney with respect to that specific sale.
  2. Sale of Land by an Agent Requires Written Authority (Art. 1874, Civil Code): When a sale of a piece of land or any interest therein is through an agent, the authority of the agent shall be in writing; otherwise, the sale shall be void. Verbal authority to sell real property produces zero legal effect, and the resulting Deed of Sale cannot be ratified merely by the buyer's good faith.
  3. Power to Sell Does Not Include Power to Mortgage, and Vice Versa (Art. 1879, Civil Code): A special power to sell excludes the power to mortgage; and a special power to mortgage does not include the power to sell, unless both powers are expressly and separately stipulated in the SPA. The drafter must carefully interview the client to ascertain the exact commercial objective and explicitly enumerate every intended power.
Professor's Illustration: Checking the SPA in a Real Estate Purchase
Suppose you are buying a parcel of land for investment, and the person negotiating with you is the owner's brother, who claims, "My sister is abroad and verbally told me over the phone to sell this lot for her." If you pay him and sign a Deed of Sale without requiring a written Special Power of Attorney signed by the sister (and apostilled/consularized if executed abroad), the sale is void ab initio under Article 1874. Similarly, if the brother presents a written SPA authorizing him only "to sell" the land, he cannot use that SPA to obtain a bank loan and mortgage the property, unless the power to mortgage is also expressly written in the instrument (Art. 1879).

VI. CORPORATE INSTRUMENTS: ARTICLES OF INCORPORATION AND THE ONE PERSON CORPORATION (OPC)

A. Drafting the Articles of Incorporation under the Revised Corporation Code

When clients wish to form a corporation, the foundational charter to be drafted and filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is the Articles of Incorporation (AOI). While the SEC provides downloadable electronic templates and the statutory form is set forth in Sections 13 and 14 of Republic Act No. 11232 (Revised Corporation Code of the Philippines or RCC), the lawyer's primary role is regulatory and legal compliance.

B. Mandatory Contents and Compliance Checklists in the AOI

  1. Corporate Name: Must include the word "Corporation" or "Incorporated" (or "Corp." / "Inc."), or "OPC" for a One Person Corporation, and must be distinguishable from any existing or reserved corporate name in the SEC database (Section 17, RCC).
  2. Purpose Clause (Primary and Secondary Purposes): States the specific lawful business activity for which the corporation is organized (e.g., developing and operating a memorial park or funeral chapel business).
    • Drafting Rule: Match the stated purposes strictly to the actual intended business operations. A corporation cannot lawfully engage in acts outside its express or implied corporate purposes (ultra vires doctrine under Section 44, RCC). Moreover, the SEC strictly scrutinizes purpose clauses to prevent entities from using a generic trading charter as a front to solicit unauthorized public investments or Ponzi scams.
  3. Principal Office Address: Must specify the exact street address, building, barangay, city or municipality, and province within the Philippines (not merely "Metro Manila" or a general region), as this determines the venue of actions by or against the corporation and the situs for SEC notices.
  4. Corporate Term (Perpetual Existence):
    • Old Corporation Code Rule (B.P. Blg. 68): Corporations were limited to a maximum term of fifty (50) years at any one time.
    • Revised Corporation Code Rule (Section 11, R.A. 11232): A corporation now has perpetual existence by default, unless its Articles of Incorporation provide otherwise. If the incorporators wish to limit the corporate lifespan (e.g., to 25 or 50 years for a specific project), the drafter must expressly state that fixed term; otherwise, the AOI simply declares that the corporation shall have perpetual existence.
  5. Incorporators and Directors (Nationality and Qualification Compliance): Lists the names, nationalities, and residences of the incorporators (up to 15 natural persons, partnerships, associations, or corporations) and initial directors/trustees.
    • Foreign Equity and Constitutional Restrictions: The corporate lawyer acts as a compliance gatekeeper. Before listing foreign nationals as incorporators, directors, or subscribers, the drafter must check the 1987 Constitution and the Foreign Investments Act (R.A. 7042, as amended) / Regular Foreign Investment Negative List (FINL). For instance, mass media requires 100% Filipino ownership, while land-owning corporations or public utilities (subject to recent statutory amendments for non-public utilities) traditionally require at least 60% Filipino and maximum 40% foreign equity.
  6. Capital Stock, Subscriptions, and Contributions: In a stock corporation, the AOI must state the Authorized Capital Stock (ACS) in Philippine Pesos, the number of shares into which it is divided, the par value per share (or if no-par value), the names, nationalities, and residences of the subscribers, and the exact number of shares and monetary amount subscribed and paid-in by each.
    • Mathematical Reconciliation: Although Section 12 of the RCC removed the general minimum capital stock requirement (except as required by special laws or for specific industries/foreign equity rules, and during capital increases where the 25% subscribed and 25% paid-up rule strictly applies under Sec. 37), traditional SEC table formatting still requires exact mathematical reconciliation among Authorized, Subscribed, and Paid-up Capital figures. A single arithmetic discrepancy will cause the SEC examiner to reject the filing.
  7. Signatures, Treasurer's Certification, and Undertakings: Incorporators and the Corporate Treasurer must sign the AOI, including the statutory undertaking to change the corporate name if another person or entity has acquired a prior right to it.
Professor's Illustration: Compliance Checking in Corporate Drafting
Suppose a group of clients—two Filipinos and three foreign investors—hires you to draft Articles of Incorporation to acquire raw land in Pangasinan and develop a memorial park and funeral chapel business. Because developing a memorial park involves acquiring ownership of private land, the corporation is subject to the constitutional restriction requiring at least 60% Filipino ownership of the outstanding capital stock. If you blindly draft the AOI giving the three foreigners 70% of the subscribed shares without checking nationality laws, the SEC will reject the incorporation or the corporation will be barred from registering land titles.

C. The One Person Corporation (OPC) vs. Sole Proprietorship

A major innovation introduced by the Revised Corporation Code (Title XIII, Chapter III, Sections 115–132 of R.A. 11232) is the One Person Corporation (OPC)—a corporation with a single stockholder (who must be a natural person, trust, or estate). When advising an individual entrepreneur, the lawyer must explain the critical legal distinction between registering a Sole Proprietorship with the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) and incorporating an OPC with the SEC:

VII. ARTICLES OF PARTNERSHIP AND JOINT VENTURE ARRANGEMENTS

A. Nature of Partnership and Choosing the Right Business Vehicle

Under Article 1767 of the Civil Code, by the contract of partnership two or more persons bind themselves to contribute money, property, or industry to a common fund, with the intention of dividing the profits among themselves.

  • Intimate Relationship (Delectus Personae) vs. Corporation: Unlike a corporation where ownership is impersonal and shares of stock are freely transferable among numerous unknown investors, a partnership is built on mutual trust and confidence (delectus personae).
  • Practical Advisory Rule: When a small group of two to three close friends approaches a lawyer to pool funds for a small venture (such as a specialty ube or matcha café), a Partnership is appropriate. However, if there are four, five, or more investors, the lawyer should generally advise forming a Corporation instead, because the withdrawal, death, or insolvency of any single partner easily triggers the dissolution of a partnership (Article 1830, Civil Code).

B. Classifications of Partners and Their Drafting Implications

When drafting the Articles of Partnership, the lawyer must accurately classify each partner under the Civil Code, as their classification dictates their contributions, management rights, and liability to creditors:

Classification of Partner Definition & Role in the Partnership Key Legal & Drafting Consequence
Capitalist Partner Contributes money or property (cash or in-kind assets with monetary value, such as a building or equipment) to the common fund. Shares in both profits and losses; cannot engage in the same kind of business as the partnership for his own account without consent (Art. 1808).
Industrial Partner Contributes only his industry, skill, labor, or personal talent (e.g., culinary expertise, salesmanship, or management skill), without contributing cash or property. Exempt from partnership losses inter se (Art. 1797); cannot engage in any other business for himself unless expressly permitted (Art. 1789).
General Partner Liability for partnership obligations extends to his separate personal property (pro rata and subsidiarily) after partnership assets are exhausted (Art. 1816). All partners in a General Partnership are general partners; at least one partner in a Limited Partnership must be a general partner.
Limited Partner Exists only in a Limited Partnership (Art. 1843); liability is limited strictly to the amount of his capital contribution. Cannot participate in management or contribute mere industry; surname cannot appear in the partnership name; requires strict statutory filing.
Managing Partner Designated in the Articles of Partnership (or subsequently) to execute all acts of administration and sign on behalf of the firm (Art. 1800). If appointed in the Articles of Partnership, his power is irrevocable without just or lawful cause and requires the vote of controlling interest to revoke.
Nominal / Silent / Dormant Nominal: Partner by name/estoppel only; Silent: Does not participate in management though known as a partner; Dormant: Both secret and silent. Drafting must specify who holds actual management and bank signing authority versus passive profit-sharing rights.

C. Essential Clauses in a Contract of Partnership

  1. Partnership Name and Purpose: States the firm name (adding "Ltd." if it is a limited partnership) and the specific business purpose.
  2. Extent and Nature of Contributions: Explicitly itemizes what each partner contributes—whether cash, property in kind (with its appraised monetary value), or industry/services.
  3. Management and Signing Authority: Identifies the Managing Partner(s) and specifies whether two or more managers must act jointly or may act separately in signing checks, contracts, and official documents.
  4. Profit and Loss Sharing Agreement: The primary motive of a partnership is to earn profit, not loss, though both contingencies must be governed by the contract:
    • Under Article 1797 of the Civil Code, losses and profits are distributed in conformity with the agreement. If only the share of each partner in the profits has been agreed upon, the share of each in the losses shall be in the same proportion. In the absence of stipulation, the share of each partner in the profits and losses shall be in proportion to what he may have contributed, while the industrial partner receives such share as may be just and equitable (and is not liable for losses among the partners).
    • Prohibition Against Pactum Leonina (Art. 1799): A stipulation which excludes one or more partners from any share in the profits or losses is void (except as to an industrial partner regarding losses).
  5. Record-Keeping, Withdrawal of Contributions, Dissolution, and Liquidation: Specifies accounting periods, audit rights, rules on partner withdrawal, and the order of distribution of assets upon dissolution and winding up under the Civil Code.

D. Mandatory Formalities and SEC Registration Rules for Partnerships

While Article 1771 of the Civil Code states the general rule that a partnership may be constituted in any form, the drafter must strictly enforce two statutory exceptions:

  1. Capital Contribution of PhP 3,000.00 or More (Article 1772, Civil Code): Every contract of partnership having a capital of Three Thousand Pesos (PhP 3,000.00) or more, in money or property, shall appear in a public instrument, which must be recorded in the Office of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).
    • Practical Note & Legal Effect: Because the 1950 Civil Code threshold of PhP 3,000.00 has not been amended to account for inflation, virtually all modern partnerships exceed PhP 3,000.00 in capital and should be notarized and registered with the SEC. However, under the second paragraph of Article 1772 in relation to Article 1768, failure to comply with the PhP 3,000.00 public instrument and SEC registration requirement does not prevent the partnership from acquiring juridical personality and does not affect the liability of the partnership and its members to third persons.
  2. Contribution of Immovable Property or Real Rights (Articles 1771 & 1773, Civil Code): Whenever immovable property (e.g., land, a building, or a condominium unit) or real rights are contributed to the partnership, two absolute requirements must concur regardless of the value of the property:
    • (a) The contract of partnership must be in a public instrument (notarized); and
    • (b) An inventory of said immovable property, signed by the parties, must be attached to the public instrument.
    • Fatal Consequence of Non-Compliance: Under Article 1773, if the contract is not in a public instrument or if the signed inventory of the immovable property is not attached, the contract of partnership is VOID and acquires no juridical personality whatsoever.
  3. Internal Agreements Limiting Liability Do Not Bind Creditors (Article 1817, Civil Code): Any stipulation among the partners exempting a partner (even an industrial partner) from liability beyond their capital contribution is valid only among the partners themselves (inter se), and cannot bind or prejudice third-party creditors, who may still hold all general partners subsidiarily and pro rata liable with their personal assets.

VIII. DRAFTING CONTRACTS OF LEASE (REAL AND PERSONAL PROPERTY)

Under Article 1643 of the Civil Code, in the lease of things, one of the parties (Lessor) binds himself to give to another (Lessee) the enjoyment or use of a thing for a price certain, and for a period which may be definite or indefinite. Because lease disputes are ubiquitous in residential boarding houses, commercial offices, and industrial land leases, the drafter must include specific protective clauses for both Lessor and Lessee:

Lease Clause Protection for the Lessor (Landlord) Protection for the Lessee (Tenant)
1. Leased Premises Description Prevents the tenant from encroaching on unleased common areas or adjoining lots. Prevents the landlord from restricting access to agreed spaces or overcharging for undefined areas.
2. Permitted Use & Restrictions Prohibits conversion of residential units into commercial hubs, warehouses, or illicit drug dens. Guarantees peaceful and adequate enjoyment (peaceful possession) of the premises for the agreed purpose.
3. Advance Rent & Security Deposit Security deposit answers for unpaid utility bills (electricity, water, Wi-Fi) and physical damages upon vacating; cannot be applied by the tenant as monthly rent. Entitles the tenant to a full refund of the remaining security deposit within a fixed period after vacating and settling final utility bills.
4. Repairs and Maintenance Shifts minor, ordinary wear-and-tear repairs (e.g., replacing busted lightbulbs or faucet washers) to the Lessee. Obligates the Lessor to shoulder major or structural repairs (e.g., replacing a roof blown away by a typhoon) under Art. 1654(2).
5. Sublease and Assignment Expressly prohibits subleasing (e.g., Airbnb arbitrage) or assignment of lease without the Lessor's prior written consent. Clarifies whether the Lessee is permitted to sublease portions of the property to recoup rent.
6. Return of Premises Requires return of the premises in "good and tenantable condition," allowing deduction for trash or intentional damage. Excludes liability for ordinary wear and tear or fortuitous events (e.g., earthquake or typhoon damage without tenant fault).

Key Statutory and Practical Drafting Rules in Leases

  1. Specificity of Leased Premises: Never describe the premises vaguely as "a property located in Barangay Lingsat, San Fernando City, La Union." Specify the exact Unit/Door Number, Floor, Building Name, Street Address, and approximate floor area in square meters.
  2. Term of Lease and Long-Term Commercial Land Leases: Residential and office leases typically run for six (6) months to one (1) year, subject to renewal upon mutual written agreement (where rental rates may be renegotiated). In contrast, commercial leases of vacant strategic land—such as for gasoline stations—frequently run for five (5) to ten (10) years or more, with stipulations allowing the Lessee to construct permanent or semi-permanent structures and obligating the Lessee to remove them and restore the land upon termination.
  3. Sublease vs. Assignment of Lease (Articles 1649 & 1650, Civil Code):
    • Assignment of Lease (Art. 1649): The lessee cannot assign the lease without the consent of the lessor, unless there is a stipulation to the contrary.
    • Sublease (Art. 1650): When in the contract of lease of things there is no express prohibition, the lessee may sublease the thing leased, in whole or in part, without prejudice to his responsibility for the performance of the contract toward the lessor. Therefore, if a landlord wants to prevent a tenant from renting a house, renovating it, and subleasing it on Airbnb, the lawyer must insert an express prohibition against subleasing in the contract.
Professor's Illustration: Typhoon Roof Damage, Security Deposits, and Live-Selling Breaches
Suppose Student X rents a residential apartment in San Fernando City under a one-year Contract of Lease with "one month advance, one month security deposit." During Typhoon Emong, the apartment's roof is blown off. Because replacing a roof is a major structural repair necessary to keep the premises fit for habitation (Article 1654[2], Civil Code), the Lessor must shoulder the cost, not the tenant. However, if X converts the strictly residential apartment into a noisy commercial warehouse for online "live-selling" ("Mine!" auctions) or abandons the unit leaving unpaid electricity, water, and PLDT/Converge Wi-Fi bills, the Lessor may terminate the lease for breach of permitted use and forfeit the security deposit to cover the unpaid utility bills.

IX. SERVICE CONTRACTS AND LEGAL RETAINERSHIP AGREEMENTS

A. Essential Elements of Service and Consultancy Agreements

A Service Contract governs an obligation to do—where an independent contractor, service provider, or professional consultant (e.g., construction contractor, janitorial/cleaning agency, laundry service, medical consultant, or retained legal counsel) binds himself to render specific services in exchange for compensation. To prevent scope creep and billing disputes, the drafter must clearly define:

  1. Scope of Work and Deliverables: Explicitly enumerate what services are included and excluded (e.g., whether a legal consultant's monthly retainer includes contract drafting and notarization, or only verbal legal advice). Specify tangible deliverables (e.g., monthly accomplishment reports, number of barangay medical missions, or specific engineering plans) and deadlines.
  2. Professional Fees and Tiered Appearance Rates (Legal Retainership): In a Legal Retainership Agreement, the lawyer must clearly state the monthly retainer fee and the separate schedule of appearance fees and pleading fees. Appearance fees should be graduated upward according to the hierarchy of courts—charging a base rate for appearances before the Municipal Trial Court (MTC), a higher rate before the Regional Trial Court (RTC), and premium rates for oral arguments or appearances before the Court of Appeals or Supreme Court Justices.
  3. Client Responsibilities, Revisions, and Defect Correction: State the client's obligation to pay fees on time and provide necessary documents. In construction, engineering, or janitorial contracts, specify whether re-work, plan changes, or correction of defects (e.g., re-cleaning a dirty facility or revising architectural designs) will incur additional charges or must be performed free of charge as warranty rectification.
  4. Confidentiality and Attorney-Client Privilege: Every legal retainership and consultancy contract must contain a strict Confidentiality Clause. For lawyers, the Attorney-Client Privilege prohibits disclosing client confidences even to the lawyer's own spouse. Under the Code of Professional Responsibility and Accountability (CPRA), this duty of confidentiality extends to the lawyer's secretary, paralegals, and office staff, who must be bound to keep all office matters strictly within the four walls of the law firm.

B. Independent Contractor Clause vs. Labor Law Reality

Service contracts routinely include a stipulation stating: "Nothing herein shall be construed as creating an employer-employee relationship between the Client and the Contractor."

  • Substance Over Form in Labor Law: Calling someone an "independent contractor" in a written contract does not conclusively settle their legal status. Under Philippine Labor Law, the existence of an employer-employee relationship is determined by the Four-Fold Test (particularly the Control Test—whether the principal controls not only the end result but also the means and methods by which the work is accomplished) and the actual factual relationship of the parties. (By way of policy critique, while private corporations are strictly penalized for illegal labor-only contracting or endo, government agencies widely utilize Contract of Service [COS] templates that classify long-term personnel as independent contractors lacking security of tenure and statutory benefits.)
Professor's Illustration: Drafting a Legal Retainership Contract for a School
Suppose you are hired as the Legal Consultant of Saint Louis College for a renewable one-year term. If your Retainership Contract merely states "The Lawyer shall render legal services for PhP 25,000.00 a month," the client might demand that you handle twenty litigation cases, draft fifty contracts, and appear in court from MTC up to the Supreme Court without paying a single centavo more. A properly drafted Retainership Contract defines the Scope of Work covered by the monthly retainer (e.g., up to 10 hours of consultation and 3 simple contracts per month) and sets a separate tiered fee schedule for court appearances, major litigation, and out-of-town travel.

Must state the settlement amount, mutual concessions, a Release, Waiver, and Quitclaim clause, and an agreement to withdraw or move for the dismissal of the pending case.

Deep-Dive: Loans and Compromise Agreements

  1. The Strict Written Interest Rule in Loans (Article 1956, Civil Code): When drafting a Loan Agreement or Promissory Note for a lender, the lawyer must never forget to put the interest stipulation in writing. Under Article 1956, even if the borrower verbally agreed to pay monthly interest, the lender cannot collect any compensatory interest if it is not expressly reduced to writing. Furthermore, if the parties stipulate in writing that interest is payable without specifying the exact rate (or as damages upon default after demand), the prevailing legal interest rate of six percent (6%) per annum applies.
  2. Compromise Agreements in Civil and Criminal Cases:
    • Civil Disputes: Parties before the court or assisted by the Integrated Bar of the Philippines (IBP) Legal Aid office execute a Compromise Agreement to settle boundary disputes, collection suits, or damages claims without undergoing protracted trial.
    • Civil Liability in Criminal Cases (Article 2034, Civil Code): There may be a compromise upon the civil liability arising from an offense, although such compromise shall not automatically extinguish the public action for the imposition of the legal penalty. In practice—such as in theft cases where stolen items are restituted in cash, or in Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Homicide where the bereaved family executes a Compromise Agreement and Affidavit of Desistance upon receiving financial compensation—victims often opt for settlement out of practical survival needs for the orphans or to avoid the psychological trauma of reliving the tragedy during trial. The drafter must clearly specify the claims released and the undertaking to manifest the settlement before the Prosecutor or the Court.
Professor's Illustration: Legal Compromise Agreement vs. Archaic "Trial by Ordeal"
In a tragic real-life incident reported in the news, two elderly claimants disputing ownership over a parcel of land resorted to an archaic indigenous "trial by ordeal" (bultong / water submersion test), agreeing that whoever stayed submerged underwater the longest would be declared the rightful owner of the land. Tragically, neither claimant surfaced alive—both drowned, leaving neither with the land. Had the parties consulted a lawyer or barangay/IBP mediator to execute a civil Compromise Agreement under Article 2028 of the Civil Code—dividing the land equitably or compensating one party via reciprocal concessions—both lives would have been saved and ownership legally settled.
