Chapter Sixteen — Strict Liability

I. The Big Picture: What is Strict Liability?

Ordinary Negligence (Art. 2176) → YOU ARE HERE: Ch. 16, Part I — Concept of Strict Liability → Part II: History of Rules
STATUTORY ADDRESS Art. 2176 (ordinary quasi-delict) contrasted with Art. 2183 & Art. 2193, New Civil Code.
CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Shifts the central question from “Was the defendant careless?” to “Did the event occur under circumstances where the law imposes liability without proof of negligence?”
WHY THE LAW CARES Certain risks (animals, objects falling from residences, nuisances) are placed on the controller even without proven fault, though statutory defenses still apply.

Imagine these three situations:

  1. Your neighbor’s dog escapes and bites a pedestrian, even though your neighbor was careful in keeping it.
  2. A heavy object falls from an apartment window and injures someone walking below.
  3. A factory creates excessive noise and smoke that substantially interfere with neighboring households, even though the factory operates with reasonable care.

In ordinary quasi-delicts, we usually ask: Was the defendant negligent?

In strict liability, the question changes: Did the event occur under circumstances in which the law imposes liability even without proof of negligence?

That distinction is the foundation of this chapter.

A. Definition

Strict liability is liability imposed by law independently of fault, negligence, or intent, once the legally required circumstances are established.

The chapter adopts the definition from Black’s Law Dictionary, emphasizing that liability may arise irrespective of the care exercised or the actor’s state of mind.

The fundamental distinction:

Ordinary quasi-delict Strict liability
The injured person generally must establish negligence or fault. The injured person does not have to establish negligence, but must prove the statutory basis and its requisites.
Remember: Strict liability does not mean automatic liability in every situation. The applicable law still determines causation, coverage, and possible defenses.

B. Strict liability versus absolute liability

Our textbook identifies two possible formulations:

Strict liability Absolute liability
Liability without proof of negligence, subject to the governing law’s limits and defenses. A more stringent form of liability, potentially allowing very limited defenses.
Article 2183 expressly recognizes force majeure and the victim’s fault. The textbook describes Article 2193 as imposing an especially strict form of liability.

Remember: The phrase strict liability does not automatically eliminate all defenses. We must read the specific statutory provision.

A. Roman-law influence

The author connects Articles 22, 2183, and 2193 with principles rooted in Roman law.

One important historical concept was the quasi-delict, which imposed liability for certain harmful events despite the absence of an ordinary contractual relationship.

The author also explains that the Roman law of quasi-delicts did not always require fault in the modern sense.

B. American-law influence

The chapter attributes much of Philippine nuisance law’s development to American law.

It emphasizes that legal responsibility may arise from maintaining conditions that interfere with the rights, safety, and comfort of others.

This historical discussion explains why our Civil Code contains both fault-based quasi-delict provisions and provisions under which fault need not be established.

III. Animals — Article 2183

Textbook §2, pp. 876–878

Part II: History of Rules → YOU ARE HERE: Ch. 16, Part III — Animals (Art. 2183) → Part IV: Falling Objects (Art. 2193)
STATUTORY ADDRESS Article 2183, New Civil Code.
CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Applies strict liability directly to the possessor or user of an animal that causes injury, even if it escaped despite care.
WHY THE LAW CARES The person who controls and benefits from keeping or using an animal bears the risk of its escape or harm, subject only to force majeure or the victim’s own fault.

A. The governing rule

Article 2183, Civil Code:

The possessor of an animal or whoever may make use of the same is responsible for the damage which it may cause, although it may escape or be lost. This responsibility shall cease only in case the damage should come from force majeure or from the fault of the person who has suffered damage.

This is one of the clearest provisions on strict liability.

B. Why is the possessor liable even without negligence?

Suppose Pedro owns a dog named Bruno.

Pedro provides Bruno with a proper enclosure, regularly feeds him, and ensures that the gates are locked. Nevertheless, Bruno escapes unexpectedly and bites a pedestrian.

Pedro argues:

“I exercised due diligence. I was not negligent!”

Does this automatically free Pedro from liability?

No.

Under Article 2183, the obligation does not depend on whether Pedro was negligent in caring for the animal.

The provision expressly imposes liability although the animal may escape or be lost.

The law places responsibility on the person possessing or making use of the animal because that person exercises control over it and ordinarily benefits from its possession or use.

C. Requisites of liability

To invoke Article 2183, the following must generally be established:

  1. There is an animal that causes damage.
  2. The defendant is the possessor of the animal or whoever makes use of it.
  3. The animal’s act caused the damage suffered by the claimant.
  4. Neither of the statutory grounds for cessation of responsibility applies.

Notice that negligence is not included as an indispensable element.

D. Who is liable: Owner or possessor?

This distinction is especially important for recitations.

Person Liability under Article 2183
Owner who possesses the animal Liable if the statutory requisites exist.
Non-owner who possesses the animal May be liable even without ownership.
Person making use of the animal May be liable based on actual use or control.
Owner without possession or use Ownership alone does not necessarily establish liability under Article 2183.

Example: Maria owns a dog but temporarily entrusts it to Juan, who keeps and uses the animal on his farm. If it injures someone while under Juan’s control, Article 2183 may apply to Juan despite his not being the owner.

The decisive consideration is the legally relevant possession or use, not simply whose name appears on an ownership record.

E. The only two statutory grounds for cessation of liability

1. Force majeure

Damage caused by an extraordinary event meeting the legal requirements of force majeure, rather than merely the animal escaping despite precautions.

2. Fault of the injured person

For example, a person deliberately provokes a properly confined animal, and that person’s conduct causes the injury.

The textbook discusses Manresa’s explanation that Article 2183 imposes liability even where another person’s fault contributes to the occurrence, unless the applicable statutory exception is established.

Thus, the intervention of a third person does not necessarily exonerate the possessor. The defendant cannot merely point to another person’s negligence and assume Article 2183 no longer applies.

F. Cases discussed in the textbook

1. Vestil v. Intermediate Appellate Court
G.R. No. 74431, November 6, 1989

The textbook discusses a child who died following a dog-bite incident while playing at a residence in Cebu.

The important doctrine is that liability can attach to a possessor of an animal even if that person is not its owner.

2. Milagros Ibarado v. Pelagia Nava
CA-G.R. No. 22887-R, January 8, 1963

The textbook discusses responsibility arising from a dog kept at the premises with the knowledge and consent of a person exercising control over the property.

The case illustrates that the inquiry is not limited to title of ownership; the circumstances showing possession, use, and control must be considered.

G. Quick application

Hypothetical: A dog escapes from a properly fenced compound after an ordinary gust of wind opens a gate. It bites a passerby. The possessor proves that he had regularly inspected the enclosure.

Would the mere proof of reasonable care absolve the possessor?

No. Due diligence by itself is not one of the statutory grounds for cessation of liability under Article 2183. Whether the wind amounted to legally sufficient force majeure requires a separate inquiry.

IV. Falling Objects — Article 2193

Textbook §3, pp. 878–879

Part III: Animals (Art. 2183) → YOU ARE HERE: Ch. 16, Part IV — Falling Objects (Art. 2193) → Part V: Liability of Employers (Art. 1711)
STATUTORY ADDRESS Article 2193, New Civil Code.
CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Shifts from animals (Art. 2183) to objects thrown or falling from inhabited buildings (Art. 2193), where the law omits express force majeure exceptions.
WHY THE LAW CARES Pedestrians cannot trace who inside a multi-unit building dropped an object; making the resident head of family responsible protects public safety below.

A. The governing rule

Article 2193, Civil Code:

The head of a family that lives in a building or a part thereof, is responsible for damages caused by things thrown or falling from the same.

This provision addresses a familiar problem: somebody walking below a building is injured by an object that falls or is thrown from an occupied portion of the building.

B. Simple illustration

A family rents a second-floor apartment.

One afternoon, a visitor accidentally knocks over a heavy flowerpot near the window. The flowerpot falls and strikes a pedestrian.

The pedestrian suffers injuries.

Who may be held liable under Article 2193?

The head of the family living in the relevant part of the building, provided the conditions for the article’s application are established.

The claimant need not prove that this person personally dropped the flowerpot or negligently supervised the visitor.

C. Essential requisites

  1. The defendant is the head of a family living in a building or a part thereof.
  2. A thing was thrown or fell from that building or occupied part.
  3. The falling or thrown thing caused damage.
  4. The defendant is the person whom Article 2193 makes responsible under the circumstances.

The textbook emphasizes that the provision does not require proof of negligence.

D. Does the building owner have to be liable?

Not necessarily.

Suppose a family rents an apartment owned by a landlord who lives elsewhere.

The landlord is not automatically the person liable under Article 2193 merely because the landlord owns the building.

The law specifically identifies the head of the family living in the building or part thereof.

This is different from Article 2183, which concerns the possessor or user of an animal.

E. Case: Dingcong v. Kanaan

72 Phil. 14

The textbook discusses a co-lessee who was held responsible after an object fell from an upper floor and caused damage.

The important lesson is that liability is not necessarily defeated by showing that somebody else actually caused the object to fall.

The textbook also contrasts Article 2193 with Article 2183: Article 2193 contains no express force majeure exemption. The author nevertheless acknowledges an opinion that force majeure may excuse liability in truly extraordinary circumstances.

For recitation, distinguish what the provision expressly says from the author’s discussion of possible exceptional defenses.

V. Liability of Employers — Article 1711

Textbook §4, pp. 879–880

Part IV: Falling Objects (Art. 2193) → YOU ARE HERE: Ch. 16, Part V — Liability of Employers (Art. 1711) → Part VI: Nuisance
STATUTORY ADDRESS Article 1711, New Civil Code; Title II, Book IV of the Labor Code.
CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Connects historical Civil Code no-fault employer compensation (Art. 1711) to the controlling 2022 Supreme Court rule in Oceanmarine Resources v. Nedic.
WHY THE LAW CARES Prevents double recovery while guiding injured workers to choose cleanly between no-fault Employees’ Compensation and fault-based Civil Code damages.

This section requires special attention because the Supreme Court subsequently clarified the law.

A. How the textbook explains Article 1711

Article 1711 originally imposed an obligation on owners of enterprises and other employers to pay compensation for work-related death, injury, or disease, even where the harmful event was purely accidental or due to a fortuitous cause.

The provision also identified circumstances affecting liability, including the employee’s own notorious negligence, voluntary act, drunkenness, or contributory lack of due care.

Example: An employee suffers an accidental injury while performing assigned duties. Under the original Article 1711 framework, compensation could arise even without proof of negligence by the employer.

The textbook discusses differing judicial approaches to whether Article 1711 survived the later enactment of the Labor Code.

B. Important current-law clarification

Controlling Supreme Court doctrine: In Oceanmarine Resources Corporation v. Nedic (G.R. No. 236263, July 19, 2022), the Supreme Court, sitting En Banc, declared Article 1711 impliedly repealed by Title II, Book IV of the Labor Code, subject to the decision’s prospective-application guidelines.

This means that we must not treat Article 1711 as an independently operative basis for new work-injury compensation claims today.

C. What remedies remain available?

Compensation under the Labor Code Damages under the Civil Code
Governed by the employees’ compensation system. Based on applicable provisions governing torts or other actionable civil wrongs.
The statutory system provides compensation without requiring proof of employer negligence. The claimant generally must establish the applicable wrongful act or negligence and its causal connection to the injury.
Governed by statutory benefits and eligibility conditions. Damages are measured under applicable Civil Code rules.

The Court recognized an election between the compensation remedy and a Civil Code damages remedy, subject to its qualifications and exceptions against double recovery.

For exams: If the professor asks, “Is Article 1711 still applicable?”, distinguish the textbook’s historical discussion from the controlling 2022 ruling. The Supreme Court preserved limited reliance on the earlier Candano doctrine for cases filed within the transitional period identified in Oceanmarine.

VI. Nuisance

STATUTORY ADDRESS Arts. 694–707, New Civil Code; P.D. No. 856 Secs. 84–85 (Code on Sanitation); R.A. No. 9514 Secs. 8–9 (Revised Fire Code of 2008).
CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Moves from discrete events (bites, falling pots) to ongoing harmful conditions of property or business that interfere with public rights or neighbors’ enjoyment.
WHY THE LAW CARES Balances property rights against community peace and safety, tightly regulating extrajudicial self-help abatement (Art. 704) so citizens do not recklessly destroy neighbor property.

Nuisance is the longest discussion in this chapter. It is particularly important because the law allows certain nuisances to be removed without first obtaining a judicial order, but only under carefully prescribed conditions.

5.01. Definition of Nuisance

A. Under the New Civil Code

Article 694 defines nuisance as any act, omission, establishment, business, condition of property, or anything else which:

  1. Injures or endangers the health or safety of others;
  2. Annoys or offends the senses;
  3. Shocks, defies or disregards decency or morality;
  4. Obstructs or interferes with the free passage of any public highway or street, or any body of water; or
  5. Hinders or impairs the use of property.

The concept is broad. A nuisance need not be an object; it can arise from a business activity, an omission, or the condition in which property is maintained.

Simple explanation

Suppose you own a house beside a poultry farm. The farm releases foul odors that regularly enter your home and substantially interfere with your family’s ordinary use of the property.

Even if the operator is not shown to have been negligent, the activity may constitute a nuisance if the statutory conditions are satisfied.

The governing principle is that the exercise of one’s property rights must respect the rights of others.

The textbook relates this principle to Article 702 on the reasonable limitations inherent in ownership, including restrictions imposed to prevent injury to others.

B. Under the Code on Sanitation

Presidential Decree No. 856, Section 84 defines nuisance in terms of injury to health, danger to life, offense to the senses, or discomfort to the community.

5.02. Kinds of Nuisance

The textbook classifies nuisance in two important ways.

A. Public nuisance versus private nuisance

Article 695 provides that a public nuisance affects a community, neighborhood, or any considerable number of persons, even though individuals may experience different degrees of harm.

A private nuisance is one not included in that definition.

Public nuisance: An obstruction that interferes with pedestrians’ use of a public sidewalk.

Private nuisance: A neighboring property condition that substantially interferes with one household’s enjoyment of its property.

Public nuisance Private nuisance
Affects a community, neighborhood, considerable number of persons, or a public right. Primarily interferes with a particular person’s private rights.
Harm to individuals need not be equal. The injury is private rather than one affecting the public generally.
Article 699 governs the remedies. Article 705 governs the remedies.

Important: A public nuisance can also cause particularized harm to one person.

For example, a blocked public drainage canal may affect an entire barangay but cause especially serious flooding in the house nearest the obstruction.

In that situation, the nuisance remains public, but the homeowner may have a special basis for seeking relief under Article 703.

B. Nuisance per se versus nuisance per accidens

This is another extremely important distinction.

Nuisance per se: A nuisance at all times and under all circumstances, because of its inherent nature.

Example: An open, dangerous obstruction intrinsically interfering with a public roadway.

Nuisance per accidens: Something that is not inherently a nuisance but becomes one because of its location, manner of operation, circumstances, or surrounding conditions.

Example: An otherwise lawful industrial activity that produces intolerable noise beside a residential neighborhood.

Why does this distinction matter?

Because a lawful business is not automatically a nuisance merely because someone dislikes it.

Example: A karaoke establishment operates legally.

  • If it plays music at a moderate volume in a commercial entertainment area, it is not automatically a nuisance.
  • If it produces excessive noise that seriously disturbs nearby residences, the manner of its operation may constitute a nuisance per accidens.

Therefore, the question is not simply whether an activity exists, but whether its nature or actual operation constitutes an actionable interference.

C. Cases discussed in the textbook

Bengzon v. Province of Pangasinan
62 Phil. 816

The textbook discusses a water-pumping installation whose operation allegedly caused vibration, noise, and discomfort.

The case illustrates the evaluation of a particular activity in light of its actual effects on adjoining occupants.

De Ayala v. Barretto

The textbook contrasts this with a brewery’s operation, where the Court did not treat the activity as a nuisance merely because of the asserted noise or smell. The actual conditions and their effect on the neighborhood mattered.

Key doctrine: Nuisance is often a question of degree, context, and reasonable enjoyment.

An activity that may be tolerable in an industrial district may be unreasonable in a quiet residential subdivision.

Relevant considerations include the nature of the locality, the character and magnitude of the interference, its duration, and surrounding circumstances.

5.03. Strict Liability and Persons Liable

Textbook pp. 883–884

The chapter emphasizes that strict liability may attach to the owner or possessor of property where a nuisance is found, even without establishing that person’s negligence.

But there is an additional question: What if the present owner did not create the nuisance?

Article 696 — Liability of successive owners or possessors

Under Article 696, every successive owner or possessor who fails or refuses to abate a nuisance started by a former owner or possessor is liable in the same manner as the one who created it.

Example: Pedro owns a parcel of land containing an open pit that constitutes a nuisance.

Pedro sells the land to Juan. Juan did not excavate the pit.

However, after acquiring the property, Juan fails or refuses to abate the existing nuisance.

Can Juan escape liability by claiming that Pedro caused it?

No, not merely for that reason.

Article 696 expressly addresses liability of successive owners or possessors who fail or refuse to abate the nuisance.

Article 697 — Abatement does not erase past liability

Suppose Juan finally removes the nuisance after neighbors have already suffered damage.

Does its removal extinguish their claim for the earlier injury?

No.

Under Article 697, abatement does not preclude recovery of damages for the nuisance’s past existence.

Remember the difference:

  • Abatement addresses the continuing harmful condition.
  • Damages compensate for legally recoverable injuries already sustained.

Abatement means removing, suppressing, or otherwise putting an end to a nuisance.

The Civil Code recognizes both judicial and extrajudicial methods.

A. Public nuisance — Article 699

The remedies are:

  1. Prosecution under the Penal Code or a local ordinance.
  2. A civil action.
  3. Abatement without judicial proceedings.

Articles 700–702 provide additional procedural directions:

These provisions should be read alongside Article 703, which expressly permits a private person’s action when a public nuisance is specially injurious to that person.

B. When can a private person abate a public nuisance?

Articles 703 and 704

This is a likely Bar or recitation question.

Article 703 permits a private person to file an action on account of a public nuisance when it is specially injurious to himself.

Article 704 permits extrajudicial abatement of such a nuisance, but imposes safeguards.

Requisites for extrajudicial abatement — Article 704 checklist:

  1. Demand was first made upon the owner or possessor to abate the nuisance.
  2. The demand was rejected.
  3. The district health officer approved the abatement, and it is executed with assistance of the local police.
  4. The value of the destruction does not exceed ₱3,000.

All four conditions must be satisfied, in addition to the requirements that the nuisance be specially injurious, and that abatement cause no breach of the peace or unnecessary injury.

The ₱3,000 figure is the amount stated in Article 704(4) and in your textbook. It remains the amount shown in the published Civil Code provision; do not substitute a different figure without an applicable amending law.

Application

A roadside structure obstructs a public passage and causes particular harm to a nearby resident.

May the resident immediately gather several people and demolish the structure?

No.

The existence of a nuisance does not give someone unlimited authority to destroy another person’s property.

The affected person must establish the applicable right to relief and comply with Article 704’s requirements before resorting to the statutory extrajudicial procedure.

The overarching rule is that abatement must be lawful, necessary, and conducted without unnecessary injury or breach of the peace.

C. Private nuisance — Articles 705 and 706

Article 705 provides two remedies:

  1. Civil action.
  2. Abatement without judicial proceedings.

Article 706 allows an injured person to remove or, if necessary, destroy the thing constituting the nuisance, provided the person does not breach the peace or cause unnecessary injury.

Crucially, Article 706 requires observance of the procedure for extrajudicial abatement of a public nuisance by a private person.

Thus, the safeguards under Article 704 also matter in extrajudicial abatement of private nuisances.

E. Fire Code of the Philippines

Textbook §5.04(c), p. 886

Republic Act No. 9514, the Revised Fire Code of the Philippines of 2008, contains provisions addressing fire hazards and their abatement.

Section 8 prohibits acts including:

  • Blocking or obstructing fire exits and escape routes.
  • Obstructing access for firefighting vehicles.
  • Interfering with firefighting operations.
  • Obstructing fire lanes and access to hydrants.
  • Other acts compromising legally required fire safety measures.

Section 9 provides for enforcement, abatement, and the declaration of qualifying dangerous buildings or structures as public nuisances.

The connection to nuisance law is straightforward: a dangerous fire hazard may endanger not only the property occupant but also the surrounding community.

The Fire Code has its own statutory enforcement procedures. These must not be confused with the separate private-person abatement procedure under Article 704.

F. Liability for damages — Article 707

An important principle: The law permits extrajudicial abatement in certain situations, but protects against misuse of that authority.

Article 707 provides that a private person or public official who extrajudicially abates a nuisance shall be liable for damages:

  1. If unnecessary injury is caused; or
  2. If the alleged nuisance is subsequently declared by the courts not to be a real nuisance.

Example: Juan believes his neighbor’s structure constitutes a nuisance. He destroys the structure without a sufficient legal basis.

A court later determines that the structure was not a nuisance.

Juan may be liable for the damage caused by his extrajudicial action.

The underlying lesson: A sincere belief that something is a nuisance is not enough.

G. Prescription and estoppel

Textbook §5.04(e), pp. 886–887

Article 698 provides:

Lapse of time cannot legalize any nuisance, whether public or private.

Suppose an establishment has maintained an actionable nuisance for 15 years.

Can the operator argue that the nuisance is lawful merely because nobody acted sooner?

No. Passage of time does not legalize the nuisance.

However, distinguish the continuing existence of a nuisance from an action to recover damages for injuries that occurred in the past. Article 698 does not mean every historical damages claim is necessarily imprescriptible.

What about estoppel? Estoppel generally prevents a person from asserting a position inconsistent with certain prior acts or representations when another has relied on them. The textbook explains that estoppel should not allow a danger to public health or safety to continue. However, it acknowledges a more nuanced view: a private person’s prior conduct may affect that person’s own right to damages, even when it cannot make the nuisance itself lawful.

5.05. Environmental Protection

Textbook pp. 887–888

The chapter presents nuisance law as an additional means of environmental protection.

A. Noise pollution as nuisance

Noise is not automatically actionable merely because it can be heard. The legal inquiry involves whether the noise is injurious or unreasonable in its particular context.

Relevant considerations discussed by the textbook include:

  • The character of the neighborhood.
  • The nature and magnitude of the noise.
  • Its frequency and duration.
  • Its impact on the health, comfort, or enjoyment of ordinary people.
  • The surrounding circumstances of the activity.
B. Case: Velasco v. Manila Electric Company

40 SCRA 342 (1971)

The textbook cites this case as part of its treatment of noise and nuisance. The discussion draws upon the principle that noise may constitute nuisance when its effects unreasonably interfere with the health or comfort of persons in the vicinity.

The chapter also explains that a certain degree of noise is an unavoidable consequence of ordinary community life. Hence, the applicable standard is not total silence.

Practical application

A machine shop operates beside a residential house. The machines produce loud vibrations every evening, interrupting sleep and causing recurring discomfort.

Under the textbook’s framework, we would determine:

  1. Whether the noise or vibration constitutes an unreasonable interference.
  2. Whether the nature of the neighborhood makes the interference objectionable.
  3. Whether the frequency, intensity, and duration are sufficient to establish a nuisance.
  4. Whether the affected residents are entitled to abatement, damages, or another appropriate remedy.

Doctrinal connection: Nuisance liability focuses on the harmful condition and the legal responsibility for its maintenance—not necessarily proof of negligent operation.

VII. Unjust Enrichment

Textbook §6, pp. 888–892

Part VI: Nuisance → YOU ARE HERE: Ch. 16, Part VII & VIII — Unjust Enrichment & Allied Banking → Ch. 17: Product & Service Liability
STATUTORY ADDRESS Article 22, New Civil Code; UP v. Philab Industries; Shinryo v. RRN; Allied Banking v. Lim Sio Wan.
CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Shifts focus from physical injury or property interference to retaining an undeserved economic benefit at someone else’s expense.
WHY THE LAW CARES Provides a subsidiary restitution safety net (accion in rem verso) when contract, quasi-contract, crime, and quasi-delict remedies are unavailable.

We are now entering the final topic of the chapter.

Although the book discusses unjust enrichment under strict liability, its conceptual foundation is different from the liability arising from animals, falling objects, and nuisance.

The central concern is not necessarily whether somebody caused an injury. Rather, it is whether someone has obtained or retained a benefit at another person’s expense without a legitimate legal basis.

A. The fundamental principle

Imagine this: You accidentally transfer ₱50,000 to another person’s bank account. The recipient does not have any legal right to receive or retain your money. Would it be fair for the recipient to keep it simply because you made the mistake? Of course not. This illustrates the fundamental principle against unjust enrichment.

Article 22 — New Civil Code:

Every person who, through an act of performance by another, or any other means, acquires or comes into possession of something at the expense of the latter without just or legal ground, shall return the same to him.

Notice two essential ideas: First: One person has received a benefit at the expense of another. Second: There is no just or legal ground for retaining that benefit.

Not every enrichment is unjust. For instance, if Maria receives ₱50,000 because Juan validly paid an existing debt, Maria has obtained a benefit. However, she is legally entitled to receive the payment. There is no unjust enrichment because there is a just or legal ground for the benefit.

6.01. Unjust Enrichment Is Not a Quasi-Contract

This distinction is expressly discussed in the textbook. Although the principle is found in Article 22, it should not automatically be treated as identical to quasi-contract.

A. What is a quasi-contract?

A quasi-contract is a juridical relation arising from certain lawful, voluntary, and unilateral acts, under which the law creates obligations to prevent unjust enrichment. Examples previously encountered in Obligations and Contracts include:

  • Solutio indebiti — payment or delivery made by mistake when there is no right to demand it.
  • Negotiorum gestio — voluntary management of another person’s business or property without authority.
B. How is Article 22 different?

The textbook explains that Article 22 establishes an independent general principle against unjust enrichment. It also explains that Article 22 can apply even where the circumstances do not amount to a quasi-contract. A major point is that Article 22 may operate as a subsidiary basis for restitution. This leads us to its most important application: accion in rem verso.

6.02. Requisites of Accion In Rem Verso

Accion in rem verso is a personal action based on unjust enrichment, used to recover a benefit obtained at another’s expense when no other adequate cause of action under the specified legal categories is available.

The textbook identifies four essential requisites:

  1. The defendant has been enriched. The defendant received or retained a benefit, including a financial advantage or release from an obligation.
  2. The plaintiff suffered a loss. The benefit came at the plaintiff’s expense.
  3. The enrichment is without just or legal ground. There is no legitimate basis for the defendant to retain the benefit.
  4. The plaintiff has no other action based on contract, quasi-contract, crime, or quasi-delict. This is what makes the action subsidiary or auxiliary.

These requisites are likewise stated in University of the Philippines v. Philab Industries, Inc. (G.R. No. 152411, September 29, 2004).

A. Nature of Accion In Rem Verso

The most important characteristic is its subsidiary nature. This means the action does not replace an available cause of action founded upon another legal institution.

Situation May accion in rem verso be invoked?
A defendant is enriched without legal ground, and the plaintiff has no other applicable action.Potentially yes, if all requisites are established.
A plaintiff has an enforceable contract providing a remedy.Generally no; the contractual remedy must be pursued.

Memory aid: Think of accion in rem verso as a remedy of last resort, not a substitute for an action that already exists.

B. Not applicable if based on contract

Textbook §6.02(b), p. 891

The textbook cites Shinryo (Philippines) Company, Inc. v. RRN Incorporated (G.R. No. 172525) for the proposition that unjust enrichment cannot be used to circumvent a contractual basis for a claim.

Example: Juan contracts with Pedro to construct a house for ₱2 million. Pedro completes the work, but Juan refuses to pay the outstanding amount. Pedro cannot simply disregard the available contractual remedy and insist upon accion in rem verso as if there were no contract. The obligation to pay is governed by their contractual relationship.

Recitation principle: If another applicable cause of action exists under contract, quasi-contract, crime, or quasi-delict, pursue that action rather than automatically invoking accion in rem verso.

B. How did Producers Bank become enriched?

This is the most important question for our study. Suppose you owe Maria ₱100,000. Instead of using your own money to pay her, somebody else’s money is improperly transferred and applied toward settling your debt. The payment causes your obligation to Maria to be extinguished. Even if you did not physically receive the money into your own hands, you benefited because your debt was discharged. That was the essential enrichment issue involving Producers Bank.

C. The Supreme Court’s ruling

The Supreme Court held that Allied Bank and Metrobank were responsible to Lim Sio Wan in a 60:40 proportion, respectively. However, Producers Bank had benefited from the misapplied payment because its debt to FCC was extinguished. Consequently, Producers Bank was held liable to reimburse Allied Bank and Metrobank for the amounts they were ordered to pay Lim Sio Wan. FCC, on the other hand, was not considered unjustly enriched because it had a legitimate right to receive payment of its matured placement. The distinction is critical: FCC was entitled to payment. Producers Bank was not entitled to have its debt discharged at Lim Sio Wan’s expense.

D. Legal doctrine

The case demonstrates that unjust enrichment is not limited to situations in which a defendant physically receives another person’s money. A benefit may take the form of the extinguishment of an obligation, and retaining that advantage without a just or legal ground may give rise to restitution under Article 22.

Part 1 — The Big Picture

WHY THE LAW CARES Consumers cannot inspect factory floors or run lab tests before buying everyday goods; law shifts defect risks onto manufacturers, importers, and suppliers.

1. What is product and service liability?

Imagine the following situation: You purchase a brand-new electric fan from an appliance store in San Fernando, La Union. After using it normally for three days, its motor suddenly explodes, causing burns to your arm. Naturally, you want to hold someone responsible. But several legal questions arise:

  • Can you sue the store that sold the electric fan?
  • Can you sue its manufacturer even though you never directly contracted with that manufacturer?
  • Must you prove that the manufacturer was negligent?
  • What if the manufacturer exercised extraordinary care in producing the electric fan, but it nevertheless contained a dangerous defect?
  • Can the manufacturer escape liability because the warranty has expired?

These questions are precisely what product liability law attempts to answer. In simple terms, product liability deals with the legal responsibility of those involved in producing or supplying products that cause injury or damage because of defects. Service liability applies similar principles to defective or unsafe services.

2. Why is this part of Torts and Damages?

Ordinarily, under Article 2176 of the Civil Code, a plaintiff suing for quasi-delict must establish negligence. Product liability introduces an important development: there are circumstances in which a manufacturer or service supplier can be held liable even without proof of negligence. This is called strict liability.

ORDINARY QUASI-DELICT
A technician carelessly installs an electrical wire, causing a fire. You ordinarily establish that the technician was negligent and that the negligence caused the damage.
STRICT PRODUCT LIABILITY
A defective, unsafe power bank explodes during normal use. You may establish liability by proving the defect, damage, and causal connection, without proving negligence.

The fundamental distinction is: Negligence focuses on the defendant’s conduct. Strict product liability focuses on the defective product and the injury attributable to it. However, strict liability is not absolute liability. The plaintiff must still establish the facts required by the applicable law, and the defendant may invoke statutory defenses.

Part 2 — Product Liability Law

A. Legal and constitutional foundation

The Philippine legal system recognizes consumer protection as a matter of public policy. Article XVI, Section 9 of the 1987 Constitution provides:

The State shall protect consumers from trade malpractices and from substandard or hazardous products.

This is the constitutional foundation of product safety regulation. Why does the Constitution recognize this protection? Because there is an inherent imbalance between manufacturers and ordinary consumers. A manufacturer generally possesses technical information concerning the product’s materials, design, production process, testing, and potential hazards. An ordinary consumer cannot reasonably be expected to investigate all these matters before purchasing. For example, when you purchase bottled water, you should not have to conduct a laboratory examination to determine whether it is safe to drink. The law therefore places appropriate responsibilities on manufacturers, sellers, and suppliers.

Part 4 — I. Fraud or Misrepresentation

Textbook: pp. 896–897

1. Fraud under the New Civil Code

Simple explanation: A seller or manufacturer induces a consumer to purchase a product by making a false representation about it. Suppose a dealership advertises a secondhand vehicle as having never been flooded. The seller knows it suffered severe flood damage but deliberately conceals that fact. The buyer purchases the vehicle in reliance on the representation and later incurs substantial repair costs. The legal problem is not merely that the vehicle is defective. It is also that the buyer was deceived into purchasing it.

Article 33 — Independent civil action for fraud

Article 33 allows a civil action for damages, separate and distinct from the criminal action, in cases of fraud, defamation, and physical injuries. The plaintiff need only establish liability by preponderance of evidence, not proof beyond reasonable doubt. For purposes of Article 33, fraud is understood broadly and includes calculated deception, concealment, or other dishonest conduct resulting in damage.

Not every inaccurate statement constitutes actionable misrepresentation
StatementLegal significance
“This vehicle has never been flooded.”A factual representation capable of verification
“This is probably the best car in the Philippines.”Ordinarily promotional opinion or puffery
“This medicine is FDA-approved,” when it is notA false representation of a material fact

An opinion may still be actionable where the circumstances establish legally significant deception. The substance and surrounding circumstances matter more than the seller’s choice of words.

2. Fraud under the Consumer Act

Article 50 of R.A. No. 7394 prohibits deceptive sales acts or practices. It covers deception occurring before, during, or after a consumer transaction. Examples include falsely representing that a product is new, that it possesses certain benefits, that repairs are necessary, or that a particular warranty exists.

Article 52 separately prohibits unfair or unconscionable sales acts or practices, including transactions induced by taking advantage of a consumer’s ignorance, infirmity, or inability to protect their interests.

Remedy under the Consumer Act — Article 60

Under Article 60, violations may result in statutory penalties. In addition, the court may grant an injunction and actual damages, or other appropriate relief, to redress injury resulting from conduct violating Articles 50 and 51. This is distinct from the remedies for an ordinary breach of warranty.

Remember: Fraud focuses on deception. A product may be defective without fraud, and a fraudulent representation may exist even if the product has not physically injured anyone.

Part 5 — II. Warranties

Textbook: pp. 897–902

This is one of the most practical sections of the chapter.

1. What is a warranty?

A warranty is an affirmation, promise, or assurance concerning a product that may form part of the seller’s legal obligations. For example, you purchase a refrigerator advertised as capable of maintaining a specified temperature. If it cannot perform as warranted, there may be a breach of warranty even if no one has suffered physical injuries. That is the essential difference between warranty liability and strict liability for personal injury: A warranty problem may concern the quality, condition, or performance of the product. A strict product liability problem concerns harm caused by a legally defective product.

A. Express warranty versus implied warranty
Express warrantyImplied warranty
Arises from the seller’s affirmation or promise, subject to legal requirementsArises by operation of law

Under Article 1546 of the Civil Code, an affirmation of fact or promise relating to a thing sold can constitute an express warranty where it naturally tends to induce the purchase and the buyer purchases in reliance upon it. A mere statement of opinion does not ordinarily create an express warranty, subject to the qualification concerning expert affirmations in Article 1546.

B. Warranties against hidden defects

Under Article 1561, the seller is responsible for certain hidden defects that render the thing sold unfit for its intended use or substantially diminish its fitness for that use. The important point is that a product need not be completely useless. Example: You buy a secondhand generator. It runs, but a concealed internal defect causes it to overheat so severely that it cannot operate reliably. Its functionality is substantially impaired despite its ability to start.

Requisites discussed in the textbook (drawing from Nutrimix Feeds Corporation v. Court of Appeals):

  1. The defect must be hidden.
  2. It must have existed at the legally relevant time of sale or delivery.
  3. It must be of a kind covered by the warranty rather than a defect validly excluded from it.
  4. It must be sufficiently serious to render the product unfit or substantially reduce its fitness.
  5. The action must be brought within the applicable period.

For injury allegedly resulting from a defective product, the chapter also emphasizes three evidentiary requirements: proof of injury, proof that the product was defective or unreasonably unsafe and caused that injury, and proof that the defect existed when the product left the seller’s or manufacturer’s control. These are related but distinct inquiries: the first concerns the Civil Code warranty; the second concerns proof of defect-related injury.

C. Remedies — Article 1567

The Civil Code provides two principal remedies for breach of warranty against hidden defects.

Acción redhibitoria
Withdraw from the contract because of the hidden defect. Return the defective product and seek appropriate restitution, with damages in proper cases.
Acción quanti minoris
Demand a proportionate reduction of the purchase price. Keep the product but seek a reduction because its value or usefulness is impaired, with damages in proper cases.

The remedies are alternative, not cumulative. Article 1571 generally requires actions arising from the preceding provisions on warranties against hidden defects to be brought within six months from delivery. Do not confuse this with the periods applicable to quasi-delict or special Consumer Act warranty rights.

D. Important case: Nutrimix Feeds Corporation v. Court of Appeals

G.R. No. 152219 | October 25, 2004

Facts: The spouses Evangelista purchased animal feeds from Nutrimix Feeds Corporation. They alleged that the feeds were defective and caused their chickens and hogs to become sick, stunted, and die.
Issue: Was Nutrimix liable for breach of implied warranty?
Ruling: The Supreme Court ruled against the buyers’ warranty claim. They failed to establish sufficiently that the feeds were defective when they left the manufacturer’s control and that the alleged defect caused the animals’ illness and death.
Doctrine: The mere fact that damage followed the use of a product does not automatically prove that the product was defective or that the defect caused the damage.

Consider the distinction: The animals became sick after eating the feeds. But the illness could have arisen from another cause, such as disease, contamination after delivery, or improper storage. Without sufficient evidence linking the harm to a legally relevant defect, the court cannot simply assume manufacturer liability.
Bar examination takeaway: Temporal sequence is not the same as legal causation.

2. Privity of contract

Privity means a direct contractual relationship between parties. Ordinarily, contractual obligations are enforceable by and against the parties to the contract, subject to exceptions. Imagine: Manufacturer → Retailer → Buyer. The buyer directly purchases from the retailer, not from the manufacturer. Does the absence of a direct contract prevent the buyer from holding the manufacturer liable? Not necessarily. The Consumer Act imposes statutory obligations, and the Civil Code recognizes certain causes of action that do not depend upon a contractual relationship.

Important case: Del Rosario v. Court of Appeals

G.R. No. 118325 | January 29, 1997

The Del Rosario spouses selected roofing materials after relying on public advertisements describing their durability and strength. Their contractor handled the purchase arrangements. Subsequently, portions of the roof were blown off during a typhoon, and evidence established defective installation and noncompliance with the manufacturer’s representations. The manufacturer argued that there was no privity of contract with the spouses. The Supreme Court rejected that defense under the established circumstances. What mattered was that the manufacturer made express representations to the public, the spouses relied upon them, and the manufacturer delivered and installed the materials improperly. The lesson is not that privity is irrelevant in every warranty dispute. Rather, lack of direct contractual dealings does not automatically defeat liability, particularly where representations, reliance, the manufacturer’s own conduct, and applicable law support the claim.

3. Warranties under the Consumer Act

Article 67 states that Civil Code provisions on conditions and warranties govern contracts of sale with conditions and warranties. Article 68 then supplies additional rules applicable to the sale of consumer products with warranties.

  • 3.01. Formalities of an express warranty: A seller or manufacturer who gives an express warranty must clearly identify: (1) warrantor and terms; (2) person to whom warranty extends; (3) products or parts covered; (4) what warrantor will do in case of defect/malfunction, including who bears expense; (5) what consumer must do to exercise warranty; and (6) period within which warrantor will perform after notice. The Consumer Act distinguishes between a full warranty (satisfies statutory minimum standards including free remedy within reasonable time and replacement/refund under Art. 68) and a limited warranty (must be so designated if it does not meet minimum standards). Statutory minimum duties do not apply in the same manner where the warrantor establishes that malfunction resulted from unreasonable use.

Part 6 — III. Negligence

Textbook: pp. 902–903

1. Product liability through quasi-delict

Under Article 2176, a person who, through an act or omission causing damage to another, is guilty of fault or negligence, with no pre-existing contractual relation between them in relation to that obligation, may be liable for quasi-delict. In product liability, negligence may occur at design, manufacture, inspection, packaging, storage, distribution, or failure to provide appropriate safety information. Example: A food company negligently allows its bottling machinery to become contaminated; contaminated beverages reach consumers and cause illness. The plaintiff must establish negligence and proximate causation.

Important case: Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals

G.R. No. 110295 | October 18, 1993

A school canteen operator discovered foreign substances in unopened bottles of soft drinks. The incident caused customers to lose confidence in the beverages, resulting in losses to her business. The manufacturer argued that the action should be governed by the rules on breach of implied warranty, including the six-month period. The Supreme Court treated the allegations as sufficient to sustain a cause of action for quasi-delict, rather than confining the complainant to a warranty action. An injured party is not necessarily restricted to contractual warranty remedies merely because defective goods are involved.

2. Violation of statute as proof of negligence

Statutory safety requirements regarding labeling, composition, packaging, ingredients, manufacturing, and safety help establish the standard of conduct expected from manufacturers and suppliers. If a manufacturer violates a safety requirement intended to prevent the kind of harm that occurred, the violation may support a finding of negligence. Important distinction: Always ask whether the applicable statute establishes a standard of care (evidence of negligence) or creates an independent statutory basis of liability (such as Article 97).

Part 7 — IV. Intentional Act: Physical Damage or Injury

Textbook: pp. 903–904

1. Independent civil action — Article 33

Suppose a restaurant owner intentionally contaminates a meal served to a customer, causing physical injuries. The facts may support a criminal prosecution, but the victim may also have an independent civil action for damages under Article 33. Under Rule 111, Section 3, independent civil actions recognized by Articles 32, 33, 34, and 2176 of the Civil Code proceed independently of the criminal action, require preponderance of evidence, and cannot result in double recovery for the same act or omission.

2. Acts contrary to law — Article 20

Article 20 provides that a person who causes damage to another through an act contrary to law, whether willfully or negligently, shall indemnify the latter for the damage. If a manufacturer violates an applicable statutory safety prohibition and that unlawful act causes compensable injury, Article 20 may provide a basis for damages. Article 20 is broader than intentional wrongdoing because it also encompasses negligent violations of law.

Part 8 — V. Delict

Textbook: pp. 904–905

1. Under the Revised Penal Code

An act involving a defective product might also constitute a criminal offense (e.g., intentionally causing bodily injury, or criminally negligent conduct implicating Article 365, or deceit). When the facts establish the applicable offense, Article 100 of the Revised Penal Code recognizes that a person criminally liable for a felony is also civilly liable.

2. Under special laws

The chapter also discusses special consumer-protection laws containing penal provisions, citing People v. Chung (sale of adulterated coffee under the Pure Food and Drugs Act). A penal statute may impose responsibility for prohibited conduct even if the offender lacked actual knowledge of the adulteration, depending on the statutory offense. Remember: A violation of a criminal or regulatory statute does not automatically prove every element of a separate civil claim; the applicable law, injury, causal relationship, and recoverable damages must still be analyzed.

Textbook: pp. 905–929

CHAIN-OF-LOGIC BRIDGE Moves to Theory VI—holding manufacturers, importers, component makers, service providers, and qualifying sellers liable without proving negligence.
WHY THE LAW CARES Manufacturers control design, testing, and risk distribution; consumers cannot investigate factory defects inside sealed products.

This is the longest and most important discussion in the chapter.

1. Understanding strict liability

Let us begin with a hypothetical. A manufacturing company produces 100,000 rice cookers. It maintains sophisticated quality-control systems, employs highly trained engineers, and regularly inspects its equipment. Nevertheless, one rice cooker leaves the factory with a defective electrical component. During ordinary use, it catches fire and injures its owner. The manufacturer argues: “We exercised all reasonable care. We were not negligent. The defective unit escaped detection despite our safety procedures.” Can the manufacturer escape liability merely by proving that it was careful? Under an applicable strict product liability provision, no. That is the fundamental character of strict liability. But do not misunderstand this principle: Strict liability does not mean that every unfortunate accident involving a product automatically makes the manufacturer liable. The defect, damage, causal connection, and applicable statutory requirements remain important.

2. Strict liability under the New Civil Code — Article 2187

Manufacturers and processors of foodstuffs, drinks, toilet articles and similar goods shall be liable for death or injuries caused by any noxious or harmful substances used, although no contractual relation exists between them and the consumers.

Example: A consumer purchases a bottled drink from a sari-sari store. Unknown to the consumer, the beverage contains a harmful substance introduced during manufacturing and causes serious illness. The fact that the consumer purchased from the sari-sari store rather than directly from the bottling company does not itself defeat an Article 2187 claim.

The relationship between Article 2187 and Article 2176

Article 2187 appears in the Civil Code’s provisions dealing with quasi-delicts, but it contains no express requirement that the claimant prove the manufacturer’s negligence. Your textbook treats Article 2187 as a strict liability provision and criticizes reasoning that would make proof of negligence necessary in every such case.

Case: Coca-Cola Bottlers Philippines, Inc. v. Meñez (G.R. No. 209906 | November 22, 2017): Meñez alleged that he consumed a beverage containing kerosene and sought damages from the bottling company. The Supreme Court recognized that a person suing under Article 2187 need not first exhaust administrative remedies before bringing the civil action. However, the Court denied the claimed moral and exemplary damages and attorney’s fees because the necessary factual and legal bases for those awards were not adequately established, particularly the proof of physical injury for the claimed moral damages. Important doctrinal distinction: Article 2187’s treatment of liability must not be confused with the requirements for awarding particular kinds of damages.

3. The American cases: Escola and Greenman

  • A. Escola v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.: A waitress was injured when a bottle of Coca-Cola exploded in her hand. The majority permitted recovery through negligence principles, including res ipsa loquitur. Justice Roger Traynor, in his concurring opinion, advocated strict liability for manufacturers when defective products placed on the market cause injuries during their intended use, arguing consumers should not be forced to prove internal manufacturing details they cannot reasonably investigate.
  • B. Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc.: A consumer was injured while operating a defective power tool. The California Supreme Court imposed strict liability on the manufacturer for the defective product, recognizing that liability could arise independently of the ordinary requirements of warranty or proof of negligence.

4. Why does the law impose strict liability?

  1. Manufacturers are better positioned to prevent defects. They control product design, manufacturing processes, testing, and quality assurance.
  2. Consumers frequently cannot prove negligence. A buyer has no access to internal production records, equipment, or employees.
  3. Liability creates incentives for safety. Greater reason to invest in reliable design, adequate warnings, and quality control.
  4. Manufacturers can distribute the cost of product-related risks. They may insure against losses and incorporate costs into pricing.
  5. Consumer expectations deserve protection. Consumers are entitled to expect an appropriate degree of safety.

5. The exploding bottle case

The textbook discusses Loreto Luciano De Salas v. San Miguel Brewery, involving a soft-drink bottle that exploded and injured a person. The Court of Appeals analyzed the incident through negligence principles because of the applicable law and circumstances at the time. The textbook uses the case to show why modern strict liability provides a more direct theory where an appropriate statute imposes liability independently of fault.

6. Requisites of product liability & Application

For a defective-product claim, the analysis generally involves establishing: (1) a legally relevant defect in the product; (2) compensable injury or damage suffered by the claimant; (3) causation—that the defect caused the injury or damage; and (4) appropriate connection to the defendant and the product’s circulation (defect existed at the legally relevant time and product was not materially altered). Notice Article 2187 and Article 97 do not have identical textual requirements. In an unopened bottle containing a harmful chemical, relevant evidence includes the bottle’s identity/source, lab analysis, seal integrity, storage/handling, causal link to injury, and whether post-control contamination occurred.

2. Article 99 — Liability for defective services

Strict liability under the Consumer Act also applies to defective services. A service is defective when it fails to provide the safety the consumer may rightfully expect, considering the manner of its performance, hazards reasonably expected, and the time it was provided. Example: A repair shop improperly secures an essential component on a customer’s motorcycle, causing an accident. The inquiry focuses on rightfully expected safety rather than admitted negligence, subject to defenses where the service was not defective or the consumer or a third party was solely at fault.

3. Persons liable under the Consumer Act

  • A. Manufacturers, producers, and importers (Art. 97): Broader than factory owners; a person who places products into commerce under their own brand/trademark may qualify as a manufacturer even when another entity physically produces the goods.
  • B. Sellers or tradesmen (Article 98): Sellers are not automatically liable in the same manner as manufacturers under Art. 97. Article 98 imposes liability where: (1) manufacturer, builder, producer, or importer cannot be identified; (2) product is supplied without clear identification of the responsible entity; or (3) seller fails to preserve perishable goods adequately (e.g., grocery store negligently failing to refrigerate meat).
  • C. Components manufacturer (Article 106): Provides for joint liability involving the responsible component manufacturer, builder, or importer and the party that incorporated the component into the product or service (e.g., defective brake component inside an assembled car).

Part 11 — What Makes a Product Defective?

A product is not necessarily defective merely because it malfunctions, is of lower quality, or fails to meet a consumer’s personal preference. Article 97 uses a safety-based standard: whether the product offers the safety rightfully expected of it, considering the relevant circumstances.

1. Manufacturing defect & 2. Design defect

  • Manufacturing defect: Occurs when a particular product departs from its intended design or manufacturing specifications (e.g., 1 out of 10,000 electric fans has an improperly installed wire). The design itself may be safe, but the particular unit was improperly manufactured.
  • Design defect: Exists when the product’s intended design itself gives rise to a legally relevant safety defect (e.g., every kettle’s handle is placed too close to the heating element). Manufacturing defect = deviation from the design. Design defect = problem with the design itself.

3. How do courts evaluate design defects? (Comparative tests)

  1. A. Consumer expectation test: Did the product perform as safely as an ordinary consumer could reasonably expect? (e.g., a chair collapsing during normal sitting).
  2. B. Risk-utility test: Weighs safety risks against usefulness and feasibility of reducing risks (usefulness, likelihood/seriousness of injury, safer alternatives, cost/feasibility, user’s ability to avoid danger).
  3. C. Reasonable alternative design: Whether a reasonably feasible safer design (like an inexpensive protective shield) was available without undermining ordinary usefulness.

4. Test under the Consumer Act (Article 97)

A product is defective when it fails to offer the safety rightfully expected of it, considering: (a) its presentation; (b) its reasonably expected use and hazards; and (c) the time it was placed into circulation. A product is not defective merely because a better-quality product later enters the market (e.g., a 2020 car is not defective just because a 2026 model has newer safety tech).

5. Packaging and presentation defect & 6. Lack of warning

  • Packaging/presentation: Selling a concentrated chemical in a beverage-like bottle or leaky packaging creates unreasonable danger.
  • Lack of warning (Arts. 74, 76, 77): Failing to warn of foreseeable hazards (e.g., dangerous fumes when two cleaners are mixed). Compulsory labeling/fair packaging rules require minimum hazard information. Regarding the American Woodill debate over whether manufacturer knowledge is required: for Philippine purposes, Article 97 expressly covers insufficient or inadequate information on product use and hazards and imposes liability independently of fault—so do not automatically import foreign negligence requirements into Art. 97.

Part 13 — Defenses to Strict Product Liability

Textbook: pp. 928–929

1. Defenses under Article 97

The manufacturer, builder, producer, or importer is not liable when it establishes:

  1. It did not place the product on the market (e.g., counterfeit device).
  2. The product had no defect, even though it was placed on the market.
  3. The consumer or a third party was solely at fault (e.g., consumer completely dismantles essential safety components).

2. Comparative negligence

The key statutory wording is solely at fault. The textbook emphasizes that the statutory defense requires sole fault and questions applying comparative-negligence rules that might undermine strict liability under Art. 97.

A consumer injured by a defective product is not restricted to a single legal theory.
