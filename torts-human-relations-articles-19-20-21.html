Overview & Purpose of the Chapter on Human Relations

Chapter 8 of the New Civil Code covers torts under Chapter 2 of the Preliminary Title ("Human Relations"). Although this chapter encompasses both negligent and intentional acts, the torts discussed here are predominantly intentional in nature or involve malice and bad faith.

Reason for the Chapter on Human Relations

  • Historical Background: Chapter 2 of the Preliminary Title on "Human Relations" was introduced by the Code Commission and was not present in the Old Civil Code.
  • Core Purpose: It formulates fundamental principles necessary for the rightful relationship between human beings and the stability of the social order.
  • Guiding Norms: It establishes norms of good conscience to run as "golden threads" through society, ensuring that the law approaches its ideal of the sway and dominance of justice by requiring everyone to respect the rights of others.

The Catch-All Provisions: Articles 19, 20, and 21

Articles 19, 20, and 21 form the legal bedrock for awarding damages whenever a person commits an act in violation of positive law or rudimentary human rights.

1. Article 19 (Principle of Abuse of Rights)

"Every person must, in the exercise of his rights and in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith."
  • Role & Nature: It declares a principle of law and sets the standard or degree of care required in human relations.
  • Limitation: By itself, Article 19 does not provide a remedy or serve as a standalone basis for an actionable tort. An actionable tort arises only when Article 19 is alleged together with Article 20 or Article 21.

2. Article 20 (Violation of Law)

"Every person who, contrary to law, wilfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same."
  • Role & Nature: Concerns violations of existing positive law as the basis for an injury.
  • Scope: Pervades the entire legal system, ensuring that anyone who suffers damage from a legal violation is not left without relief.
  • State of Mind: Uniquely covers acts done either willfully (intentional) or negligently (consciously done without intending the harmful result).

3. Article 21 (Acts Contrary to Morals, Good Customs, or Public Policy)

"Any person who wilfully causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy shall compensate the latter for damages."
  • Role & Nature: Addresses injuries caused by acts not explicitly prohibited by positive law.
  • State of Mind: Requires the act to be willful (there must be an intention to perform the act and a desire to achieve the outcome).

Key Concepts, Elements, and Requisites

1. Requisites & Distinctions Among Articles 19, 20, and 21

  • Common Element (Articles 19 & 21): The act causing injury must be intentional.
  • Distinction under Article 20: The act may be either willful or negligent.
  • Abuse of Right as a Legal Violation: Abuse of right under Article 19 can serve as a basis for recovery under Article 20 because an abuse of right is contrary to law—the law being Article 19 itself.

2. Principle of "No Wrong Without a Remedy" (Article 21)

  • Fills Statutory Gaps: Designed to remedy "countless gaps in the statutes" where human foresight cannot anticipate every form of human misconduct or moral wrong.
  • Transmutes Morality into Law: Transmutes moral norms into legal rules, ensuring no person can cause damage to another with impunity simply because no specific statute was broken.
  • Comparative Law: A similar provision exists under Article 826 of the German Civil Code.
  • Public Policy Violations: Applies to acts contrary to public policy not forbidden by statute, such as unfair/oppressive business practices or oppressive actions by landholders/employers against tenants/employees.

3. Requisites for Damage as an Essential Element

  • Requirement of Injury/Damage: To sustain an action under Articles 19, 20, and 21, the plaintiff must suffer actual damage or injury (material, moral, honor, or credit). A complaint seeking purely abstract judicial recognition without alleging damage will be dismissed.
  • Moral Damages Exception: It is not necessary to prove actual or compensatory physical damages to recover moral damages under the Chapter on Human Relations (Arts. 19 to 36). Moral injury alone suffices.

4. Requisites Regarding Good Faith & State of Mind

  • Evil Intent Not Required: A defendant can be liable under Articles 19 and 21 even if they acted without bad intentions, ill-will, or evil intent. Actionable wrong is judged independently of the actor's state of mind.
  • Effect on Exemplary Damages: While good faith does not excuse liability for moral or compensatory damages, acting in good faith shields the defendant from exemplary damages.
  • Wrongful Means Destroy Good Faith: A person is not acting in good faith if they willfully adopt wrongful means that are substantially certain to cause humiliation, even if the underlying motive (e.g., protecting one's property) was legitimate. Both the means and the ends must be right.

Examples Given by the Author & Referenced Case Law

1. Enrique J.L. Ruiz et al. v. The Secretary of National Defense (Damage as an Essential Element)

  • Facts: Plaintiffs filed an action under Article 21 seeking a judicial declaration/recognition that they were the co-architects of a hospital building to enhance their professional prestige.
  • Ruling: The Supreme Court dismissed the action because the plaintiffs did not show or allege that they suffered any material, moral, or credit damage. Courts will not entertain actions that merely seek abstract recognition without damage.

2. Llorente v. Sandiganbayan (Bad Faith and Undue Discrimination)

  • Facts: A public official refused to issue a clearance to a retiring employee who was forced to resign due to the abolition of his position. The official claimed he was merely following procedures and lacked ill-will or a personal grudge.
  • Ruling: The Supreme Court held that ill-will is not required. The facts demonstrated unfairness and undue discrimination, constituting bad faith and a violation of Article 19 in relation to Article 27.

3. Abuse of Legal Rights (Velayo v. Shell Co., Sevilla v. CA, Valenzuela v. CA)

  • Principle: Exercising a legally recognized right becomes illegal when executed contrary to the norms of Article 19.
  • Examples:
    • Disposing of corporate property (a legal act) specifically to place it beyond the reach of a creditor (Velayo).
    • Terminating an agency contract (a legal act) when done improperly to deprive the agent of their legitimate business (Sevilla; Valenzuela).

4. Grand Union Supermarket, Inc. v. Espino, Jr. (Public Humiliation vs. Good Faith Defense)

  • Facts: A customer at a supermarket placed a small item (a $1.50 file) in his pocket while shopping, forgot to pay for it at the checkout, and was stopped outside. Supermarket security guards detained him, shouted at him, fined him, and threatened to call the police in front of a crowd of onlookers, causing him intense embarrassment and mental anguish.
  • Rulings on Liability & Damages:
    • Moral Damages Awarded: The supermarket willfully caused loss and injury contrary to morals, good customs, or public policy under Articles 19 and 21, entitling the customer to moral damages for humiliation and besmirched reputation.
    • Contributory Negligence: The customer's forgetfulness contributed to the incident, acting as a factor that reduced the amount of recoverable damages under Article 2214.
    • Exemplary Damages Denied: The supermarket acted with probable cause in attempting to protect its property under Article 429. Because it acted in good faith without deliberate intent to parade or humiliate him, exemplary damages were denied.
  • Author's Analysis: The author notes that even if there was no ill intent to humiliate, using wrongful, disproportionate means that are substantially certain to cause public embarrassment constitutes an intentional tort, as a person cannot be considered in good faith when employing wrongful means.

This chapter covers Abuse of Right under Article 19 of the New Civil Code and introduces Acts Contra Bonus Mores under Article 21.

I. Concept and Foundation of Abuse of Right

The principle of abuse of rights is a departure from the traditional maxim qui iure suo utitur neminem laedit ("he who exercises his own right harms nobody"). Instead, it restates the fundamental Roman law principles:

  • Honeste vivere (to live honestly);
  • Alterum non laedere (not to injure another); and
  • Jus suum cuique tribuere (to render to everyone his due).

While a right may be legal because it is recognized or granted by law, its exercise must conform to the norms enshrined in Article 19. Under Article 19, every person must:

  1. Act with justice;
  2. Give everyone his due; and
  3. Observe honesty and good faith.

When a right is exercised in a manner that fails to conform to these standards and causes damage to another, a legal wrong is committed, and the wrongdoer is held responsible. Article 19 sets the standard, while Article 20 (for violations of law) and Article 21 (for acts contrary to morals, good customs, or public policy) provide the sanction and basis for awarding damages.

II. Elements of Abuse of Right

1. Elements under Philippine Jurisprudence (Albenson Enterprises v. Court of Appeals)

To successfully maintain an action for abuse of right under Article 19, the following three requisites must be present:

  • The legal right or duty is exercised in bad faith; and
  • The exercise is for the sole intent of prejudicing or injuring another.

2. Elements cited from the Supreme Court of Spain

  • The exercise of a right which is objective and apparently legal;
  • Damage or injury to an interest not specifically protected by a legal precept; and
  • Immorality or antisocial character of the damage or injury caused, either with intent to injure or without a serious or legitimate purpose.

III. Examples of Cases Where Abuse of Right WAS Committed

1. Abuse of Rights of Creditors

  • Velayo v. Shell Co. of the Philippines: A creditor took advantage of inside knowledge regarding impending insolvency proceedings and schemed to transfer its credit to a sister company in the United States, which then attached the debtor’s aircraft. This deprived other creditors of their lawful share in the insolvent's assets.
  • Philippine National Bank v. Court of Appeals: A bank twice disapproved a debtor’s proposed lease of a sugar quota to force an increase in the consideration, knowing that the agricultural year was about to expire and that the debtor would be unable to utilize the quota.
  • Uypitching v. Quiamco: A mortgagee, accompanied by policemen, forcibly seized a mortgaged motorcycle from the debtor's establishment without a court order or search warrant, rather than bringing a proper civil action for foreclosure.

2. Abuse of Right of Principal

  • Valenzuela v. CA / Sevilla v. CA: A principal unreasonably and in bad faith terminated an agency agreement for selfish reasons.

3. Abuse of Right of Agents

  • Jose Arlegui v. Court of Appeals: Officers of an apartment tenants' association secretly purchased the building for themselves, violating the trust reposed in them by the other tenant-members.

4. Abuse of Right of Public Officers

  • Llorente v. Court of Appeals: A public officer withheld the issuance of a clearance to a resigning employee while routinely issuing clearances to other similarly situated employees.
  • Villanueva v. Rosqueta: A superior officer prevented a subordinate from performing her official duties despite a court injunction enjoining such replacement.

5. Abuse of Court Processes

  • Sergio Amonoy v. Spouses Jose Gutierrez: Petitioners proceeded with demolishing the respondents' house despite a Temporary Restraining Order (TRO) issued by the Court of Appeals. The principle of damnum absque injuria (damage without injury) did not apply because the act exceeded the bounds of a valid exercise of a right.

6. Abuse of Right by Contracting Parties

  • Petrophil Corporation v. Court of Appeals: A corporation arbitrarily terminated a hauling contract without giving the contractor an opportunity to be heard, purely as retaliation because the contractor sympathized with picketing workers.
  • Yalung, Sr. v. Magsaysay Films International Corp.: Movie post-production services produced a disastrous, unwatchable film. The defendants conveniently reassured the producers instead of correcting the defects, causing unquantifiable financial and reputational losses.

7. Deprivation of Water Service

  • MWSS v. Act Theater, Inc. / Metroheights Subdivision Homeowners Assoc. v. CMS Construction: Cutting off or disconnecting a customer's water supply without prior notice.

8. Abuse of Right by Educational Institutions

  • University of the East v. Jader: A law school allowed a student to attend graduation ceremonies, celebrate with a party, and enroll in bar review classes, only to belatedly inform him months later that he had failed a removal exam and could not take the bar.
  • Saint Louis University, Inc. v. Olairez: A university suddenly imposed harsher, punitive graduation requirements (Revised Comprehensive Oral and Written Examination) in the middle of the final school year.

9. Abuse of Right of Spouse

  • Navarro-Banaria v. Banaria: A wife promised to bring her frail, 90-year-old husband to a birthday party organized by his children from a prior marriage, but failed to bring him or send an explanation, leaving 200 guests stranded.

IV. Examples of Cases Where Abuse of Right WAS NOT Committed

When a party legitimately exercises a right in good faith, no action for damages will prosper, even if inconvenience or damage results (damnum absque injuria).

1. Absolute Rights

  • The refusal to enter into a contract is an absolute right; courts cannot inquire into the motives for refusing to contract.

2. Rights of Corporations and Directors

  • Mita Pardo de Tavera v. Philippine Tuberculosis Society: Board directors declared an executive position vacant in strict accordance with the society's constitution and bylaws.
  • Manzanal v. Illusorio: A non-stock corporation sent standard demand letters to a member for unpaid charges following routine enforcement practices.

3. Exercise of Rights Included in Ownership

  • Custodio v. Court of Appeals: A landowner fenced his property adjoining a highway, blocking inner tenants who had no easement of right of way.

4. Rights of Schools, Teachers, and Administrators

  • Virginia M. Andrade v. CA: Placing a teacher on a list of excess staff without undue motives.
  • University of San Carlos v. CA: Withholding graduation honors in accordance with established rules.
  • Garciano v. Court of Appeals: School administrators were not liable when a teacher voluntarily ceased teaching after colleagues threatened to resign upon her reinstatement; the colleagues were merely exercising free speech and dissent.

5. Right to Sue and Litigate

  • Antonio Diaz v. Davao Light and Power Co., Inc. / UCPB v. Basco: Commencing a lawsuit or submitting a case for prosecution does not per se make the action wrongful. The right to litigate serves as a peaceful escape valve in society.
  • Baron's Marketing Corporation v. Court of Appeals: A creditor rejected a debtor's offer to pay via post-dated checks/installments and instead filed a collection suit.

6. Legitimate Business Decisions of Contracting Parties

  • Dart Philippines, Inc. v. Spouses Calogcog: Refusing to renew a distributorship agreement based on legitimate audit findings.
  • Astroland Developers, Inc. v. GSIS: Unilaterally cancelling a project management agreement pursuant to an express contractual provision.
  • Development Bank of the Philippines v. CA: Extrajudicially foreclosing a mortgage upon default as authorized by contract.
  • Chevron Philippines, Inc. v. Mendoza: Denying a dealership application based on reasonable commercial grounds.

V. Acts Contra Bonus Mores (Article 21) & General Concepts

Article 21 expands civil liability to cover moral wrongs. It applies to acts that are not explicitly forbidden by positive law, ensuring that every imaginable wrong is compensable.

This source covers Acts Contra Bonus Mores (Article 21 of the New Civil Code) and its various application scenarios in Philippine tort law.

Below is a detailed breakdown of all the concepts, requisites, elements, legal rules, and author-provided examples discussed in this chapter:

1. Acts Contra Bonus Mores (General Concepts)

Article 21 expands civil liability beyond statutory violations by providing a legal remedy for moral wrongs that are not explicitly forbidden by positive law. It embodies the principle that "every good law draws its breath of life from morals".

⚖️
Legal Act
There is an act which is legal
🚫
Contrary to Morals
Contrary to morals, good customs, public order, or public policy
🎯
Intent to Injure
The act is done with intent to injure
  • Elements of Acts Contra Bonus Mores (Albenson Enterprises v. Court of Appeals):
    • There is an act which is legal;
    • The act is contrary to morals, good customs, public order, or public policy; and
    • The act is done with intent to injure.
  • Key Concepts & Definitions:
    • Morals & Good Customs: Generally accepted principles of morality that have received social and practical confirmation.
    • Public Order & Public Policy: Principles aimed at promoting the public weal, safety, health, and general well-being of society.

The terms morals, good customs, public order, and public policy are general legal concepts that elude precise definitions and are interpreted flexibly by courts depending on the circumstances of each case.

2. Breach of Promise to Marry

  • General Rule: A mere breach of promise to marry is not actionable per se because marriage is a fundamental human right involving personal autonomy and choice (Guevarra v. Banach).
  • Requisites / Exceptions (When Actionable under Arts. 19, 20, 21, or 2176):
    • Financial Damage Incurred: Formal wedding preparations were made, invitations printed/distributed, dresses bought, or reception venues booked (De Jesus v. Syquia; Wassmer v. Velez—where the groom walked out two days before the wedding).
    • Social Humiliation Caused: Formally setting a date and publicly backing out at the last minute.
    • Moral Seduction / Deceit: Where a promise to marry was a fraudulent device used to induce a person into sexual intercourse (Gashem Shookat Baksh v. CA; Bunag, Jr. v. CA).
  • When NOT Actionable:
    • Sexual intercourse resulting from voluntariness, mutual desire, or passion without moral seduction (Constantino v. Mendez; Tanjanco v. CA).
  • Inapplicability of In Pari Delicto:
    • In cases of moral seduction, the seduced party is not in pari delicto because her consent was procured through fraud and deceit (Baksh v. CA).
  • Author's Illustrative Case (Jhanna Guevarra et al. v. Jan Banach, 2021):
    • Facts: Banach, a German citizen, concealed his existing marriage and true identity, wooed Guevarra, and gave her ₱500,000 to buy a conjugal home. When Guevarra discovered his lies, she broke off the engagement. Banach sued for the return of the money.
    • Ruling: The Supreme Court ruled that because Banach acted in bad faith and deceit, he could not claim damages or force the return of the ₱500,000 gift under human relations rules.

3. Seduction and Sexual Assault

  • Seduction: Accomplishing sexual intercourse through deceit, enticement, superior power, or abuse of confidence is actionable under Article 21, even without a promise to marry.
  • Sexual Assault & Harassment: Includes crimes like rape, acts of lasciviousness, seduction under the Revised Penal Code, or non-criminal sexual coercion (e.g., a married man coercing a woman to yield to his lust in Quimiguingu v. Icao).

4. Breach of Marital Obligations

  • General Rule: Civil actions for damages between spouses solely for a breach of marital obligations are not permissible (Ty v. Court of Appeals).
  • Exception: Actionable when a spouse willfully deserts the other, denies consortium, or refuses to perform marital duties in a manner contrary to law, morals, or good customs (Pastor B. Tenchavez v. Vicenta F. Escaño—where the wife abandoned her husband and obtained an invalid foreign divorce).

5. Trespass and Deprivation of Property

5.01. Real Property Trespass

  • Unlawful invasion of real property or possession in bad faith (Art. 451).
  • Builder/Planter/Sower in Bad Faith: Directly liable for damages under Articles 448 and 456.
  • Builder/Planter/Sower in Good Faith: Not liable for damages under Article 448 (subject only to statutory option rules), unless negligent under Article 2176.

5.02. Personal Property Trespass

  • Depriving a person of movable property in bad faith or to force compliance (e.g., Magbanua v. IAC—where a landlord cut off tenants' water supply to force them off the land).
  • Unlawfully taking over business operations or vehicles (Cogeo-Cubao Operators and Drivers Assoc. v. CA).

5.03. Disconnection of Utility Services

  • Unjustified disconnection of electricity or gas service without mandatory prior notice required by rules constitutes an actionable tort under Article 21 and Abuse of Right (Meralco v. CA; Ardiente v. Pastorfide).

6. Abortion and Wrongful Death

  • Civil Liability: Damages are recoverable against a physician or person who intentionally or negligently performs an abortion (Geluz v. Court of Appeals).
  • Requisite for Moral Damages: The plaintiff (such as a spouse/parent) must present factual proof of actual distress or mental anguish caused by the loss of the unborn child.

7. Illegal Dismissal

  • General Rule: Employers have a legal right to terminate employees for just or authorized causes under labor laws.
  • When Actionable under Arts. 19 & 21:
    • When dismissal is carried out in an antisocial, oppressive, or abusive manner (Quisaba v. Sta. Ines-Melale; Globe Mackay v. CA).
    • When dismissal is based on false imputations or fabricated grounds (e.g., falsely claiming serious financial losses in AHSI v. NLRC).

8. Malicious Prosecution & Abuse of Process

8.01. Definition & Bases

Instituting criminal or civil proceedings maliciously and without probable cause for the purpose of vexation or injury.

8.02. Requisites / Elements of Malicious Prosecution (Albenson Enterprises; Drilon v. CA):

1
PROSECUTION & ACQUITTAL
Defendant was prosecutor & action ended with acquittal
2
NO PROBABLE CAUSE
Prosecutor acted without probable cause
3
LEGAL MALICE
Impelled by sinister motive or intent to vex/injure
  1. The fact of prosecution and that the defendant was himself the prosecutor, and the action was finally terminated with an acquittal;
  2. That in bringing the action, the prosecutor acted without probable cause; and
  3. The prosecutor was actuated or impelled by legal malice.
  • Key Definitions:
    • Malice: An inexcusable intent to injure, oppress, vex, annoy, or humiliate (Pro Line Sports Center v. CA; Manila Gas Corp. v. CA).
    • Probable Cause: Facts and circumstances that excite belief in a reasonable mind that the person charged is guilty.
    • Acquittal: Includes a final court dismissal or a prosecutor's dismissal during preliminary investigation.
    • Civil Cases: Applies equally to baseless civil suits filed strictly to harass or humiliate (Inhelder Corp. v. CA).

8.03. Abuse of Process

Actionable under Articles 2176 and 26 when gross negligence or improper process causes false arrest or indictment (Gregorio v. CA—where a non-signatory was wrongly arrested under B.P. 22 due to failure to verify identity/address).

9. Public Humiliation

Actionable under Article 21 when positive acts directed against a person cause public embarrassment, humiliation, or personal affront.

  • Examples of Actionable Public Humiliation:
    • Slapping someone in public (Patricio v. Leviste; Maria Ford v. CA—slapping a public school teacher/COMELEC deputy at a polling station).
    • False shoplifting accusations accompanied by public verbal abuse and accusatory letters sent to an employer (Grand Union Supermarket v. Espino; California Clothing, Inc. v. Quiñones).
  • Counter-Example (No Liability):
    • Hotel Nikko Manila Garden v. Reyes ("Amay Bisaya"): Politely and discreetly asking an uninvited guest to leave an exclusive party without rudeness or bad faith does not constitute abusive conduct.
