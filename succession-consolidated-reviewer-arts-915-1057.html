I. GENERAL PRINCIPLES & ESSENTIAL REQUISITES OF DISINHERITANCE (ARTICLES 915–917)

A. Nature and Scope of Disinheritance

Under Article 915 of the Civil Code, disinheritance is the testamentary act by which a testator, for a just and legal cause expressly stated in a valid will, deprives a compulsory heir of his or her legitime. Because compulsory heirs succeed to their legitime by operation of law regardless of the testator's wishes, the testator cannot arbitrarily strip them of this reserved portion; deprivation is permissible only when the compulsory heir commits a grave statutory offense against the testator.

Conversely, voluntary or testamentary heirs (such as friends, strangers, or collateral relatives like brothers and sisters) cannot be the subject of disinheritance. Because voluntary heirs inherit solely out of the testator's liberality from the free portion of the estate, a testator who wishes to exclude them simply omits their names from the will or revokes an existing testamentary institution.

B. The Seven (7) Essential Requisites of a Valid Disinheritance

Pursuant to Articles 915, 916, and 917 of the Civil Code, a valid and effective disinheritance requires the concurrence of seven strict requisites:

  1. It must be made in a valid will (Art. 916): Disinheritance is strictly a testamentary disposition. It cannot be effected through an inter vivos public instrument, an affidavit, a deed of disinheritance, or an oral declaration. Furthermore, the will itself must comply with all extrinsic formalities required by law (under Articles 804 to 814) and must be duly admitted to probate.
  2. It must be made expressly, identifying the disinherited heir: The testator must unequivocally manifest the intent to disinherit a specifically identified compulsory heir. Tacit omission does not constitute disinheritance; rather, the unintentional or unexplained omission of a compulsory heir in the direct line results in preterition under Article 854.
  3. It must be based on a cause expressly specified by law (Art. 915): The grounds enumerated in Articles 919, 920, and 921 of the Civil Code are exclusive (numerus clausus). A testator cannot invent personal grounds based on whim, caprice, or petty family grievances.
  4. The legal cause must be expressly stated in the will itself (Art. 916): It is insufficient for a legal ground to exist in fact; the testator must explicitly articulate the specific statutory cause within the text of the will so the probate or settlement court can evaluate its legal sufficiency.
  5. The cause must be certain and true, and existing at the time of execution (Arts. 916–917): The offense or ground relied upon must already exist at the time the testator executes the will. It cannot be predicated on a future, speculative, or conditional offense that has not yet occurred.
  6. It must be unconditional: Because disinheritance is a punitive sanction for an offense already committed against the testator, the deprivation of the legitime cannot be made to depend upon the fulfillment of a suspensive or resolutory condition.
  7. It must be total and not partial: The legitime cannot be partially forfeited. Because statutory grounds for disinheritance constitute grave offenses of ingratitude against the testator, the law does not permit the testator to quantify the degree of offense and deduct a mere fraction (such as one-third or one-half) of the heir's legitime. The testator must either forgive the offense entirely or disinherit the compulsory heir completely.

C. Conditional Disinheritance (Void) vs. Conditional Revocation of Disinheritance (Valid)

While the act of disinheritance itself cannot be subjected to a condition, Philippine succession law distinguishes between a conditional disinheritance (which is void) and a conditional revocation or lifting of a disinheritance (which is valid). When a testator disinherits a compulsory heir based on an existing, actual statutory ground, the disinheritance is immediately valid and unconditional; if the testator subsequently attaches a condition allowing the heir to regain his or her successional rights upon reforming or making amends, the condition attaches not to the disinheritance per se, but to the revocation of the disinheritance (or the testator's conditional pardon).

  • Application Example (Unapproved Marriage as a Future/Conditional Ground — VOID): Suppose a father states in his will: "I hereby disinherit my daughter if she marries a man below our family's socioeconomic bracket." This disinheritance is void on two independent grounds:
    1. Marrying a person against the parents' socioeconomic preferences is not among the exclusive grounds enumerated in Article 919 of the Civil Code; and
    2. The ground is a future, contingent event rather than an existing cause at the time of the execution of the will, violating the rule that disinheritance must be based on an actual, completed offense.
  • Professor's Illustration (Conditional Lifting of Disinheritance — VALID): A testator provides in his will: "I hereby disinherit my daughter because she is currently living a dishonorable and disgraceful life. If, however, she mends her ways, leaves that lifestyle, returns to my house, and serves me well, this disinheritance shall be revoked and lifted." This testamentary provision is valid. The disinheritance itself is anchored on an existing, statutory ground under Article 919(7). The condition attaches not to the imposition of the disinheritance, but to its revocation, effectively operating as a valid conditional pardon by the testator.

D. Burden of Proof in Contested Disinheritance (Article 917)

Under Article 917 of the Civil Code, if the disinherited compulsory heir denies or contradicts the truth of the cause stated in the will, the burden of proof (onus probandi) rests upon the other heirs of the testator who seek to sustain the disinheritance. The disinherited heir is not required to prove a negative or establish his or her innocence. Because the other heirs stand to benefit financially from the forfeiture of the disinherited heir's legitime, they must affirmatively establish by preponderance of evidence that the statutory ground alleged by the testator actually occurred.

II. SPECIFIC GROUNDS FOR DISINHERITANCE OF CHILDREN AND DESCENDANTS (ARTICLE 919)

Article 919 of the Civil Code establishes eight (8) exclusive causes for the disinheritance of children and descendants, whether legitimate or illegitimate.

1. Attempt Against the Life of the Testator, Spouse, Descendants, or Ascendants — Art. 919(1)

A child or descendant may be disinherited when he or she has been found guilty of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her spouse, descendants, or ascendants.

  • Three Indispensable Requisites:
    1. The compulsory heir committed an intentional felony against the life of the testator, the testator's spouse, the testator's descendant (e.g., a sibling or child of the heir), or the testator's ascendant (e.g., the testator's parent). (Reckless imprudence resulting in homicide or physical injuries does not qualify because there is no intent to kill).
    2. The felony reached at least the attempted stage under Article 6 of the Revised Penal Code (consummated or frustrated stages are necessarily included).
    3. The heir was prosecuted and found guilty by final judgment in a criminal proceeding.
  • Effect of Executive Clemency: If the convicted child or descendant is subsequently granted an absolute presidential pardon, the testator may still validly disinherit him or her. An executive pardon blots out the criminal liability and penal consequences against the State, but it cannot erase the personal ingratitude and moral offense already committed against the testator.
  • Effect of Non-Prosecution (The Maltreatment Fallback): If the child attempts to kill the testator, but the family refuses to file a criminal case to avoid public scandal, the child cannot be disinherited under Article 919(1) because the statutory element of being "found guilty" is absent. However, the testator may validly disinherit the child under Article 919(6) for maltreatment of the testator by deed, which does not require a prior criminal conviction! (Note: If the unprosecuted attempt was against the testator's spouse, ascendant, or other descendant rather than the testator personally, Art. 919(6) will not apply because maltreatment under Art. 919(6) must be directed against the testator himself).

2. Groundless Accusation of a Crime Punishable by Six Years or More — Art. 919(2)

Disinheritance is warranted when a child or descendant has accused the testator of a crime for which the law prescribes imprisonment for six years or more (prision correccional in its maximum period to reclusion perpetua), if the accusation has been found groundless.

  • Scope of "Accusation": The term encompasses filing a criminal complaint, acting as a private complainant, or voluntarily testifying as a prosecution witness against the testator.
  • Distinction Between Acquittal on Reasonable Doubt and a "Groundless" Accusation: The mere acquittal of the testator does not automatically render the accusation "groundless." If the criminal court acquits the testator merely on the ground that the prosecution failed to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt (insufficiency of evidence) or due to a procedural technicality, Article 919(2) is not satisfied. To justify disinheritance, the judgment of acquittal must positively declare or unmistakably demonstrate that the accusation was false, malicious, or fabricated—meaning the crime was not committed at all, or the child knew the testator was innocent.

3. Conviction of Adultery or Concubinage with the Testator's Spouse — Art. 919(3)

A child or descendant may be disinherited when he or she has been convicted of adultery or concubinage with the spouse of the testator.

  • Requirement of Final Conviction: Just as in the first ground, a criminal prosecution and final judgment of conviction for adultery (Article 333, Revised Penal Code) or concubinage (Article 334, Revised Penal Code) are indispensable.
  • Application to Step-Parents: This ground does not necessarily involve biological incest. It frequently applies where the testator's first marriage was dissolved by death or declaration of nullity, the testator contracted a subsequent marriage, and the testator's child from the prior marriage engaged in an illicit sexual relationship with the stepmother or stepfather resulting in a criminal conviction.

4. Fraud, Violence, Intimidation, or Undue Influence in Making or Changing a Will — Art. 919(4)

A child or descendant may be disinherited when, by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence, he or she causes the testator to make a will or to change one already made. This ground penalizes any coercive or deceitful interference with the testator's testamentary freedom, even if the coerced will was subsequently revoked and replaced by a new, freely executed will containing the disinheritance.

5. Refusal Without Justifiable Cause to Support the Testator — Art. 919(5)

A child or descendant may be disinherited for refusal without justifiable cause to support the parent or ascendant who disinherits such child or descendant.

  • Four Requisites:
    1. The testator-ascendant was in actual need of support under the Family Code;
    2. A formal or extrajudicial demand for support was made upon the child or descendant;
    3. The child or descendant had the financial means and capacity to provide support (otherwise, the refusal has a justifiable cause); and
    4. The child or descendant unjustifiably refused to provide the requested support.

6. Maltreatment of the Testator by Word or Deed — Art. 919(6)

Maltreatment of the testator by word or by deed by the child or descendant is a sufficient cause for disinheritance.

  • Absence of Conviction Requirement: Unlike Article 919(1), maltreatment requires no criminal conviction.
  • Scope of Verbal and Physical Maltreatment: Maltreatment "by deed" covers any physical violence, assault, or unprosecuted attempt against the life of the parent. Maltreatment "by word" encompasses grave oral defamation, vicious cursing, public humiliation, or broadcasting vile insults and derogatory attacks against the parent on social media platforms.
  • Exclusive to Descendants: Notice that maltreatment by word or deed is a ground to disinherit a child/descendant (Art. 919[6]), not a parent/ascendant under Article 920 (because parents exercise disciplinary authority over children, though excessive harshness by a parent may cause loss of parental authority under Art. 920[6]).

7. Leading a Dishonorable or Disgraceful Life — Art. 919(7)

A child or descendant may be disinherited when he or she leads a dishonorable or disgraceful life. The phrase "leads a life" implies continuity, habituality, and permanence of conduct that brings shame and moral disrepute to the family name, rather than an isolated indiscretion. Furthermore, this ground is not limited to sexual immorality; it includes habitual criminality, chronic drug trafficking, or notorious public scandal.

8. Conviction of a Crime Carrying the Penalty of Civil Interdiction — Art. 919(8)

Disinheritance is authorized upon the child's or descendant's conviction of a crime which carries with it the penalty of civil interdiction.

  • Crimes Carrying Civil Interdiction: Under Articles 34 and 41 of the Revised Penal Code, civil interdiction is an accessory penalty that attaches by operation of law to the principal penalties of death (when not executed), reclusion perpetua, and reclusion temporal (imprisonment of 12 years and 1 day to 40 years).
  • Offense Need Not Be Committed Against the Testator: Unlike Article 919(1), where the attempt on life must be directed against the testator or specific close family members, the crime under Article 919(8) may be committed against a total stranger. Because crimes punishable by reclusion temporal or reclusion perpetua (such as murder, robbery with homicide, or plunder) are exceptionally heinous, final conviction thereof severs the moral bond between parent and child and taints the family honor, empowering the testator to disinherit the offender.

III. SPECIFIC GROUNDS FOR DISINHERITANCE OF PARENTS AND ASCENDANTS (ARTICLE 920)

When the testator has no surviving children or descendants, the parents and ascendants become compulsory heirs. Article 920 of the Civil Code enumerates eight (8) exclusive grounds by which a descendant-testator may disinherit his or her parents or ascendants.

1. Abandonment of Children vs. Inducement to Immorality or Attempt Against Virtue — Art. 920(1)

Parents or ascendants may be disinherited when they have abandoned their children or induced their daughters to live a corrupt or immoral life, or attempted against their virtue. This provision contains three distinct acts with different gender applications:

  • Clause 1: Abandonment of Children (Gender-Neutral): "When the parents have abandoned their children" applies equally to both male and female descendants (sons, daughters, grandsons, or granddaughters). Abandonment signifies the total deprivation of basic parental care, financial support, and moral guidance during the child's minority, rather than mere temporary physical separation.
  • Clause 2: Inducement to Live a Corrupt or Immoral Life (Female-Specific): "Induced their daughters to live a corrupt or immoral life" (e.g., forcing a daughter into prostitution) applies strictly to daughters and granddaughters (female descendants).
  • Clause 3: Attempt Against Their Virtue (Female-Specific): "Attempted against their virtue" likewise applies to female descendants and encompasses sexual offenses committed by an ascendant against a daughter or granddaughter, such as attempted rape, acts of lasciviousness, or sexual abuse, even without a prior criminal conviction.

2. The Five (5) Overlapping Grounds Between Descendants (Art. 919) and Ascendants (Art. 920)

Five grounds under Article 920 mirror the exact provisions of Article 919, operating reciprocally when the descendant is the testator and the ascendant is the compulsory heir:

  • Article 920(2): Conviction of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her spouse, descendants, or ascendants (mirrors Art. 919[1]).
  • Article 920(3): Groundless accusation of a crime punishable by imprisonment for six years or more (mirrors Art. 919[2]).
  • Article 920(4): Conviction of adultery or concubinage with the spouse of the testator (mirrors Art. 919[3]).
  • Article 920(5): Causing the testator by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence to make a will or change one already made (mirrors Art. 919[4]).
  • Article 920(7): Refusal without justifiable cause to support the children or descendants (mirrors Art. 919[5]).

3. Loss of Parental Authority — Art. 920(6)

A parent or ascendant may be disinherited for the loss of parental authority for causes specified in the Code (now governed by the Family Code, such as judicial deprivation of parental authority due to excessive harshness, corrupting orders, or conviction of a crime carrying civil interdiction). To constitute a ground for disinheritance, the loss of parental authority must be attributable to the fault or culpability of the parent, not due to benign statutory termination such as the child reaching the age of majority (emancipation). (Note: Even if parental authority is later recovered, the offense was already committed and remains a valid ground unless there is bilateral reconciliation under Art. 922).

4. Attempt by One Parent Against the Life of the Other — Art. 920(8) vs. Art. 920(2)

Under Article 920(8), a child or descendant may disinherit a parent for "an attempt by one of the parents against the life of the other, unless there has been a reconciliation between them."

⚡ EXAM TRAP ALERT: THE CRITICAL DISTINCTION BETWEEN ART. 920(8) AND ART. 920(2) ON PARENTAL RECONCILIATION Why does the Civil Code have both Article 920(2) (attempt against the life of an ascendant) and Article 920(8) (attempt by one parent against the life of the other)? Because of Conviction and Who Must Reconcile! * Situation A — NO Final Conviction (Art. 920[8] Governs): If the father attempts to kill the mother, the child can disinherit the father under Article 920(8) even if no criminal case was filed and there was no conviction. However, because the primary offended party is the mother, if the mother subsequently forgives and reconciles with the father, the child loses the right to disinherit the father under Article 920(8). * Situation B — WITH Final Conviction (Art. 920[2] Governs): If the father attempts to kill the mother (who is an ascendant of the child-testator), is prosecuted, and is convicted by final judgment, the child's right to disinherit the father falls squarely under Article 920(2). Once a final conviction is handed down, a subsequent reconciliation between the mother and the father does NOT bar the child from disinheriting the father under Article 920(2)! Under Article 922, only a personal reconciliation between the offender (the father) and the testator himself (the child) can extinguish the child's right to disinherit under Article 920(2).

  • Professor's Illustration (Parental Reconciliation After Conviction): A father attempts to murder the testator's mother. A criminal case for frustrated parricide is filed, and the father is convicted by final judgment. While serving his sentence, the mother forgives the father and fully reconciles with him. Can the child-testator still validly disinherit the father? Yes. While parental reconciliation extinguishes the ground under Article 920(8) (which applies when there is no conviction), the father's final conviction triggers Article 920(2) (conviction of an attempt against the life of the testator's ascendant). Only a personal reconciliation between the father and the child-testator under Article 922 can defeat a disinheritance anchored on Article 920(2).

IV. DISINHERITANCE OF A SPOUSE, RECONCILIATION, REPRESENTATION, AND INEFFECTUAL DISINHERITANCE (ARTICLES 918, 921–923)

A. Specific Grounds for Disinheritance of a Spouse (Article 921)

Under Article 921 of the Civil Code, a testator may validly disinherit his or her surviving spouse upon any of six (6) exclusive statutory causes:

  1. Conviction of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her descendants, or ascendants (Art. 921[1]).
  2. Groundless accusation against the testator of a crime for which the law prescribes imprisonment for six years or more (Art. 921[2]).
  3. Causing the testator by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence to make a will or to change one already made (Art. 921[3]).
  4. When the spouse has given cause for legal separation (Art. 921[4]).
  5. When the spouse has given grounds for the loss of parental authority (Art. 921[5]).
  6. Unjustifiable refusal to support the children or the other spouse (Art. 921[6]).

B. Incorporation of Family Code Grounds for Legal Separation (Art. 921[4] + Art. 55, Family Code)

Under Article 921(4), a final judicial decree of legal separation is not required to disinherit a spouse; it is sufficient that the offending spouse "has given cause" for legal separation.

  • Why a Decree is Not Needed: If a final decree of legal separation has already been granted by the Family Court prior to the testator's death, the offending spouse is disqualified from inheriting by operation of law under Article 63(4) of the Family Code without any need for testamentary disinheritance. Thus, Article 921(4) is specifically utilized when the testator chooses not to file (or has not yet completed) a petition for legal separation, but executes a will disinheriting the guilty spouse based on any of the ten (10) grounds under Article 55 of the Family Code:
    1. Repeated physical violence or grossly abusive conduct directed against the testator, a common child, or a child of the testator (e.g., a spouse who habitually batters or physically abuses the testator).
    2. Physical violence or moral pressure to compel the testator to change religious or political affiliation.
    3. Attempt of the respondent spouse to corrupt or induce the testator, a common child, or a child of the testator, to engage in prostitution, or connivance in such corruption or inducement.
    4. Final judgment sentencing the respondent spouse to imprisonment of more than six years, even if pardoned.
    5. Drug addiction or habitual alcoholism of the respondent spouse.
    6. Lesbianism or homosexuality of the respondent spouse.

D. The Doctrine of Reconciliation (Article 922)

Under Article 922 of the Civil Code, a subsequent reconciliation between the offender and the offended person (the testator) produces two automatic legal effects:

  1. If made before the execution of a will, it deprives the testator of the right to disinherit the offending heir for that specific offense.
  2. If made after the execution of a will containing a valid disinheritance, it renders the disinheritance ineffectual by operation of law, without requiring the testator to execute a new will or codicil revoking the disinheritance.
  • Bilateral Nature of Reconciliation vs. Unilateral Pardon: For Article 922 to operate, there must be a genuine bilateral reconciliation, not a mere unilateral pardon or general expression of forgiveness. Reconciliation requires:
    1. An offer of forgiveness by the offended testator;
    2. An acceptance of that forgiveness by the offending compulsory heir; and
    3. An actual restoration of the former familial relationship and harmony between the parties.
  • Professor's Illustration (Deathbed Forgiveness vs. True Reconciliation): A testator who previously executed a valid will disinheriting an erring son lies on his deathbed. Fearing spiritual judgment as he is about to expire, the testator declares before witnesses: "I forgive everyone who has wronged me, including the children I disinherited in my will." The testator dies hours later without the disinherited son accepting the pardon or resuming their filial relationship. The disinheritance remains valid and effective. A unilateral, general deathbed pardon does not satisfy Article 922, which requires mutual reconciliation and the actual restoration of former relations between the offender and the offended testator. (Contrast this with Article 1033 on Unworthiness, discussed in Module 4, where an express written pardon or implied testamentary institution is required when no disinheritance was made).

E. Representation of the Disinherited Heir (Article 923)

Under Article 923 of the Civil Code, although a validly disinherited compulsory heir forfeits his or her legitime, the children and descendants of the person disinherited shall take his or her place and shall preserve the rights of compulsory heirs with respect to the legitime.

  • Underlying Rationale: Grounded in the principle that the guilt of the parent must not be visited upon the innocent child (peccata patrum non nocent filiis), the law elevates the disinherited heir's descendants by right of representation.
  • Lineal Scope of Representation in Disinheritance: Because representation takes place only in the direct descending line and never in the ascending line (Art. 972), only disinherited children/descendants can be represented by their own children/descendants under Article 923. A disinherited parent/ascendant or a disinherited surviving spouse can never be represented.
  • Extent of Representation & Disqualification from Usufruct/Administration: The representatives are entitled only to the legitime that the disinherited parent would have received (unless the testator also left the free portion undisposed, in which case the representatives also represent the disinherited parent in the intestate distribution of the undisposed free portion). Furthermore, the disinherited parent is strictly barred from enjoying the usufruct or administration of the property that constitutes the legitime inherited by his or her children.

F. Legal Effects of Imperfect or Ineffectual Disinheritance (Article 918) vs. Preterition (Article 854)

Under Article 918 of the Civil Code, a disinheritance is ineffectual (imperfect or defective) when it is made:

  1. Without expression of the cause;
  2. For a cause the truth of which, if contradicted, is not proved; or
  3. For a cause which is not one of those set forth in the Civil Code.
  • Effect on the Institution of Heirs (Partial vs. Total Annulment): Ineffectual disinheritance annuls the institution of heirs insofar as it may prejudice the person disinherited, meaning the invalidly disinherited compulsory heir must first be given his or her full legitime; "but the devises and legacies and other testamentary dispositions shall be valid to such extent as will not impair said legitime."
    • Crucial Contrast with Preterition (Art. 854): In preterition (total omission of a compulsory heir in the direct line), the institution of heirs is totally annulled (causing the entire estate, except inofficious legacies/devises, to fall into intestacy). In imperfect disinheritance (Art. 918), the institution of heirs is annulled only partially—strictly to the extent necessary to restore the prejudiced heir's legitime. The institution of heirs over the free portion remains valid!
  • When an Invalidly Disinherited Heir Receives MORE Than the Legitime: While an invalidly disinherited heir is always guaranteed at least his or her legitime, he or she will receive more than the legitime (i.e., a share in the free portion via intestacy) if:
    1. The testator failed to dispose of the free portion (e.g., the will only contained the invalid disinheritance or specific legacies that did not exhaust the free portion); or
    2. The institution of heirs was totally annulled due to the concurrent preterition of another compulsory heir in the direct line!

  • Facts: Testator X dies leaving four legitimate children (A, B, C, and D) and a net estate of ₱120 Million. In his will, X states: "I disinherit my son D. I institute my children A and B as my sole and universal heirs." (Notice C was completely omitted).
  • Legal Analysis & Computation:
    1. The disinheritance of D is ineffectual under Article 918 because no legal cause was stated.
    2. Simultaneously, the complete omission of C—a compulsory heir in the direct line—constitutes preterition under Article 854, which totally annuls the institution of A and B.
    3. Because both the disinheritance of D and the institution of A and B are void, the entire ₱120 Million estate passes by intestate succession, dividing equally among A, B, C, and D at ₱30 Million each (exceeding D's strict legitime of ₱15 Million).
2. Professor's Illustration — Scenario 2: Invalid Disinheritance Without Preterition
  • Facts: Assume the same facts (estate of ₱120 Million; legitimate children A, B, C, and D), except X's will states: "I disinherit my son D. I give my entire estate to my children A, B, and C."
  • Legal Analysis & Computation:
    1. Here, there is no preterition because all four children were mentioned in the will.
    2. The disinheritance of D is invalid under Article 918 for lack of a stated cause, which annuls the institution of A, B, and C only insofar as it prejudices D's legitime.
    3. The collective legitime of the four legitimate children is one-half of the estate (₱60 Million), giving A, B, C, and D ₱15 Million each.
    4. Because the institution of A, B, and C over the ₱60 Million free portion remains valid, they divide the free portion equally (₱20 Million each).
    5. Final Shares: A, B, and C receive ₱35 Million each (₱15M legitime + ₱20M free portion = ₱105M total), while D receives only his strict legitime of ₱15 Million.
3. Professor's Illustration — Scenario 3: Valid Disinheritance With and Without Descendants
  • Facts: Assume the same facts as Scenario 2 (estate of ₱120 Million; will gives the entire estate to A, B, and C), except that X validly disinherited D based on an expressly stated, proven ground under Article 919.
  • Sub-Case 3A (If D has NO children):
    • D receives ₱0. Because D has no descendants to represent him and the entire estate was instituted to A, B, and C, A, B, and C divide the entire ₱120 Million estate equally, receiving ₱40 Million each (₱20M legitime in their own right + ₱20M free portion).

Question 1.1: Unprosecuted Attempt on Life vs. Maltreatment; Imperfect Disinheritance

Don Lorenzo executed a notarial will containing the following clauses: (1) "I hereby disinherit my legitimate son, Marco, because last year he tried to stab me to death with a kitchen knife during a heated argument, although out of parental pity I never filed a criminal complaint against him"; and (2) "I hereby disinherit my legitimate daughter, Sofia, of one-half (1/2) of her legitime because she eloped with a jobless drummer against my wishes. I leave my entire estate of ₱90 Million to my only other legitimate child, Lucas." Upon Don Lorenzo's death, both Marco and Sofia contested their disinheritances. How should the ₱90 Million net estate be distributed among Marco, Sofia, and Lucas?

  • A Answer: The ₱90 Million estate should be distributed as follows: Marco receives ₱0 (validly disinherited), Sofia receives ₱15 Million (her strict legitime due to imperfect disinheritance), and Lucas receives ₱75 Million (₱15 Million legitime + ₱15 Million vacated legitime of Marco + ₱45 Million free portion; or ₱30M legitime + ₱45M free portion).
  • L Legal Basis: Under Article 919(1) of the Civil Code, disinheritance based on an attempt against the life of the testator requires a final judgment of conviction. However, under Article 919(6), maltreatment of the testator by word or by deed is an independent ground for disinheriting a descendant that does not require a criminal conviction. Conversely, under Articles 915 to 918 of the Civil Code, a disinheritance must be total (never partial) and must be based on a cause specified by law; otherwise, it is an imperfect or ineffectual disinheritance under Article 918, which annuls the institution of heirs only insofar as it prejudices the compulsory heir's legitime.
  • A Application:
    1. As to Marco: Although Marco was never convicted—barring application of Article 919(1)—his physical act of attempting to stab his father constitutes maltreatment of the testator by deed under Article 919(6), which was expressly stated in the will and requires no criminal conviction. Thus, Marco's disinheritance is valid, and since he has no children to represent him under Article 923, he gets ₱0.
    2. As to Sofia: Sofia's disinheritance is void/ineffectual under Article 918 for two reasons: (a) disinheritance cannot be partial (depriving her of only 1/2 of her legitime is prohibited), and (b) eloping against parental wishes is not a statutory ground under Article 919. Under Article 918, Lucas's institution to the entire estate is annulled only to the extent that Sofia's legitime is impaired.
    3. Mathematical Computation: The collective legitime of the legitimate children is 1/2 of ₱90M = ₱45 Million. Had Marco not been disinherited, the three children would have had a baseline legitime of ₱15M each; or, with Marco validly disinherited without issue, Sofia and Lucas are the two remaining compulsory heirs entitled to the ₱45M collective legitime (or Sofia recovers her prejudiced legitime while Lucas absorbs the rest of the estate under the will). Even under the strict three-child baseline (₱15M legitime each), Lucas takes the entire ₱45M Free Portion as sole instituted heir plus his legitime (and Marco's vacated share), while Sofia recovers her unimpaired legitime. (Note: Whether Sofia's legitime is computed at ₱15M [based on 3 children] or ₱22.5M [based on 2 remaining children in their own right per Art. 1021], state both numbers clearly on the exam).
  • C Conclusion: Marco is validly disinherited under Article 919(6), while Sofia's imperfect disinheritance under Article 918 entitles her to recover her legitime without annulling Lucas's institution over the free portion.

Question 1.2: Parental Attempt on Life, Conviction, and Reconciliation (Art. 920[2] vs. Art. 920[8])

Carlos died unmarried and childless, leaving a ₱50 Million estate and survived by his legitimate parents, Julio and Elena. Two years before Carlos died, Julio shot Elena in a fit of jealousy. Julio was prosecuted and convicted by final judgment of frustrated parricide. Six months later, Elena visited Julio in prison, forgave him, and fully reconciled with him. Nevertheless, Carlos executed a valid will disinheriting his father Julio on the ground that Julio was convicted of attempting to kill Carlos's mother Elena, and instituting Elena as sole heir. Julio contests the disinheritance, invoking his reconciliation with Elena under Article 920(8). Rule on Julio's contention.

  • A Answer: Julio's contention is untenable; his disinheritance by Carlos remains valid and effective.
  • L Legal Basis: Under Article 920(8) of the Civil Code, an attempt by one parent against the life of the other—even without a criminal conviction—is a ground for disinheritance by a child, unless there has been a reconciliation between the parents. However, under Article 920(2), when the parent has been found guilty (convicted by final judgment) of an attempt against the life of the testator's ascendant (the mother), it constitutes a separate, independent ground for disinheritance that is extinguished only by a bilateral reconciliation between the offender (Julio) and the testator himself (Carlos) under Article 922.
  • A Application: Because Julio was convicted by final judgment of frustrated parricide against Carlos's mother Elena (an ascendant of Carlos), Carlos's disinheritance of Julio is anchored on Article 920(2), not merely Article 920(8). Consequently, the reconciliation between Elena and Julio cannot erase the offense as against Carlos. Since Carlos himself never reconciled with Julio under Article 922, the disinheritance stands.
  • C Conclusion: Julio is validly disinherited and receives nothing; Elena inherits the entire ₱50 Million estate.

I. NATURE AND STATUTORY CAUSES OF LEGAL OR INTESTATE SUCCESSION (ARTICLE 960)

A. Fundamental Nature of Intestate Succession

Legal or intestate succession is the mode of transmission of hereditary rights established by operation of law in default of a valid and operative testamentary disposition. It rests upon the presumed will of the decedent, allocating the hereditary estate to relatives in accordance with natural affection, blood proximity, and familial duty.

  1. Absence of a Free Portion in Pure Intestacy: In pure intestate succession, the concepts of "legitime" and "free portion" do not strictly operate as separate dispositive buckets because the decedent left no last will and testament. The entire net hereditary estate is distributed completely among the surviving legal or intestate heirs according to the proportions fixed by law, leaving no undisposed remainder.
  2. Preservation of Compulsory Shares (Non-Impairment Check): Although there is no formal free portion in pure intestacy, where compulsory heirs concur in intestate succession (particularly legitimate and illegitimate descendants), the law mandates a preliminary check against their statutory legitimes to guarantee that the resulting intestate distribution does not impair the minimum shares guaranteed to them by law.
  3. The State as Ultimate Default Intestate Heir and the Fifth-Degree Collateral Rule (Arts. 1010–1014):
    • Impossibility of Total Absence of Intestate Heirs: Intestate succession can never fail for lack of an heir. Under Articles 1011 to 1014, the State (humorously referred to by the Professor as our "kabalikat sa kaunlaran") stands at the very end of both the regular and irregular orders of intestate succession as the ultimate default legal heir.
    • The Fifth-Degree Collateral Limit (Art. 1010): The right to inherit ab intestato in the collateral line does not extend beyond the fifth (5th) civil degree of relationship. If a decedent dies without any surviving descendants, ascendants, illegitimate children, surviving spouse, or collateral relatives within the fifth civil degree, the entire estate escheats to the State (for the benefit of public schools and public charitable institutions under Art. 1013).
    • Professor's / Dean Navarro's Illustration (5th-Degree Collateral Computation): Decedent X dies intestate without any closer relatives, survived only by Y, the legitimate child of X's first cousin (anak ng pinsang buo). Can Y inherit from X before the State steps in? Yes. Under civil degree computation (Arts. 963–966): X up to his parent is 1st degree; up to his grandparent (common ancestor) is 2nd degree; down to his uncle/aunt is 3rd degree; down to his first cousin is 4th degree; and down to the child of his first cousin (Y) is the 5th civil degree. Thus, Y is the farthest collateral relative permitted by Article 1010 to inherit ab intestato.

B. The Six (6) Causes of Legal or Intestate Succession (Article 960 & Doctrinal Additions)

Under Article 960 of the Civil Code, supplemented by civil law doctrine, legal or intestate succession takes place in six instances:

  1. Absence, Nullity, or Revocation of a Will (Art. 960[1]): When a person dies without a will, or with a void will (e.g., lacking essential formal requisites under Arts. 804–808), or with a will that has subsequently lost its validity (such as a will validly revoked under Art. 830 without a subsequent will taking its place). (Note: "Lost its validity" refers to loss of efficacy such as revocation, because a will is never valid at execution and invalid later—validity is tested at execution).
  2. Lack of Institution or Partial Disposition (Art. 960[2]): When the will does not institute an heir to, or does not dispose of, all the property belonging to the testator. In such case, legal succession shall take place only with respect to the property of which the testator has not disposed, resulting in mixed succession.
  3. Failure of a Suspensive Condition (Art. 960[3]): If the suspensive condition attached to the institution of an heir does not happen or is not fulfilled, rendering the conditional testamentary institution ineffective.
  4. Predecease, Incapacity, or Repudiation Without Substitute or Accretion (Art. 960[3] & [4]): When the instituted heir predeceases the testator, or is incapacitated to succeed (Art. 960[4]), or repudiates the inheritance (Art. 960[3]), and no substitution has been designated and no right of accretion (or representation) takes place.
  5. Fulfillment of a Resolutory Condition (Doctrinal Addition / Art. 871): Upon the fulfillment of a resolutory condition attached to the institution of an heir, which extinguishes the testamentary right and triggers intestate distribution as to that portion.
  6. Expiration of a Resolutory Term (Doctrinal Addition / Art. 885): Upon the expiration of a resolutory term or period attached to the institution of an heir, after which the property passes to the legal heirs unless otherwise directed by the testator.
  • Professor's Illustration (Combined Causes of Intestacy): Testator A, whose sole surviving relative is his brother B, executes a valid will devising a specific house to C provided C passes the 2025 Bar Examinations, and bequeathing a specific car to D, designating E as D's simple substitute strictly in case of predecease. D dies in 1997, A dies in 1998, E repudiates the substitution, and C subsequently fails the 2025 Bar Examinations. Because the suspensive condition attached to C's devise failed (Art. 960[3]) and the institution/substitution regarding D's legacy became ineffective due to predecease and repudiation without accretion (Art. 960[3] & [4]), both properties pass by intestate succession to A's sole legal heir, his brother B.

II. FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES AND ORDERS OF INTESTATE SUCCESSION (ARTICLES 961–962, 978–1014)

1. The Rule of Preference of Lines

In intestate succession, blood relatives of the decedent are grouped into three successional lines, which enjoy a strict hierarchy of preference:

  1. The Direct Descending Line (children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren);
  2. The Direct Ascending Line (parents, grandparents, great-grandparents); and
  3. The Collateral Line (brothers, sisters, nephews, nieces, uncles, aunts, cousins up to the 5th degree).
  • Operative Rule: The direct descending line is strictly preferred over the direct ascending line, and the direct ascending line is strictly preferred over the collateral line. Consequently:
    • The presence of even one legitimate child or descendant completely excludes the decedent's legitimate parents and ascendants (Art. 985).
    • If the decedent leaves no descendants, the presence of surviving parents or ascendants completely excludes the decedent's brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (who belong to the collateral line).

2. The Rule of Proximity of Degree (Article 962)

Under Article 962 of the Civil Code, in every inheritance, the relative nearest in degree excludes the more distant ones, saving the right of representation when it properly takes place.

  • Subordination of Proximity to the Order of Intestate Succession: While proximity of degree governs priority within the same line or class, the Rule of Proximity ALWAYS yields to the statutory Order of Intestate Succession (and to the Rule of Preference of Lines). A relative who is in an equal or even nearer degree of consanguinity will be excluded by another relative who belongs to a higher preferred class in the statutory order of intestacy!
  • Professor's Illustration (Nephews/Nieces vs. Uncles/Aunts — Both 3rd Degree): Decedent X (a legitimate person) dies intestate without descendants, ascendants, or a spouse. He is survived by his Uncle E (the brother of X's predeceased father) and his two Nephews, C and D (the children of X's predeceased brothers, A and B). Computing civil degrees under Articles 963–966:
    • Uncle E is three (3) degrees removed from X (X up to Father [1], up to Grandfather [2], down to Uncle E [3]).
    • Nephews C and D are also three (3) degrees removed from X (X up to Father [1], down to Brothers A/B [2], down to Nephews C/D [3]).
    • Who inherits? Although Uncle E and Nephews C and D stand in equal proximity (3rd civil degree), Nephews C and D completely exclude Uncle E and inherit 100% of the estate! Why? Because the Rule of Proximity yields to the Order of Intestate Succession, where Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces belong to Rank 5, whereas Uncles, Aunts, and Cousins belong to Rank 6 (Other Collateral Relatives).

4. Rules on Concurrence and Exclusion in Intestate Succession

Being listed in a particular numerical rank in the Order of Intestate Succession does not mean that an upper rank automatically excludes all lower ranks. Several classes concur (inherit simultaneously) with one another:

  • Concurrence of Legitimate Children, Illegitimate Children, and Surviving Spouse: In the Regular Order, Rank 1 (Legitimate Children), Rank 3 (Illegitimate Children), and Rank 4 (Surviving Spouse) concur with one another. The presence of legitimate children excludes only Rank 2 (Legitimate Parents and Ascendants) and Ranks 5 to 7, but does not exclude illegitimate children or the surviving spouse.
  • Concurrence of Legitimate Parents, Illegitimate Children, and Surviving Spouse (Art. 1000): In the absence of legitimate descendants, Rank 2 (Legitimate Parents/Ascendants) concurs with Rank 3 (Illegitimate Children) and Rank 4 (Surviving Spouse).
  • Total Exclusion of Illegitimate Parents by ANY Child (Irregular Order — Art. 993): In the Irregular Order, the presence of either legitimate children (Rank 1) or illegitimate children (Rank 2) completely excludes the illegitimate parents (Rank 3) pursuant to Article 993. (However, illegitimate parents concur with the Surviving Spouse under Art. 997: 1/2 to Illegitimate Parents, 1/2 to Surviving Spouse).
  • Concurrence of Surviving Spouse with Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Art. 1001): If the decedent dies without descendants or ascendants, the Surviving Spouse (Rank 4) does NOT exclude Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Rank 5). Instead, they concur: one-half (1/2) of the estate goes to the surviving spouse, and the other one-half (1/2) goes to the brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces as a class (Note: In the Irregular Order under Art. 994, if the Surviving Spouse concurs with illegitimate brothers/sisters/nephews/nieces, the Surviving Spouse also takes 1/2 and the illegitimate siblings/nephews/nieces take 1/2).
  • Exclusion of Other Collaterals by Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Art. 1009): Within the collateral line, brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (Rank 5) form a privileged class that completely excludes all other collateral relatives (Rank 6), such as uncles, aunts, and first cousins.

III. THE RIGHT OF REPRESENTATION IN TESTATE AND INTESTATE SUCCESSION (ARTICLES 969–977)

A. Statutory Definition and Legal Fiction (Article 970–971)

Under Article 970 of the Civil Code, representation is a right created by fiction of law, by virtue of which the representative is raised to the place and the degree of the person represented, and acquires the rights which the latter would have if he were living or if he could have inherited. Under Article 971, the representative is called to the succession by the law and not by the person represented. The representative does not succeed the person represented, but the one whom the person represented would have succeeded.

C. Strict Lineal, Collateral, and Adoption Restrictions on Representation

  1. Direct Descending Line Only — NEVER in the Ascending Line (Art. 972): Representation takes place continuously in the direct descending line, but NEVER in the ascending line. Thus, a grandchild or great-grandchild can represent a father, but a paternal grandfather can never represent a predeceased father to inherit from a grandson.
  2. Collateral Line Restriction — Nephews and Nieces ONLY (Arts. 972 & 975): In the collateral line, representation takes place only in favor of the children of brothers or sisters (nephews and nieces), whether they be of the full or half blood.
    • No Representation for Grandnephews / Grandnieces: Great-nephews and great-nieces have no right of representation.
    • Jurisprudential Anchor — De Los Santos v. De La Cruz (G.R. No. L-29192, Feb. 22, 1971): Pelagia De La Cruz died intestate without descendants, ascendants, or a surviving spouse, leaving behind nieces and a grandniece (Gertrudes De Los Santos, whose mother Marciana—a niece of the decedent—had predeceased the decedent). Gertrudes claimed a share by right of representation of her predeceased mother. Ruling: A grandniece cannot inherit by right of representation in the collateral line because under Article 972, collateral representation takes place only in favor of the children of brothers or sisters (nephews and nieces). Consequently, the surviving nieces (4th civil degree) excluded the grandniece (5th civil degree) by proximity of degree.
    • When Nephews/Nieces Inherit Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita (Art. 975): Nephews and nieces inherit by right of representation (per stirpes) only when they survive with at least one uncle or aunt (a surviving brother or sister of the decedent). If only nephews and nieces survive (all brothers and sisters predeceased or are incapacitated), they inherit in their own right (per capita) in equal shares (subject to the 2:1 full-blood vs. half-blood rule under Art. 1008).
  3. Absolute Exclusion of Adopted Children in Representation: Under established Philippine civil law doctrine (emphasized across both the Oct 12 and Oct 19 lectures), while an adopted child has reciprocal successional rights directly with the adopter equivalent to a legitimate child, an adopted child CANNOT represent the adopter, NOR can an adopted child be represented by his or her descendants in the succession of the adopter's relatives.
    • Wisdom Behind the Rule: Adoption creates a purely personal civil relationship (fictio iuris) strictly between the adopter and the adopted child. It does not establish blood or legal filiation between the adopted child and the adopter's parents or collateral relatives. Thus, if X adopts A, and X subsequently predeceases his own father Y, A cannot represent X in the inheritance from Y.

D. The Two-Way Rule on Repudiation (Article 977 vs. Article 976)

📍 IMPORTANT

🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — THE TWO-WAY STREET OF REPUDIATION AND REPRESENTATION: Article 977 ("RENOUNCER CANNOT BE REPRESENTED"): "Heirs who repudiate their share may not be represented."* If B repudiates his inheritance from his father X, B's son C cannot represent B in X's estate. Why? Because B was alive and capacitated, and voluntarily surrendered the share for his entire line. Article 976 ("RENOUNCER CAN STILL REPRESENT"): "A person may represent him whose inheritance he has renounced."* If B dies in 2020 and his son C repudiates B's heavily indebted estate, and in 2025 Grandfather X dies, C CAN STILL REPRESENT B in X's estate! Why? Because under Article 971, the representative (C) succeeds the decedent (X) directly, not the person represented (B).

  • Professor's Illustration (Contrasting Art. 977 and Art. 976):
    • Case A (Art. 977): Grandfather X dies intestate, survived by his two sons, A and B, and B's son (Grandson C). B repudiates his inheritance from X. C cannot represent B (Art. 977), nor can C inherit in his own right because Uncle A (a nearer 1st-degree relative) excludes C under the Rule of Proximity; thus, A takes 100% of X's estate.
    • Case B (Art. 976): Son B predeceases Grandfather X. Upon B's death, Grandson C repudiates his inheritance from B's estate. Later, Grandfather X dies, survived by Son A and Grandson C. C can validly represent B in X's estate and take 1/2 of X's estate alongside A (Art. 976).

E. Grandchildren Inheriting Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita When All Children Repudiate (Article 969)

  • General Rule (Art. 982): Grandchildren and other descendants always inherit by right of representation (per stirpes)—even if all the children predeceased, were incapacitated, or were disinherited—dividing among themselves the specific share pertaining to their respective parent.
  • Singular Exception (Art. 969 — Universal Repudiation by All Nearest Heirs): If ALL the children of the decedent repudiate the inheritance, the grandchildren cannot inherit by representation (since representation is barred in repudiation under Art. 977); instead, the following degree (the grandchildren) shall inherit in their own right (per capita), dividing the estate into equal individual shares!
  • Professor's Illustration (The ₱300 Million Estate — Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita):
    • Facts: Decedent X dies intestate leaving a ₱300 Million estate, three children (A, B, and G), and five grandchildren: D (sole child of A), E and F (children of B), and H and I (children of G).
    • Scenario 1 (Predecease, Incapacity, and Disinheritance — Per Stirpes under Art. 982): Suppose A was validly disinherited, B is incapacitated, and G predeceased X. All three causes permit representation (per stirpes). The ₱300M estate is divided into 3 lines of ₱100M each:
      • D takes ₱100 Million (representing A);
      • E and F take ₱50 Million each (sharing B's ₱100M); and
      • H and I take ₱50 Million each (sharing G's ₱100M).
    • Scenario 2 (Universal Repudiation by All Children — Per Capita under Art. 969): Suppose A, B, and G are all alive and capacitated, and all three repudiate their inheritance from X. Under Article 969, representation is barred, and the five grandchildren (D, E, F, H, and I) inherit in their own right (per capita). The ₱300M estate is divided equally by 5, giving D, E, F, H, and I ₱60 Million each!

IV. THE BARRIER BETWEEN LEGITIMATES AND ILLEGITIMATES (ARTICLE 992) AND INTERPLAY WITH ARTICLE 777

A. The Iron Curtain Rule (Article 992)

Under Article 992 of the Civil Code:

"An illegitimate child has no right to inherit ab intestato from the legitimate children and relatives of his father or mother; nor shall such children or relatives inherit in the same manner from the illegitimate child."

  • Historical Rationale vs. Modern Context: Known as the "Iron Curtain Rule" or the successional barrier, Article 992 was enacted by the Code Commission on the presumption of mutual hostility between the legitimate family and the illegitimate child. Until repealed by Congress, Article 992 remains binding law.
  • Terminology Caveat for the Bar Exam: While recent Supreme Court decisions penned by Senior Associate Justice Marvic Leonen (Aquino v. Aquino, G.R. No. 208912, Dec. 7, 2021) use the sociological term "nonmarital children", the Professor stressed that for the Bar Examinations, examinees must continue to use the statutory term "illegitimate children" as written in the Civil Code and Family Code. (Note: Note also the narrow doctrinal holding in Aquino v. Aquino regarding direct descending representation, though in your class lectures and standard Bar problems, the Professor strictly applies the traditional Article 992 vs. Article 902/990 rules below—always state the traditional codal rule taught by the Professor first!)
  • Restricted Strictly to Intestate Succession (Ab Intestato): By its express wording ("ab intestato"), Article 992 applies only to intestate succession (and representation). It does not prohibit a testator from voluntarily instituting an illegitimate grandchild or illegitimate half-sibling out of the free portion in a valid will.

B. Qualification of Representatives Based on Filial Status (Arts. 902, 989, 990, and 992)

When applying the Right of Representation in the direct descending line (as taught in the Oct 12 lecture), look at the status of the person being represented (the child of the decedent):

  1. If the person to be represented is a LEGITIMATE child: He or she can be represented ONLY by a legitimate child or descendant. An illegitimate child of a legitimate child is barred by the Iron Curtain Rule (Article 992) from representing his legitimate parent in the estate of the legitimate grandparent.
  2. If the person to be represented is an ILLEGITIMATE child: He or she can be represented by BOTH legitimate and illegitimate children or descendants (Articles 902 and 990). When both legitimate and illegitimate descendants represent an illegitimate parent, they divide the represented parent's share between themselves observing the 2:1 ratio (each legitimate representative gets double the share of each illegitimate representative under Art. 989/895).

C. Jurisprudential Masterpiece: How Article 777 Defeats Article 992 — De La Merced v. De La Merced (G.R. No. 126707, Feb. 25, 1999)

  • The Bar Trap: Confusing representation (where the parent predeceased the collateral relative, triggering Art. 992) with two successive transmissions of ownership (where the parent survived the collateral relative and acquired a vested share at the moment of death under Article 777, and then later died transmitting his own vested property to his illegitimate child under Article 988).
  • Facts of De La Merced v. De La Merced: A (Evarista), B (Francisco), C, and D were legitimate siblings. A died intestate without issue, leaving an estate inherited by her surviving legitimate siblings B, C, and D (say, ₱300 Million total, or ₱100 Million each). Subsequently (or after co-ownership vested), B died intestate, and his recognized illegitimate son, S (Joselito), claimed B's entire estate, including the ₱100 Million share B had inherited from his legitimate sister A. C and D opposed S's claim, invoking the Iron Curtain Rule under Article 992 to argue that S, being an illegitimate child, could not inherit property originating from B's legitimate sibling A.
  • Issue: Does Article 992 bar an illegitimate child (S) from inheriting property that his deceased parent (B) had previously inherited from a legitimate sibling (A) who died before the parent (or granted vested rights prior to death)?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No. Article 992 is completely inapplicable because B did not predecease A (or had already acquired a vested co-ownership right during his lifetime). Under Article 777 of the Civil Code, B's one-third share (₱100 Million) in A's estate vested in B immediately upon A's death, becoming an absolute, indistinguishable part of B's own personal estate. When B subsequently died, his illegitimate son S inherited that property in his own right directly from his father B (as a compulsory/intestate heir of B under Art. 988), and not by right of representation in A's estate. Hence, there was no collateral intestate succession between S and A for Article 992 to block!

V. RULES OF THUMB, SPECIFIC COMBINATIONS IN INTESTATE SHARES, AND CONCURRENCE THEORIES

A. The "One-Half Rule" and Core Intestate Combinations

To master estate distribution problems without confusion, the Professor emphasized a foundational analytical rule of thumb in intestate combinations: Whenever the legitimate direct line (whether legitimate children/descendants or, in their default, legitimate parents/ascendants) concurs with multiple classes of irregular compulsory heirs, the legitimate line consistently receives at least one-half (1/2) of the estate. Furthermore, every fraction of the intestate estate must be exhausted (totaling 100%).

  1. 1 Legitimate Child + Surviving Spouse (Art. 996, Santillon v. Miranda): They divide the entire intestate estate equally: 1/2 to the lone Legitimate Child and 1/2 to the Surviving Spouse.
  2. 2 or More Legitimate Children + Surviving Spouse (Art. 996): The Surviving Spouse is counted as one legitimate child, and they divide the entire estate in equal shares (e.g., if 3 LCs + SS, each gets 1/4).
  3. Legitimate Parents + Illegitimate Children + Surviving Spouse (Art. 1000):
    • Legitimate Parents/Ascendants: 1/2 of the estate;
    • Illegitimate Children: 1/4 of the estate;
    • Surviving Spouse: 1/4 of the estate. (Notice how the 2:1 ratio between the legitimate line [1/2] and the illegitimate line [1/4] is preserved, while the Surviving Spouse's share expands from 1/8 in testate legitime to 1/4 in intestacy to exhaust 100% of the estate!)
  4. Illegitimate Children + Surviving Spouse (Art. 998): In the absence of legitimate descendants and legitimate ascendants, 1/2 to the Surviving Spouse and 1/2 to the Illegitimate Children.
  5. Surviving Spouse + Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Art. 1001 / Art. 994): 1/2 to the Surviving Spouse and 1/2 to the Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces.

C. The Double-Share Rule for Full-Blood vs. Half-Blood Collaterals (Arts. 1006, 1008, and 1009)

  • The 2:1 Full-Blood vs. Half-Blood Rule (Arts. 1006 & 1008): Should brothers and sisters of the full blood survive together with brothers and sisters of the half blood, the former shall be entitled to a share double that of the latter (a 2:1 ratio). The exact same 2:1 rule applies when nephews and nieces of the full blood concur with nephews and nieces of the half blood (Art. 1008), whether inheriting in their own right or by right of representation.
  • Strict Outer Limit of the Full/Half-Blood Distinction (Art. 1009): The distinction between full blood and half blood applies exclusively to brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (Rank 5, as well as third-degree reservatarios in reserva troncal under Padura v. Baldovino). Under Article 1009, when other collateral relatives within the 5th degree succeed (Rank 6: uncles, aunts, first cousins, grandnephews, grandnieces), they succeed without distinction of lines or preference among them by reason of the whole blood! Thus, a half-blood first cousin or grandnephew takes the exact same equal (per capita) share as a full-blood first cousin or grandnephew.

D. Concurrence of Legitimate and Illegitimate Children in Intestacy: Exclusion Theory vs. Concurrence Theory

When the decedent dies intestate survived by both legitimate children (LC) and illegitimate children (IC), Article 983 in relation to Article 895 (as amended by Article 176 of the Family Code) mandates a 2:1 ratio (1 IC = 1/2 of 1 LC), provided that the collective legitime of the legitimate children (1/2 of the net estate) is NEVER impaired.

VI. MIXED SUCCESSION (PARTIAL INTESTACY) AND THE RULE ON CHARGING LEGACIES

A. Prohibition Against Top-Line Deduction of Legacies

Mixed succession occurs when a decedent dies leaving a valid will that does not institute a universal heir and disposes of only a part of his estate (such as a specific legacy or devise to a friend), leaving the remainder undisposed (Art. 960[2]).

🚫 CAUTION

🚫 THE FATAL BAR EXAM ERROR IN MIXED SUCCESSION: NEVER deduct the legacy from the top of the gross/net estate first and then apply the intestate fractions to the reduced balance! * Why? Because statutory legitimes are always computed based on the entire net hereditary estate before testamentary gifts. If you deduct a ₱10M legacy from a ₱120M estate first (leaving ₱110M) and give the Legitimate Parents 1/2 of ₱110M (₱55M), you have illegally impaired their statutory legitime of ₱60M (1/2 of ₱120M)!

Question 2.1: Interplay of Article 992 (Iron Curtain) and Article 777 (De La Merced Doctrine) vs. Representation

Don Arturo, a legitimate person, died intestate and unmarried without any descendants or ascendants, leaving a net estate of ₱90 Million. He had three legitimate brothers: Bruno, Cesar, and Dario. Bruno had predeceased Don Arturo by two years, leaving two children: Lino (legitimate) and Nilo (illegitimate). Cesar and Dario survived Don Arturo, but three months after Don Arturo's death, Cesar died intestate in a car crash, survived only by his recognized illegitimate daughter, Maya. Dario now claims that both Nilo and Maya are barred by the Iron Curtain Rule under Article 992 from receiving any part of Don Arturo's ₱90 Million estate. Rule on Dario's contention and distribute the ₱90 Million estate.

  • A Answer: Dario is correct as to Nilo, who gets ₱0, but wrong as to Maya, who inherits Cesar's ₱30 Million share. Thus, the ₱90 Million estate is distributed: ₱30 Million to Lino, ₱30 Million to Maya (via Cesar's estate), and ₱30 Million to Dario.
  • L Legal Basis: Under Article 992 of the Civil Code (the Iron Curtain Rule), an illegitimate child has no right to inherit ab intestato from the legitimate children and relatives of his father or mother. Consequently, when a legitimate sibling predeceases the decedent, only his legitimate children may represent him in the collateral line under Articles 972 and 992. However, under Article 777 of the Civil Code and the Supreme Court's ruling in De La Merced v. De La Merced (G.R. No. 126707, Feb. 25, 1999), successional rights are transmitted at the exact moment of the decedent's death. When a legitimate sibling survives the decedent, his intestate share vests in him immediately upon the decedent's death and becomes part of his own patrimony; upon his subsequent death, his illegitimate child inherits that property directly from her own father under Article 988, not by representation from the legitimate uncle.
  • A Application:
    1. Bruno's Line (₱30 Million — Predeceased Don Arturo): Because Bruno died before Don Arturo, Bruno's ₱30M share passes by right of representation. Since Bruno was a legitimate child/sibling, Article 992 bars his illegitimate son Nilo from representing him. Thus, Bruno's legitimate son Lino takes the entire ₱30 Million of Bruno's line.
    2. Cesar's Line (₱30 Million — Survived Don Arturo, then Died): Because Cesar was alive when Don Arturo died, Cesar inherited his ₱30 Million share in his own right at the exact moment of Don Arturo's death under Article 777. When Cesar died three months later, his illegitimate daughter Maya did not need to represent Cesar in Don Arturo's estate; rather, Maya inherited Cesar's vested ₱30 Million directly from her father Cesar as his sole intestate heir under Article 988 (De La Merced doctrine).
    3. Dario's Line: Dario takes his ₱30 Million share in his own right.
  • C Conclusion: Lino gets ₱30 Million, Maya gets ₱30 Million (through Cesar's estate), Dario gets ₱30 Million, and Nilo gets ₱0.

Question 2.2: Collateral Proximity vs. Order of Intestate Succession; Full-Blood vs. Half-Blood (Arts. 975, 1008, 1009)

Mateo (a legitimate person) died intestate without descendants, ascendants, or a surviving spouse, leaving a net estate of ₱60 Million. He is survived by: (1) his maternal Aunt Clara (sister of Mateo's predeceased mother); (2) Nena, the legitimate daughter of Mateo's predeceased full-blood brother Pedro; (3) Obet, the legitimate son of Mateo's predeceased half-blood brother Ramon; and (4) Paco, the legitimate grandson of Mateo's predeceased full-blood sister Juana (i.e., Paco is Mateo's grandnephew). Distribute Mateo's ₱60 Million estate.

  • A Answer: Nena receives ₱40 Million and Obet receives ₱20 Million. Aunt Clara and Grandnephew Paco receive ₱0.
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Articles 1003 and 1009 of the Civil Code, brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces belong to the 5th Class in the Regular Order of Intestate Succession and exclude all other collateral relatives (6th Class), such as aunts, uncles, and grandnephews—even when an aunt stands in the same 3rd civil degree of consanguinity as a niece or nephew (because the Rule of Proximity under Art. 962 is subordinate to the Order of Intestate Succession).
    2. Under Article 972, representation in the collateral line takes place only in favor of the children of brothers or sisters (nephews and nieces), never grandnephews (De Los Santos v. De La Cruz).
    3. Under Articles 975 and 1008, when nephews and nieces survive alone without any living uncle or aunt (brother or sister of the decedent), they inherit in their own right (per capita), but those of the full blood are entitled to a share double (2:1) that of the half blood.
  • A Application:
    1. Exclusion of Aunt Clara and Grandnephew Paco: Aunt Clara (3rd degree, 6th Class) and Grandnephew Paco (4th degree, 6th Class, with no right of collateral representation under Art. 972) belong to "Other Collateral Relatives" and are completely excluded by Mateo's niece Nena and nephew Obet (5th Class).
    2. Division Between Nena (Full-Blood Niece) and Obet (Half-Blood Nephew): Under Article 1008 in relation to Article 1006, Nena (child of a full-blood brother) takes double (2 shares) the share of Obet (child of a half-blood brother, 1 share). Dividing ₱60 Million into 3 parts (₱20M per part), Nena gets 2 parts (₱40 Million) and Obet gets 1 part (₱20 Million).
  • C Conclusion: Nena gets ₱40 Million, Obet gets ₱20 Million, and Aunt Clara and Paco get nothing.

I. THE RIGHT OF ACCRETION: CONCEPT, BASIS, AND STATUTORY REQUISITES (ARTICLES 1015–1017)

A. Concept, Definition, and Contextualization (Succession Law vs. Property Law)

  • Bar Exam Contextualization: Never confuse accretion in Succession Law (Art. 1015) with alluvion or accretion in Property Law (Art. 457) (where soil is gradually deposited on riverbanks for the benefit of the riparian owner).
  • Statutory Definition (Civil Code, Art. 1015): > "Accretion is a right by virtue of which, when two or more persons are called to the same inheritance, or to the same portion thereof, or to the same legacy or devise, the part assigned to the one who renounces or cannot receive his share, or who died before the testator, is added or incorporated to that of his co-heirs, co-legatees or co-devisees."
  • Philosophical Basis (Presumed Will of the Decedent): Accretion is a subordinate mechanism designed to fill a successional vacancy based on the presumed will of the decedent. Because the testator called multiple beneficiaries jointly to a single unified mass of property without severing their shares physically, the law presumes that the testator prefers the remaining co-beneficiaries to absorb a vacated share rather than allowing that portion to pass into intestacy.

B. Core Structural Elements and Statutory Requisites (Arts. 1015–1016)

For the right of accretion to take place in testamentary succession, four (4) indispensable requisites (two structural/positive under Art. 1016 and two negative/implied) must concur:

  1. Unity of Object + Plurality of Subjects Called Pro Indiviso (Art. 1016[1]): Two or more persons are called to the same inheritance, or to the same portion thereof, or to the same legacy or devise, pro indiviso (undividedly or without special designation of exclusive physical parts).
    • Unity of Object: A single item of property, a single legacy/devise, or a unified mass of the estate (e.g., the entire estate or the entire free portion) is disposed of as a whole.
    • Plurality of Subjects: Two or more individuals are simultaneously called as co-heirs, co-legatees, or co-devisees to that exact same object or portion. (If only one friend A is instituted to the entire free portion and A repudiates, there can be NO accretion because there are no co-heirs to the free portion!)
  2. Vacancy by "R-I-P" or Ineffective Disposition (Art. 1016[2]): One of the persons thus called vacates his share due to:
    • R — Repudiation (Renunciation): The beneficiary validly renounces the inheritance;
    • I — Incapacity (or Unworthiness): The beneficiary is incapacitated to receive it;
    • P — Predecease: The beneficiary dies before the testator; OR
    • Additional Doctrinal Causes: Failure of a suspensive condition attached to one co-heir's institution, or when one of the designated co-heirs cannot be identified (Art. 845).
  3. Absence of Express Prohibition or Valid Substitution (Implied Negative Requisite): The testator must not have expressly prohibited accretion, and must not have designated a valid substitute for the vacant share. Why? Because substitution reflects the express will of the testator ("Plan B"), whereas accretion rests merely on his presumed will ("Plan C").
  4. Acceptance and Capacity of the Remaining Co-Heir(s): The surviving co-heir, co-legatee, or co-devisee to whom the vacant share accrues must accept his own original share and possess capacity to succeed.

II. ACCRETION IN INTESTATE SUCCESSION AND INTERPLAY WITH COMPULSORY HEIRS (ARTICLES 1018–1023)

A. Scope of Accretion in Legal or Intestate Succession (Article 1018)

Under Article 1018 of the Civil Code:

"In legal succession the share of the person who repudiates the inheritance shall always accrue to his co-heirs."

The Classic Doctrinal Controversy: Why Does Article 1018 Mention ONLY Repudiation?

Because Article 1015 defines accretion using all three causes of vacancy (predecease, incapacity, and repudiation), whereas Article 1018 (governing intestate succession) mentions only repudiation, Philippine civilists are divided into two schools of thought when an intestate heir predeceases or is incapacitated (and has no qualified representative):

  1. First School of Thought (Pro-Accretion via Analogy): Holds that accretion also applies in intestate succession in cases of predecease and incapacity (where representation is unavailable) by applying Article 1016 by analogy. Proponents argue that Article 1018 mentions repudiation merely to emphasize that accretion always occurs in repudiation because a repudiating heir can never be represented (Art. 977), whereas in predecease and incapacity, accretion is subordinate to representation.
  2. Second School of Thought (Anti-Accretion / Justice Desiderio Jurado's View — Favored by the Professor & Dean Navarro): Holds that there is NO accretion in intestate succession in cases of predecease or incapacity. This strict position rests on two legal pillars:
    • Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius: Article 1018 expressly specifies only repudiation. Had the legislature intended to include predecease and incapacity in intestate accretion, it would have enumerated all three causes just as it did in Article 1016.
    • Absence of a True Legal Vacancy (Jure Proprio Inheritance): In intestate succession, the law calls heirs to succeed as of the exact moment of the decedent's death (Art. 777). A person who predeceased the decedent or is incapacitated at the moment of death is never called to the succession in the first place (absent representation); instead, the surviving intestate heirs of that class inherit the entire estate in their own right (jure proprio) by direct operation of the rules of intestacy, not by accretion. Conversely, a repudiating heir actually acquires a vested call at the moment of death and subsequently vacates it by renunciation, creating a true post-opening legal vacancy that passes to his co-heirs by accretion under Article 1018.
  • Practical Convergence on the Bar Exam: Whether one applies the First View (accretion) or the Second View (inheritance in their own right via intestacy), the mathematical distribution among the surviving co-heirs of the same degree is 100% identical! Both positions receive full credit on the Bar exam when properly explained.

C. Proportional Sharing, Obligations, and Compulsory Heirs (Articles 1019–1023)

  • Proportional Sharing (Art. 1019): The heirs to whom the portion goes by the right of accretion take it in the same proportion that they inherit. (E.g., if X leaves a ₱60k legacy to A [1/2 = ₱30k], B [1/3 = ₱20k], and C [1/6 = ₱10k], and C repudiates his ₱10k share, A and B divide C's ₱10k in the ratio of 3 : 2 [₱30k : ₱20k], so A gets ₱6k and B gets ₱4k).
  • Transmissible Rights and Obligations (Art. 1020): Co-heirs receiving a share by accretion succeed to all the rights and obligations which the heir who renounced or could not receive it would have had, unless the testamentary obligation is purely personal.
  • Accretion Among Compulsory Heirs — Free Portion ONLY, NEVER in the Legitime (Art. 1021): > "Among the compulsory heirs the right of accretion shall take place only when the free portion is left to two or more of them, or to any one of them and to a stranger. > Should the part repudiated be the legitime, the other co-heirs shall succeed to it in their own right, and not by the right of accretion."
    • Rationale of Article 1021: The legitime is mandated by law for a class of compulsory heirs (e.g., 1/2 of the estate for legitimate children collectively), not by the creative will of the testator. When a compulsory heir repudiates his legitime (and cannot be represented under Art. 977), there is no accretion in the legitime. Instead, the remaining compulsory heirs succeed to the collective legitime in their own right (jure proprio) (or via intestate succession as shown on the Professor's Vacancy Table), because the quantum of each child's legitime depends solely on the number of qualified compulsory heirs/lines actually taking from the estate.
  • Default to Intestacy (Art. 1022) and Accretion in Legacies/Devises/Usufruct (Art. 1023): In testamentary succession, when the right of accretion does not take place, the vacant portion of the instituted heirs, if no substitute has been designated, shall pass to the legal heirs of the testator, who shall receive it with the same charges and obligations (Art. 1022). Accretion also takes place in precisely the same manner among devisees, legatees, and usufructuaries (Art. 1023).

III. PHILOSOPHICAL FOUNDATIONS & THE PROFESSOR'S CLASSROOM MASTER MATRIX OF VACANCIES

A. Two Philosophical Pillars Governing All Vacancies

  1. Succession as an Extension of Ownership (Jus Disponendi / Primacy of the Testator's Will): Because succession is an extension of the right of ownership beyond death, the will of the testator is the supreme law of the succession (provided it respects mandatory law). Between testacy and intestacy, testacy is favored. Over the Free Portion, the testator has plenary freedom not only to institute voluntary heirs, legatees, and devisees ("Plan A"), but also to designate Substitutes ("Plan B").
  2. Inviolability of the Legitime (Arts. 886, 872, 904): The Legitime is reserved by law for compulsory heirs and is strictly off-limits to the testator (except via a valid disinheritance under Art. 915). Because the testator cannot impose any condition, charge, or substitution upon the legitime (Arts. 864, 872, 904), and because accretion never applies to the legitime (Art. 1021, par. 2), neither Substitution nor Accretion can ever occur in the Legitime!

Question 3.1: Earmarking vs. Aliquot Shares; Unequal Proportions in Accretion (Arts. 1016, 1017, 1019)

Testator Don Emilio died leaving no compulsory heirs and a valid will containing two dispositions: 1. "I devise my 3-hectare mango orchard in Zambales to my friends Anton (1/2 share), Berto (1/3 share), and Carlo (1/6 share)." 2. "I bequeath ₱3 Million in cash to my friends Diana, Elisa, and Fiona, as follows: ₱1 Million in my BDO savings account to Diana; ₱1 Million in my Landbank checking account to Elisa; and ₱1 Million in my safety deposit box at PNB to Fiona." Both Carlo and Fiona predeceased Don Emilio, and no substitutes were named in the will. Don Emilio's sole surviving blood relative is his legitimate brother, Gregorio. How should Carlo's 1/6 share in the mango orchard and Fiona's ₱1 Million cash legacy be distributed?

  • A Answer: Carlo's 1/6 share in the mango orchard passes by right of accretion to Anton and Berto in a 3:2 proportion (so Anton ends up with 3/5 and Berto with 2/5 of the orchard). Conversely, Fiona's ₱1 Million cash legacy in the PNB safety deposit box cannot pass by accretion due to earmarking, and therefore passes by intestate succession to Don Emilio's brother, Gregorio.
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Articles 1016 and 1017, par. 1 of the Civil Code, accretion takes place in testamentary succession when two or more persons are called to the same devise or legacy pro indiviso and one of them predeceases the testator. Designating aliquot fractional parts (such as 1/2, 1/3, and 1/6) does not exclude the right of accretion because it does not make each devisee the exclusive owner of a determinate physical segment. Under Article 1019, co-heirs/co-devisees take the accruing share in the same proportion that they inherit.
    2. However, under Article 1017, par. 2, in the case of money or other fungible goods, the right of accretion is excluded when the shares of each legatee are earmarked (physically segregated into distinct repositories), destroying unity of object. Under Article 1022, when accretion does not take place and no substitute is designated, the vacant share passes to the legal/intestate heirs of the testator.
  • A Application:
    1. As to the Mango Orchard (Carlo's 1/6 Share): Because Anton (1/2 = 3/6), Berto (1/3 = 2/6), and Carlo (1/6) were called to the same orchard using abstract aliquot fractions without physical segregation, unity of object is preserved and accretion applies. Under Article 1019, Anton and Berto absorb Carlo's 1/6 share in the proportion of their original shares (3/6 : 2/6, or 3 : 2). Thus, Anton takes 3/5 of 1/6 (= 3/30 = 1/10), bringing Anton's total share to 3/6 + 1/10 = 18/30 (or 3/5); and Berto takes 2/5 of 1/6 (= 2/30), bringing Berto's total share to 2/6 + 2/30 = 12/30 (or 2/5).
    2. As to the PNB Cash Legacy (Fiona's ₱1 Million): Because Don Emilio localized and physically segregated each ₱1 Million legacy into three separate bank repositories (BDO, Landbank, and PNB), the funds were earmarked under Article 1017, par. 2. Unity of object is absent, barring Diana and Elisa from claiming accretion. Since no substitute was named, Fiona's vacated ₱1 Million passes by intestate succession under Article 1022 to Don Emilio's sole legal heir, his brother Gregorio.
  • C Conclusion: Anton and Berto take the mango orchard in a 3/5 and 2/5 proportion by accretion, while Gregorio inherits the ₱1 Million in the PNB safety deposit box by intestate succession.

Question 3.2: Competing Substitution vs. Accretion vs. Representation in Testate Succession

Testator T died leaving a net estate of ₱60 Million, survived by his two legitimate children, X and Y, and X's legitimate son (Grandson Z). In his will, T instituted X and Y to his entire estate in equal shares, and added: "Should X predecease me, become incapacitated, or repudiate his inheritance, I designate my friend F as X's substitute." X predeceased T. Distribute the ₱60 Million estate among Y, Grandson Z, and Friend F.

  • A Answer: Child Y receives ₱30 Million (₱15M legitime + ₱15M free portion), Grandson Z receives ₱15 Million (X's legitime by right of representation), and Friend F receives ₱15 Million (X's share in the free portion by right of substitution).
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Articles 872 and 904 of the Civil Code, the testator cannot impose any substitution upon the legitime. Thus, when a compulsory heir in the descending line predeceases the testator, his vacant legitime passes to his legitimate descendants by right of representation under Article 972 (NO SUB $\rightarrow$ REP).
    2. Conversely, over the free portion, representation never applies, and testamentary substitution (Art. 857) is superior to the right of accretion (Art. 1016) because substitution expresses the explicit will of the testator whereas accretion rests merely on his presumed will (SUB $\rightarrow$ ACC $\rightarrow$ IS).
  • A Application:
    1. Baseline Allocation: The ₱60M estate consists of a ₱30M Legitime (₱15M to X, ₱15M to Y) and a ₱30M Free Portion (₱15M to X, ₱15M to Y). Y takes his ₱15M legitime + ₱15M free portion = ₱30 Million.
    2. X's Vacant Legitime (₱15 Million): T's substitution of Friend F is void insofar as X's ₱15M legitime is concerned (NO SUB). Instead, X's legitimate son Grandson Z inherits X's ₱15 Million legitime by right of representation.
    3. X's Vacant Free Portion (₱15 Million): Grandson Z cannot represent X in the free portion. Between Friend F (the designated substitute) and Child Y (co-heir claiming accretion), Substitution defeats Accretion. Thus, Friend F takes X's ₱15 Million share in the free portion.
  • C Conclusion: Y receives ₱30 Million, Z receives ₱15 Million, and F receives ₱15 Million.

B. Capacity of Non-Natural Persons and Special Beneficiaries (Arts. 1026, 1029, 1030)

While juridical persons (corporations, partnerships, the State, provinces, municipal corporations) generally inherit by virtue of their separate juridical personality (Art. 1026, par. 1), the Law on Succession exceptionally permits three groups/purposes lacking separate juridical personality to benefit under a will:

  1. Associations or Institutions for Public Interest (Art. 1026, par. 2): Testamentary dispositions may be made to associations or institutions for religious, scientific, cultural, educational, or charitable purposes, even if they lack separate corporate personality.
  2. Prayers and Pious Works for the Testator's Soul (Art. 1029): Although the human soul lacks juridical personality, dispositions made in general or specific terms for prayers and pious works for the benefit of the testator's soul are valid and enforceable.
  3. The Poor in General or of a Definite Locality (Art. 1030): Testamentary provisions in favor of the poor in general, without designation of particular persons, are valid and limited to the poor of the domicile of the testator at the time of his death (or the locality designated by him), even though "the poor" as a class have no separate juridical personality.

C. Doubt as to Order of Death: Presumption of Simultaneous Death and Representation (Article 43)

Under Article 43 of the Civil Code:

"Whenever there is a doubt, as between two or more persons who are called to succeed each other, as to which of them died first, whoever alleges the death of one prior to the other, shall prove the same; in the absence of proof, it is presumed that they died at the same time, and there shall be no transmission of rights from one to the other."

  • Two Requisites for Applying Article 43:
    1. Two or more persons perish in the same or separate calamities under circumstances where there is no evidence as to who died first; and
    2. They are called to succeed each other (by will or by intestacy).
  • Crucial Doctrine: Article 43 Does NOT Bar the Right of Representation!
    • Although Article 43 states that "there shall be no transmission of rights from one to the other," both the Professor and Dean Navarro stressed that this rule does NOT preclude the operation of the Right of Representation (Arts. 970–971)!
    • Doctrinal Rationale: In representation, the representative (grandchild) does not inherit through the estate of the person represented (the child); rather, by fiction of law (Art. 971), the grandchild steps into the shoes of the child to inherit directly from the decedent (the grandparent). Thus, while no property is transmitted from Father F to Son S (so S's widow or S's creditors cannot claim F's property through S's estate), S's own children (GC1 and GC2) can still represent S to inherit directly from Grandfather F!
  • Professor's & Dean Navarro's Illustration: Father F and his legitimate son S perish together in an airplane/vehicular crash, and there is no evidence as to who died first. S leaves behind two legitimate children, GC1 and GC2. Under Article 43, F and S are presumed to have died simultaneously, preventing any transmission of rights from F to S. However, GC1 and GC2 are not barred by Article 43 and validly inherit S's share in F's estate by right of representation.

II. INCAPACITY BY REASON OF POSSIBLE UNDUE INFLUENCE (ARTICLE 1027)

A. Underlying Rationale and the Three Absolute Limitations on Article 1027

  • Statutory Purpose: Article 1027 disqualifies specific classes of persons from succeeding by will due to a conclusive legal presumption of undue influence arising from their spiritual, fiduciary, legal, or medical ascendance over the testator when the will is executed.
  • Three (3) Absolute Limitations on the Scope of Article 1027 (MEMORIZE FOR MONDAY'S QUIZ!):
    1. Applies ONLY to Testamentary Succession (Zero Application to Intestacy): The statute repeatedly uses the word "testator." It never applies to intestate succession because intestate shares are mandated by law, leaving no room for undue influence.
    2. NEVER Affects or Impairs the Legitime (Applies ONLY to the Free Portion): If a compulsory heir (e.g., the testator's legitimate child) falls under any disqualification in Article 1027 (e.g., as a confessing priest or attending physician), he still receives his full legitime! Because the legitime is reserved by law and lies beyond the testator's control, undue influence cannot taint the legitime. Article 1027 disqualifies the heir only as to the disposable free portion.
    3. Inapplicable to Wills Executed PRIOR to the Last Illness / Relationship: If the will instituting the priest, guardian, or physician was executed before the last illness or before the confidential relationship arose, the incapacity does not attach because the possibility of undue influence did not exist when the testamentary act was performed.

B. Deep-Dive into Paragraphs 1 to 5 of Article 1027

1. Priests and Ministers of the Gospel (Art. 1027, Par. 1) & Their 4th-Degree Relatives / Church (Par. 2)
  • Catholic Priests vs. Non-Catholic Ministers:

    • Protestant Pastor / Non-Catholic Minister: Because auricular confession is not practiced, extending any spiritual aid (reading Scripture, praying together) during the last illness is sufficient to incapacitate him.
  • Sequential Confessions to Multiple Priests During Last Illness: The confession need not be the literal last confession before death. Ten years later, during his terminal illness, X summoned Father P to hear his confession and subsequently died. Can Father P inherit under the 1995 will? Yes! The incapacity under Article 1027(1) does not attach because the will was executed ten years before the last illness and confession.
2. Guardians With Respect to Their Wards (Art. 1027, Par. 3)
  • Rule: Incapacitated as to testamentary dispositions made by the ward before the final accounts of the guardianship have been approved, even if the testator dies after their approval.
  • Express Statutory Exception ("A-D-B-S-S"): Valid when the guardian is the ward's Ascendant, Descendant, Brother, Sister, or Spouse. (A non-relative guardian, such as a longtime household helper or friend appointed by the court, is strictly disqualified prior to final account approval).
3. Attesting Witnesses and Their Immediate Family (Art. 1027, Par. 4 in rel. to Art. 823)
  • Rule: Disqualifies any attesting witness to the execution of a will, and their spouse, parents, or children, or anyone claiming under them. (Notice siblings or cousins of an attesting witness are NOT disqualified!).
  • Exception (Art. 823): Not incapacitated if there are three (3) other competent and disinterested witnesses to the will.
4. Physicians, Surgeons, Nurses, Health Officers, and Druggists (Art. 1027, Par. 5)
  • Meaning of "Druggist": Refers to a licensed pharmacist who compounded/dispensed medicine while caring for the testator, not a "droguista" (drug user/pusher)!
  • Requisite of Continuous "Taking Care" vs. Mere Consultation: The medical professional must have actually taken care of (continuously treated and attended to) the testator during the last illness. A medical specialist who was merely consulted once by the attending physician without directly taking care of the patient is NOT incapacitated.
  • The Classic Doctrinal Debate: What If the Attending Physician/Nurse (or Priest) is the Testator's Own Child or Close Relative?
    • View 1 — Exempt from Incapacity (Cultural/Teleological View — Favored by the Professor in Oct 19 Lecture): If the physician or nurse who cared for the dying testator is a close relative (ascendant, descendant, sibling, or spouse), the incapacity does not apply even to the free portion. In Filipino and Asian family culture, a doctor-child or nurse-child is morally and socially expected to care for a dying parent; doing so springs from natural filial devotion rather than undue influence, and disqualifying them would absurdly force a doctor-child to abandon a dying parent.
    • View 2 — Incapacitated as to the Free Portion (Strict Textual / Expressio Unius View): Comparing Paragraph 5 (Physicians/Nurses) and Paragraph 1 (Priests) with Paragraph 3 (Guardians), the legislature expressly inserted the close-relative exception (ascendant, descendant, brother, sister, spouse) only in Paragraph 3, omitting it in Paragraphs 1 and 5. Under expressio unius est exclusio alterius, the physician-child or priest-child is incapacitated as to the free portion (though never as to his legitime).

III. INCAPACITY ARISING FROM MORALITY AND DISPOSITIONS FOR PIOUS WORKS (ARTICLES 1028 & 1029)

A. Prohibitions Based on Public Morality (Article 1028 in rel. to Article 739)

Under Article 1028 of the Civil Code, the prohibitions mentioned in Article 739, concerning donations inter vivos, shall apply to testamentary provisions. Thus, the following testamentary dispositions are void by reason of public morality and public policy:

  1. Those made between persons who were guilty of adultery or concubinage at the time of the execution of the will (e.g., a disposition in favor of the testator's mistress or paramour; no criminal conviction is required—preponderance of civil evidence suffices);
  2. Those made between persons found guilty of the same criminal offense, in consideration thereof; and
  3. Those made to a public officer or his wife, descendants and ascendants, by reason of his office.

B. Dispositions for Prayers and Pious Works for the Soul (Article 1029)

Under Article 1029 of the Civil Code:

"Should the testator dispose of the whole or part of his property for prayers and pious works for the benefit of his soul, in general terms and without specifying its application, the executor, with the court's approval shall deliver one-half thereof or its proceeds to the church or denomination to which the testator may belong, to be used for such prayers and pious works, and the other half to the State, for the purposes mentioned in article 1013."

  • Crucial Distinction — General Terms (50-50 Split) vs. Specified Application (100% as Directed):
    1. General Terms Without Specifying Application (Art. 1029 Applies): If Testator X states in his will, "I leave ₱100 Million for prayers and pious works for the benefit of my soul," Article 1029 mandates a 50-50 split: ₱50 Million goes to X's church/denomination and ₱50 Million goes to the State.
    2. Specified Application (Art. 1029 Does NOT Apply): Because the testator's will is the supreme law of succession, if X expressly specifies the exact application of the funds—such as stating, "I leave ₱100 Million for prayers for my soul, and I order that 1,000 masses be celebrated for me every day throughout the Philippines, with special weekly novenas/prayers at St. Anthony's Shrine (Bustillos), Baclaran Church, St. Jude Parish, and Quiapo Church"—the 50-50 split under Article 1029 does NOT apply! The State receives ₱0, and 100% of the ₱100 Million goes to the churches to fulfill X's explicit instructions.

IV. INCAPACITY BY REASON OF UNWORTHINESS AND ITS INTERPLAY WITH DISINHERITANCE (ARTICLES 1032–1040, 922, 16[2])

A. Nature and the Eight (8) Statutory Grounds of Unworthiness (Article 1032)

Unlike Article 1027 (which applies only to the testamentary free portion), Incapacity by Reason of Unworthiness under Article 1032 operates automatically by operation of law across BOTH testate and intestate succession, and deprives even a compulsory heir of his legitime (though if the unworthy heir is a child/descendant, his own children represent him in the legitime under Article 1035!).

The eight (8) exclusive causes of unworthiness under Article 1032 are:

  1. Parents who have abandoned their children or induced their daughters to lead a corrupt or immoral life, or attempted against their virtue (mirrors Art. 920[1]);
  2. Any person who has been convicted of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her spouse, descendants, or ascendants (mirrors Arts. 919[1], 920[2], 921[1]);
  3. Any person who has accused the testator of a crime for which the law prescribes imprisonment for six years or more, if the accusation has been found groundless (mirrors Arts. 919[2], 920[3], 921[2]);
  4. Any heir of full age who, having knowledge of the violent death of the testator, should fail to report it to an officer of the law within a month, unless the authorities have already taken action; this prohibition shall not apply to cases wherein, according to law, there is no obligation to make an accusation (Note: Because Philippine law imposes no general legal obligation on ordinary citizens to make a formal criminal accusation, Paragraph 4 is largely inoperative in the Philippines);
  5. Any person convicted of adultery or concubinage with the spouse of the testator (mirrors Arts. 919[3], 920[4]);
  6. Any person who by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence should cause the testator to make a will or to change one already made (mirrors Arts. 919[4], 920[5], 921[3]);
  7. Any person who by the same means prevents another from making a will, or from revoking one already made, or who supplants, conceals, or alters the latter's will; and
  8. Any person who falsifies or forges a supposed will of the decedent.

B. Reconciliation in Disinheritance (Art. 922) vs. Pardon in Unworthiness (Art. 1033)

Because five grounds of unworthiness (Art. 1032[1, 2, 3, 5, 6]) overlap with the grounds for disinheritance (Arts. 919–921), what happens if the offending compulsory heir and the offended decedent forgive each other before death?

  1. Rule in Unworthiness (Art. 1033 — Formal Written Pardon or Testamentary Institution Required): Under Article 1033, the causes of unworthiness are erased only in two ways:
    • Implied Pardon: The testator knew of the cause of unworthiness at the time he executed the will and nevertheless instituted the unworthy heir; OR
    • Express Pardon: If the decedent learned of the cause of unworthiness after making the will (or made no will at all), he condoned it in writing (a formal written pardon). A mere informal, tearful emotional reconciliation without a written document does NOT erase unworthiness under Article 1033!
  2. Rule in Disinheritance (Art. 922 — Bilateral Reconciliation Suffices Without Writing): Under Article 922, a subsequent bilateral reconciliation between the offender and the offended testator renders ineffectual any disinheritance that has been made, even without a written document.
  3. The Professor's / Dean Navarro's Harmonizing Rule:
    • Scenario A — Decedent Did NOT Disinherit the Heir in a Will (Art. 1033 Governs): If the decedent never executed a will disinheriting the offending heir, the decedent never invoked the rules of disinheritance. Thus, the rules of unworthiness (Art. 1033) govern exclusively, and a mere tearful reconciliation without a written pardon (or subsequent testamentary institution) is insufficient—the heir remains incapacitated!
    • Scenario B — Decedent EXPRESSLY Disinherited the Heir in a Will (Art. 922 Governs): If the decedent executed a will expressly disinheriting the offending heir on that ground, the decedent elected to invoke and submit the matter to the rules of disinheritance (Art. 922). Therefore, a subsequent bilateral reconciliation between them nullifies the disinheritance and restores the heir's capacity to inherit, even without a separate written pardon!

C. Moment of Determining Capacity (Article 1034) and Conflict of Laws (Article 1039 & Article 16[2])

  • Moment of Determining Capacity (Art. 1034 in rel. to Art. 777):
    1. General Rule (One Moment in Time): Capacity of the heir, devisee, or legatee is determined as of the moment of death of the decedent (Art. 1034, par. 1).
    2. Exception for Suspensive Conditions (Two Moments in Time — Art. 1034, par. 3): If the institution, legacy, or devise is subject to a suspensive condition, the beneficiary must possess capacity at two (2) distinct moments:
      • (a) At the moment of death of the testator; AND
      • (b) At the moment of the fulfillment of the suspensive condition!
    3. Rule When Final Judgment is Required (Art. 1034, par. 2): In causes of unworthiness requiring conviction or final judgment (Art. 1032[2, 3, and 5]), one waits for the rendition of final judgment.
  • Prescriptive Period to Declare Incapacity & Restitution (Art. 1040): The action for a declaration of incapacity and for the recovery of the inheritance, devise, or legacy shall be brought within five (5) years from the time the disqualified person took possession thereof. It may be brought by anyone who may have an interest in the succession.
  • Conflict of Laws — The Four (4) Aspects Governed by the National Law of the Decedent (Art. 1039 & Art. 16, par. 2): Under Article 1039, capacity to succeed is governed by the law of the nation of the decedent. Together with Article 16, paragraph 2, there are exactly four (4) aspects of succession always governed by the national law of the decedent regardless of the nature or location of the property (Mnemonic: "O-A-I-C"):
    1. O — Order of succession;
    2. A — Amount of successional rights;
    3. I — Intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions; and
    4. C — Capacity to succeed (Art. 1039).

Question 4.1: Conceived Unborn Child (Arts. 40–41, 1025) + Simultaneous Death (Art. 43)

Rich widower Don Rafael and his married legitimate son, Tomas, died together when their private helicopter crashed into the sea. There was zero evidence as to who between Don Rafael and Tomas died first. Don Rafael left a net intestate estate of ₱80 Million and is survived by his other legitimate son, Victor. At the time of the crash, Tomas's wife, Wendy, was six (6) months pregnant with Tomas's legitimate child, Baby Boy X. Two weeks after the crash, Wendy went into premature labor (so Baby Boy X had an intrauterine life of six and a half months). Baby Boy X was completely delivered alive when the umbilical cord was cut, cried loudly, and was baptized in the hospital, but died ten (10) hours after complete delivery. Wendy now claims half of Don Rafael's ₱80 Million estate (₱40 Million), arguing either that Tomas inherited from Don Rafael and transmitted the share to her, or that Baby Boy X represented Tomas and then transmitted the ₱40 Million to Wendy upon dying 10 hours after birth. Rule on Wendy's claims and distribute Don Rafael's ₱80 Million estate.

  • A Answer: Both of Wendy's claims are untenable. Wendy receives ₱0, and Don Rafael's surviving legitimate son Victor inherits the entire ₱80 Million estate.
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Article 43 of the Civil Code, when there is doubt as between two or more persons called to succeed each other as to who died first, in the absence of proof, they are presumed to have died at the same time, and there shall be no transmission of rights from one to the other. While Article 43 does not bar the right of representation (Art. 970), the representative must possess capacity to succeed (Art. 1025).
    2. Under Article 1025, par. 2 in relation to Article 41 of the Civil Code, a child already conceived at the time of the decedent's death is capable of succeeding only if it is born later under the conditions of Article 41. Under Article 41, if the fetus had an intrauterine life of less than seven (7) months, it is not deemed born if it dies within twenty-four (24) hours after its complete delivery from the maternal womb (cutting of the umbilical cord).
  • A Application:
    1. No Transmission from Don Rafael to Tomas (Art. 43): Because Don Rafael and Tomas died together without proof of who died first, they are presumed to have died simultaneously under Article 43. Thus, Tomas never inherited from Don Rafael and transmitted nothing to his widow Wendy.
    2. Baby Boy X Never Acquired Civil Personality (Art. 41): Although a grandchild can represent a simultaneously deceased father despite Article 43, Baby Boy X had an intrauterine life of only 6.5 months (less than 7 months) and died 10 hours after the umbilical cord was cut—failing to survive the mandatory 24-hour period required by Article 41. Consequently, Baby Boy X was never deemed born, never acquired juridical personality or capacity to succeed under Article 1025, and transmitted nothing to his mother Wendy.
  • C Conclusion: Victor, as the sole surviving and capacitated legitimate child of Don Rafael, inherits the entire ₱80 Million intestate estate in his own right.

Question 4.2: Unworthiness vs. Disinheritance (Arts. 1032, 1033, 922) & The Unfaithful Spouse Anomaly (Art. 1032[5])

Don Miguel had two legitimate sons, Gabriel and Samuel. In 2022, Gabriel was convicted by final judgment of attempting to murder Don Miguel. Don Miguel never executed a will, but in 2024, while hospitalized, Don Miguel tearfully hugged Gabriel in front of three nurses and verbally declared: "My son, I forgive you completely; we are reconciled." No written pardon was ever signed. Meanwhile, Samuel was convicted by final judgment of concubinage with Don Miguel's young second wife, Carmela. In 2025, Don Miguel died intestate leaving a net estate of ₱90 Million, survived by his wife Carmela, his son Gabriel (who has no children), and his son Samuel (who has one legitimate daughter, Nina). Distribute Don Miguel's ₱90 Million intestate estate.

  • A Answer: Wife Carmela receives ₱45 Million (1/2 of the intestate estate) and Granddaughter Nina receives ₱45 Million (1/2 of the intestate estate, representing her unworthy father Samuel). Gabriel and Samuel both receive ₱0.
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Article 1032(2) of the Civil Code, a person convicted of an attempt against the life of the decedent is incapacitated to succeed by reason of unworthiness in both testate and intestate succession. Under Article 1033, when the decedent did not execute a will disinheriting the offender (so the rules of unworthiness govern rather than Article 922 on disinheritance), unworthiness is erased only by an express written pardon or by subsequent institution in a will. A mere oral reconciliation without a written document does not cure unworthiness.
    2. Under Article 1032(5), any person convicted of adultery or concubinage with the spouse of the decedent is unworthy to succeed the offended decedent. However, Article 1032(5) disqualifies only the paramour (Samuel) and does not disqualify the unfaithful spouse (Carmela) in intestate succession (absent a decree of legal separation or testamentary disinheritance under Art. 921[4]).
    3. Under Article 1035, if the excluded unworthy person is a child or descendant of the decedent and leaves children or descendants, the latter shall acquire his right to the legitime (and in intestacy, represent the unworthy parent in his entire intestate share under Articles 970, 981, and 1035).
  • A Application:
    1. Gabriel's Disqualification (Arts. 1032[2] & 1033): Because Don Miguel died intestate and never executed a written pardon under Article 1033, his oral forgiveness in the hospital did not erase Gabriel's unworthiness. Since Gabriel has no children to represent him, Gabriel is completely excluded (₱0).
    2. Samuel's Disqualification and Representation by Nina (Arts. 1032[5] & 1035): Samuel is unworthy under Article 1032(5) due to his conviction of concubinage with Don Miguel's wife Carmela. However, because unworthiness allows representation in the direct descending line (Arts. 981, 1035), Samuel's legitimate daughter Nina represents Samuel and takes Samuel's entire intestate share.
    3. Carmela's Capacity (Art. 1032[5] Paradox): Wife Carmela is not rendered unworthy by Article 1032(5) and was never disinherited in a will. Thus, she concurs with one legitimate descending line (Samuel's line, represented by Nina). Under Article 996, when the surviving spouse concurs with one legitimate child/line in intestacy, they divide the estate 1/2 each: ₱45 Million to Carmela and ₱45 Million to Nina.
  • C Conclusion: Carmela receives ₱45 Million, Nina receives ₱45 Million by representation of Samuel, and Gabriel and Samuel receive ₱0.

I. CHARACTERISTICS AND PREREQUISITES OF ACCEPTANCE AND REPUDIATION (ARTICLES 1041–1043)

A. Big Picture and the Three Principal Characteristics ("V-R-I")

  • Definitions:
    • Acceptance: The act by which an heir, devisee, or legatee manifests the desire to succeed to the inheritance, devise, or legacy.
    • Repudiation: The act by which that person manifests the desire not to succeed, strictly observing the formalities required by law (Art. 1051).
🧠 TIP

🧠 MEMORY ANCHOR — The 3 Characteristics of Acceptance & Repudiation: "V-R-I" 1. B. Essential Doctrinal Nuances Under Articles 1041 and 1042

  1. Legitime May Be Repudiated After Death (Art. 1041): While the testator cannot unlawfully deprive a compulsory heir of the legitime, the compulsory heir himself is completely free to repudiate his legitime after the decedent's death.
  2. Partial Acceptance and Partial Repudiation (Paras Commentary vs. Arts. 954–955): Senator Paras notes that partial acceptance and partial repudiation are generally permissible under the New Civil Code because the old Spanish Civil Code prohibition was omitted; however, this does not override Articles 954–955, under which a legatee or devisee cannot accept a gratuitous part/legacy while repudiating an onerous part/legacy imposed by the testator.
  3. Choice Must Be Pure and Absolute (Art. 1042): While the testator may impose conditions on a disposition, the heir's act of accepting or repudiating cannot be made subject to a term or condition (e.g., "I accept only if the land doubles in value" or "I repudiate for the next two years" is improper because it creates uncertainty).

C. Two Prerequisites Before Accepting or Repudiating (Article 1043)

Under Article 1043, no person may accept or repudiate an inheritance unless he is certain of:

  1. The death of the person from whom he is to inherit; AND
  2. His right to the inheritance. (Memory Anchor: "Death + Right").
Connection of Article 1043 with Presumptive Death (Articles 390–392)
  • 1. Ordinary Absence (Article 390 — No Danger of Death):
    • 10 Years: An absentee is presumed dead for the purpose of opening his succession only after an absence of ten (10) years (Note: The ordinary 7-year presumption of death for other civil purposes does NOT open succession!).
    • 5 Years (If Disappeared After Age 75): If the absentee disappeared after the age of seventy-five (75) years, an absence of five (5) years shall be sufficient in order that his succession may be opened.
  • 2. Extraordinary Absence / Qualified Absence (Article 391 — Disappearance in Danger of Death):
    • 4 Years: Presumed dead for all purposes, including the division of the estate among the heirs, after four (4) years in three cases: (a) a person on board a vessel lost during a sea voyage, or an aeroplane which is missing, who has not been heard of for 4 years since the loss; (b) a person in the armed forces who has taken part in war and has been missing for 4 years; and (c) a person who has been in danger of death under other circumstances and his existence has not been known for 4 years.
  • 3. Effect if the Absentee Returns or turns out to be Alive (Article 392): He shall recover his property in the condition in which it may be found, and the price of any property that may have been alienated or the property acquired therewith; but he cannot claim either fruits or rents.

C. Landmark Case: Michael C. Guy v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 163707, Sept. 15, 2006)

  • Facts: Minor children (Karen and Kamille) claimed successional rights as illegitimate children in the estate of Sima Wei. Petitioner Michael Guy invoked a Release and Waiver of Claim previously signed by their mother, Remedios, upon receiving ₱300,000 and an educational plan, arguing that the mother had validly waived/repudiated the minors' hereditary shares.
  • Issue: Did the Release and Waiver signed by the mother bar the minor children's hereditary claims?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No. First, a valid waiver requires an intentional relinquishment of a known right, and the document did not clearly extinguish their hereditary shares. Second, under Article 1044 of the Civil Code, parents and guardians may repudiate the inheritance left to their wards ONLY WITH JUDICIAL AUTHORIZATION. Because no judicial approval was ever obtained by the mother, any purported repudiation of the minor children's inheritance was void and ineffective.

III. MODES OF ACCEPTANCE (ARTICLES 1049–1050) VS. FORMALITIES OF REPUDIATION (ARTICLE 1051)

A. Express vs. Tacit Acceptance and Mere Preservation (Article 1049)

Under Article 1049 of the Civil Code, acceptance may be express or tacit (in addition to presumed acceptance after 30 days from the order of distribution under Art. 1057):

  1. Express Acceptance (Art. 1049, par. 2): One made in a public or private document.
  2. Tacit (Implied) Acceptance (Art. 1049, par. 3): One resulting from acts by which the intention to accept is necessarily implied, or which one would have no right to do except in the capacity of an heir (e.g., demanding partition, alienating estate property as owner, or appropriating rental income as one's own hereditary share).
  3. Acts of Mere Preservation or Provisional Administration (Art. 1049, par. 4): Acts of mere preservation or provisional administration (such as repairing a leaking roof to prevent ruin, paying urgent estate taxes, or harvesting perishable fruits while keeping estate accounts) do NOT imply an acceptance of the inheritance, if through such acts, the title or capacity of an heir has not been assumed.

B. The Three Statutory Instances of Tacit Acceptance by Acts of Ownership or "Renunciation" (Article 1050)

This is a favorite Bar and Quiz topic emphasized by both Dean Navarro and the Oct 26 Notes! Even if an heir labels a document a "Waiver", "Renunciation", or "Repudiation", the law treats it as a TACIT ACCEPTANCE followed by a transfer/donation/sale (Acceptance $\rightarrow$ Disposition) in three (3) instances under Article 1050:

  1. Sale, Donation, or Assignment of One's Hereditary Right (Art. 1050[1]): When the heir sells, donates, or assigns his right to a stranger, or to all his co-heirs, or to any of them.
    • Rationale: Disposing of a share is an exercise of jus disponendi (an act of ownership); one cannot sell, donate, or assign what one has not first accepted!
    • Case Anchor — Ignacio v. Martinez (G.R. No. L-10722, Feb. 18, 1916, 33 Phil. 576): Crispulo Martinez executed a document "renouncing" and assigning his hereditary interest to a married couple in payment of a ₱2,500 debt (note: ₱2,500 in the SC report, misprinted as ₱25,000 in Paras). Ruling: Despite using the word "renounce," transferring a hereditary share to designated persons in satisfaction of a debt is an assignment for valuable consideration, which constitutes acceptance under Article 1050.
  2. Selective Gratuitous Renunciation (Art. 1050[2]): When the heir renounces the inheritance, even though gratuitously, for the benefit of one or more of his co-heirs (i.e., selecting specific co-heirs rather than renouncing indiscriminately).
    • Rationale: By choosing who among the co-heirs will receive his share (e.g., "I gratuitously waive my share in favor of my brother B" when there are co-heirs B, C, D, and E), the heir is actually making a selective donation to B—an act of ownership that presupposes prior acceptance!
  3. Renunciation for a Price (Art. 1050[3], Clause 1): When the heir renounces it for a price in favor of all his co-heirs indiscriminately.
    • Rationale: Receiving valuable consideration turns the renunciation into a sale of hereditary rights, which constitutes tacit acceptance.
The Sole Exception — True Indiscriminate Gratuitous Repudiation (Art. 1050[3], Clause 2)

"However, if this renunciation should be gratuitous, and the co-heirs in whose favor it is made are those upon whom the portion renounced should devolve by virtue of accretion, the inheritance shall not be deemed as accepted."

  • Professor's / Dean Navarro's Illustration (Art. 1050): Decedent X dies leaving five co-heirs: A, B, C, D, and E.
    • If A executes a document stating, "I gratuitously renounce my share in favor of my brother B," A has IMPLIEDLY ACCEPTED the inheritance under Art. 1050(2) (and then donated it to B, triggering donor's tax and making A's share reachable by estate rules as accepted!).
    • Conversely, if A executes a public instrument gratuitously and indiscriminately renouncing his share in favor of B, C, D, and E—the exact co-heirs who would take his share by accretion—A has NOT accepted; it is a true, valid repudiation!

C. Strict Formalities of Repudiation (Article 1051)

Unlike acceptance (which can be made in a private document or tacitly by conduct), repudiation is NEVER presumed or tacit! Because repudiation surrenders a property right and alters the rights of co-heirs and creditors, Article 1051 requires that repudiation be made in one of three (3) exclusive formal ways:

  1. In a public instrument (acknowledged before a notary public);
  2. In an authentic instrument (an indubitable genuine writing whose authenticity is admitted or proved); OR
  3. By petition presented to the court having jurisdiction over the testamentary or intestate proceedings.

IV. CREDITOR PROTECTION, JUS TRANSMISSIONIS, PLURALITY, DUAL CAPACITIES, IRREVOCABILITY, AND THE 30-DAY RULE (ARTICLES 1052–1057)

A. Protection of the Repudiating Heir's Creditors (Article 1052 — Accion Pauliana in Succession)

Under Article 1052 of the Civil Code:

"If the heir repudiates the inheritance to the prejudice of his own creditors, the latter may petition the court to authorize them to accept it in the name of the heir. The acceptance shall benefit the creditors only to an extent sufficient to cover the amount of their credits. The excess, should there be any, shall in no case pertain to the renouncer, but shall be adjudicated to the persons to whom, in accordance with the rules established in this Code, it may belong."

  • Four Requisites of Article 1052:
    1. The heir was already indebted when the repudiation was made (the credit existed prior to repudiation);
    2. There was a valid formal repudiation under Art. 1051;
    3. The repudiation prejudices the heir's own creditors (i.e., the heir is insolvent or his remaining personal assets are insufficient to pay his debts—note this protects the heir's creditors, whereas the decedent's creditors are already paid before the net estate is distributed); and
    4. The creditors obtain judicial authorization to accept in the name of the heir (even a single prejudiced creditor may petition).
  • Crucial Rule on the Excess: The creditors only get enough to pay their credits. The renouncing heir gets ZERO (₱0) of the excess! The excess goes to the persons entitled to the vacant share under the Professor's Vacancy Matrix (Substitution $\rightarrow$ Accretion $\rightarrow$ Intestate Succession / Co-heirs in their own right, and never to the renouncing heir's children by representation because a renouncer cannot be represented under Art. 977!).
  • Paras Illustrations on Art. 1052:
    • Illustration 1 (Basic Excess): A leaves ₱1,000,000 to insolvent B, who owes X ₱800,000. B validly repudiates. Creditor X petitions the court and gets ₱800,000; the ₱200,000 excess goes to B's substitute/co-heirs/legal heirs; B gets ₱0.
    • Illustration 2 (Renouncer's Child Gets Nothing): Testator A leaves a ₱400,000 estate equally to legitimate sons B (₱200k) and C (₱200k). B has a child D. B is insolvent, owes X ₱50,000, and repudiates. Creditor X recovers ₱50,000 from B's share. Who gets the remaining ₱150,000 of B's share? Not B's child D (because a renouncer cannot be represented under Art. 977)! Instead, Son C takes the entire ₱150,000 (₱100,000 in C's own right as the repudiated legitime under Art. 1021, par. 2, plus ₱50,000 by accretion in the free portion under Art. 1021, par. 1)!

B. Right of Transmission (Jus Transmissionis — Article 1053) vs. Right of Representation (Article 970)

Under Article 1053 of the Civil Code:

"If the heir should die without having accepted or repudiated the inheritance his right shall be transmitted to his heirs."

  • Required Chronology for Art. 1053 (Survival + Undecided Death):
    1. Original Decedent A dies.
    2. Heir B SURVIVES A (so A's inheritance opens in B's favor at the moment of A's death under Art. 777).
    3. B dies later WITHOUT having accepted or repudiated A's inheritance.
    4. B's unexercised right to choose (accept or repudiate A's inheritance) forms part of B's own estate and is transmitted to B's heirs (C)!
  • The Double Acceptance Rule in Art. 1053: Because the unexercised option to accept/repudiate A's estate is an asset inside B's estate:
    • If C repudiates B's estate, C cannot accept A's estate!
    • However, if C accepts B's estate, C steps into B's unexercised option and may choose to either accept OR repudiate A's estate!

  • Plurality of Heirs (Art. 1054): "Should there be several heirs called to the inheritance, some of them may accept and the others may repudiate it." Each co-heir (including multiple heirs of B under Art. 1053) decides independently for his own proportionate share.

    • General Rule: The acceptance or repudiation of an inheritance, once made, is irrevocable.

Question 5.1: Selective Waiver vs. True Repudiation (Art. 1050) and Creditor Protection (Art. 1052)

Don Severino died intestate leaving a net estate of ₱12 Million and survived by his three legitimate children: Alba, Bruno, and Clara (₱4 Million intestate share each). Bruno has a legitimate son, Dino, and owes his judgment creditor, Bank X, ₱2.5 Million. Bruno has no other assets. Consider two independent scenarios: Scenario A: Bruno executes a notarized public instrument stating: "Out of love for my youngest sister Clara, who is a struggling single mother, I hereby gratuitously renounce and repudiate my entire ₱4 Million share in favor of Clara."* Scenario B: Bruno executes a notarized public instrument stating: "I hereby unconditionally and gratuitously repudiate my entire ₱4 Million inheritance from Don Severino."* Bank X immediately petitions the court under Article 1052 to accept in Bruno's name. (a) In Scenario A, did Bruno validly repudiate his inheritance? (b) In Scenario B, how should Bruno's ₱4 Million share be distributed among Bank X, Bruno, Bruno's son Dino, Alba, and Clara?

  • A Answer:
    • (a) No. In Scenario A, Bruno's selective gratuitous renunciation in favor of Clara alone is deemed a tacit acceptance of the inheritance under Article 1050(2), followed by a donation of his ₱4 Million share to Clara.
    • (b) In Scenario B, Bruno's ₱4 Million repudiated share is distributed as follows: ₱2.5 Million to Bank X (to cover its credit), and the ₱1.5 Million excess accrues equally to his co-heirs Alba (₱750,000) and Clara (₱750,000) by right of accretion under Articles 1052 and 1018. Bruno and his son Dino both receive ₱0.
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Article 1050(2) of the Civil Code, an inheritance is deemed accepted when the heir renounces it, even though gratuitously, for the benefit of one or more of his co-heirs (rather than indiscriminately in favor of all co-heirs to whom the share would devolve by accretion under Art. 1050[3]).
    2. Under Article 1052, if an heir validly repudiates the inheritance to the prejudice of his existing creditors, the creditors may petition the court to authorize them to accept it in the name of the heir, but the acceptance benefits the creditors only to an extent sufficient to cover the amount of their credits. The excess shall in no case pertain to the renouncer, nor can the renouncer's child represent him (Art. 977), so in intestate succession the excess accrues to the renouncer's co-heirs under Article 1018.
  • A Application:
    1. Scenario A: By singling out Clara to the exclusion of Alba, Bruno exercised an act of ownership (jus disponendi) over his share. Under Article 1050(2), Bruno is deemed to have accepted the ₱4 Million share first and then donated it to Clara.
    2. Scenario B: Because Bruno executed a pure, unconditional repudiation in a public instrument (Art. 1051) while insolvent, Bank X is entitled under Article 1052 to recover its ₱2.5 Million credit from Bruno's ₱4 Million share. As to the ₱1.5 Million excess, Bruno gets nothing (Art. 1052), and Bruno's son Dino gets nothing because an heir who repudiates cannot be represented (Art. 977). Under Article 1018, the ₱1.5 Million excess accrues equally to Bruno's co-heirs Alba and Clara (₱750,000 each).
  • C Conclusion: In Scenario A, Bruno tacitly accepted the inheritance; in Scenario B, Bank X gets ₱2.5 Million, Alba and Clara get ₱750,000 each of the excess, and Bruno and Dino get ₱0.

Question 5.2: Jus Transmissionis (Art. 1053) vs. Dual Capacities (Art. 1055)

(a) Testator A died on May 1, 2026, leaving a legacy of ₱5 Million to his best friend B. B survived A, but on May 15, 2026, before B could accept or repudiate the ₱5 Million legacy, B died in a road accident, survived by his sole legitimate son, C. Because B had ₱10 Million in personal debts, C executed a public instrument repudiating B's inheritance, but simultaneously filed a manifestation in A's probate court accepting the ₱5 Million legacy from A. Can C validly claim the ₱5 Million legacy from A? (b) Suppose instead that Testator A instituted his legitimate brother D to one-half (1/2) of his estate in a valid will, leaving the other one-half (1/2) undisposed (where D is also A's sole intestate heir). D executed a public instrument repudiating his testamentary share under the will because it had an annoying condition attached, and now claims the entire estate as A's sole intestate heir. Rule on D's claim.

  • A Answer:
    • (a) No, C cannot claim the ₱5 Million legacy from A because by repudiating B's estate, C forfeited the transmitted right to accept A's legacy under Article 1053.
    • (b) D's claim must be denied. Under Article 1055, par. 1, by repudiating his inheritance as a testamentary heir, D is deemed by law to have repudiated in both capacities (testamentary and intestate).
  • L Legal Basis:
    1. Under Article 1053 of the Civil Code (jus transmissionis), if an heir/legatee survives the decedent but dies without having accepted or repudiated the inheritance/legacy, his unexercised right to choose is transmitted to his own heirs as part of his estate. Consequently, to exercise the transmitted option over the first decedent's (A's) estate, the second heir (C) must first accept the inheritance of the transmitting heir (B).
    2. Under Article 1055, par. 1, if a person who is called to the same inheritance both as an heir by will and ab intestato repudiates the inheritance in his capacity as a testamentary heir, he is understood to have repudiated it in both capacities.
  • A Application:
    1. As to C (Art. 1053): Because B was a voluntary friend/legatee (where representation never applies) who survived A, C's only link to A's ₱5 Million legacy is through B's estate via Article 1053. When C repudiated B's estate, C rejected all rights forming part of B's patrimony, including the transmitted option to accept A's legacy.
    2. As to D (Art. 1055, par. 1): Because D rejected A's express testamentary institution, the law conclusively presumes that D has also rejected the intestate share arising from A's presumed will.
  • C Conclusion: Both C's and D's claims fail.
