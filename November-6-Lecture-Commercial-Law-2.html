I. FUNDAMENTAL CONCEPTS AND CLASSIFICATIONS OF MARKS

Statutory Definitions and Classifications (Sec. 121, R.A. 8293)

Under Section 121.1 of the Intellectual Property Code (R.A. 8293), a mark means any visible sign capable of distinguishing the goods (trademark) or services (service mark) of an enterprise and shall include a stamped or marked container of goods. Jurisprudence further defines a trademark as any word, name, symbol, emblem, sign, or device, or any combination thereof, adopted and used by a manufacturer or merchant to identify his goods and distinguish them from those manufactured, sold, or dealt in by others.

  • Trademark: A visible sign adopted and used by a manufacturer or merchant exclusively to identify and distinguish tangible goods or products (e.g., Apple or Samsung logos on cellular phones) from those of other manufacturers.
  • Service Mark: A visible sign capable of distinguishing the services rendered by one enterprise from those of others, rather than physical goods (e.g., ride-hailing apps like Move It and Angkas, broadcasting networks like ABS-CBN, hospitals like St. Luke's and Lorma, and educational institutions). A service mark is not permanently restricted to services; it transitions into a trademark when applied directly to physical merchandise or goods produced by that enterprise.
  • Collective Mark (Sec. 121.2): Any visible sign designated as such in the application for registration and capable of distinguishing the origin or any other common characteristic, including the quality of goods or services, of different enterprises which use the sign under the control of the registered owner of the collective mark. It is non-exclusive to a single brand and may appear across multiple competing brands that meet the certifying body's standards or geographical sourcing requirements.
  • Trade Name (Sec. 121.3): The name or designation identifying or distinguishing an enterprise or business entity itself, rather than a specific logo or emblem attached to a product (e.g., Harvard, Ateneo, or LUELCO for the La Union Electric Company).

Co-Existence of Marks on a Single Product

Professor's Illustration: A single commercial product can simultaneously bear a trade name, a trademark, and a collective mark. Consider a cup of coffee sold by Starbucks: the word designation "Starbucks" identifies the business enterprise and functions as the trade name; the green twin-tailed Siren emblem printed on the cup distinguishes the product and serves as the trademark; and if the packaging bears a "Halal Certified" seal or a fair-trade origin symbol shared by compliant producers, that seal operates as a collective mark.

Purposes of Trademark Protection

  1. Protection of Property Rights: To safeguard the business proprietor's investment, goodwill, and commercial reputation built around the mark in the course of trade.
  2. Protection of the Consuming Public: To prevent the public from being deceived or defrauded by counterfeit goods and misleading claims of authenticity (e.g., online sellers on Shopee or Lazada falsely claiming a ₱500 counterfeit item is a "legit" version of a ₱1,500 mall product).

Territoriality Principle and the Well-Known Mark Exception

  • General Rule & Market Extension: While intellectual property rights are generally territorial, trademarks acknowledge no strict territorial boundaries where a trader's goods have become known and identified by his use of the mark in the market.
  • Well-Known Mark Doctrine: Even if a foreign mark is not registered in the Philippines, the Intellectual Property Office (IPO) will deny a local applicant's attempt to register an identical or confusingly similar mark if the foreign mark is internationally and locally well-known (e.g., a local merchant cannot register the brand "Apple" for electronic devices in the Philippines by claiming Apple Inc. originated in California).

Separation of Trademark from the Underlying Property

A trademark registration confers no exclusive property right over the generic article or product itself, nor does it empower the trademark owner to prevent competitors from manufacturing, producing, or selling the same type of commodity. Exclusivity attaches strictly to the mark, brand name, or symbol used to identify the origin of the goods, unless the product itself is separately covered by an invention patent.

Application Example: Jisulife manufactures high-capacity portable handheld fans and holds a registered trademark over the "Jisulife" brand. Competing brands like Goojodoq and Orashare subsequently manufacture and sell portable handheld fans of similar utility at lower prices. Jisulife cannot sue Goojodoq or Orashare to prohibit them from manufacturing portable fans on the basis of its trademark registration. Unless Jisulife holds a valid invention patent over a novel technical mechanism inside the fan, its trademark only prevents competitors from affixing the "Jisulife" mark to their fans.

II. ACQUISITION, MAINTENANCE, AND PRIORITY OF OWNERSHIP

Acquisition of Ownership and the First-to-File Rule

  • Registration as Prima Facie Evidence (Sec. 138): The rights in a mark are acquired through valid registration with the IPO. A Certificate of Registration serves as prima facie evidence of the validity of the registration, the registrant's ownership of the mark, and the registrant's exclusive right to use the same in connection with the goods or services and those that are related thereto. Because it is merely prima facie, it is disputable and may be overturned by proof of prior bad-faith appropriation or superior prior ownership.
  • The First-to-File Rule: Where two or more applications are filed for the same or confusingly similar marks, the first-to-file rule prioritizes the applicant with the earliest filing date or priority date, operating to block any subsequent application from being registered for the same or closely related goods.

Registration vs. Prior Actual Use in Infringement Suits

It is crucial to distinguish between the First-to-File Rule (which determines priority in registration proceedings before the IPO) and the Right to File an Infringement or Cancellation Action based on prior actual use:

  • Unlike patents—where an inventor has zero right to sue for patent infringement without an issued Certificate of Letters Patent—the prior actual user and true owner of a mark or trade name who has established goodwill in the market is protected against subsequent bad-faith registrants.
  • A prior continuous user in good faith cannot be sued for trademark infringement by a subsequent filer who managed to register the mark first (Sec. 159.1), and an unregistered well-known mark or trade name owner may oppose registration, petition for cancellation, or sue for unfair competition (and trade name infringement) on the strength of actual use alone.

Acquisition of Trade Names Without Registration (Sec. 165.2)

Under Section 165.2(a) of the IPC, trade names are acquired strictly by actual use in commerce, not by registration. Notwithstanding any law or regulation providing for an obligation to register trade names, such names shall be protected, even prior to or without registration, against any unlawful act committed by third parties. Any subsequent use of the trade name by a third party—whether as a trade name, trademark, or collective mark—likely to mislead the public is deemed unlawful. Furthermore, under Section 165.4, any change in the ownership of a trade name must be made together with the transfer of the enterprise or part thereof identified by that name.

III. NON-REGISTRABLE MARKS UNDER SECTION 123.1

Under Section 123.1 of the Intellectual Property Code, a mark cannot be registered if it falls under any of the following statutory prohibitions:

  • Sec. 123.1(a) – Immoral, Deceptive, Scandalous, or Disparaging Matter: Consists of immoral, deceptive, or scandalous matter, or matter which may disparage or falsely suggest a connection with persons (living or dead), institutions, beliefs, or national symbols, or bring them into contempt or disrepute.
    • Classroom Example: Naming a brand of vape pods "Rizal" is non-registrable because it falsely suggests a connection with the national hero and brings his memory into disrepute.
  • Sec. 123.1(b) – Flags, Coats of Arms, and Official Insignia: Consists of the flag or coat of arms or other insignia of the Philippines or any of its political subdivisions, or of any foreign nation, or any simulation thereof.
  • Sec. 123.1(c) – Names, Portraits, or Signatures of Living Individuals or Deceased Presidents:
    • Living Individual: Cannot be registered except by his or her written consent.
    • Deceased President of the Philippines: Cannot be registered during the life of his widow, if any, except by the written consent of the widow. (If the widow is already deceased, the statutory bar under this specific clause ceases to apply).
  • Sec. 123.1(d) – Identical or Confusingly Similar to a Registered Mark or Earlier Filing: Identical with a registered mark belonging to a different proprietor or a mark with an earlier filing or priority date, in respect of: (i) the same goods or services, or (ii) closely related goods or services, or (iii) if it nearly resembles such a mark as to be likely to deceive or cause confusion.
  • Sec. 123.1(e) – Identical or Confusingly Similar to an Internationally and Locally Well-Known Mark (Identical/Similar Goods): Identical with, or confusingly similar to, or constitutes a translation of a mark considered by the competent authority of the Philippines to be well-known internationally and in the Philippines, whether or not it is registered here, as being already the mark of a person other than the applicant, when used for identical or similar goods or services. In determining whether a mark is well-known, account is taken of the knowledge of the relevant sector of the public, rather than of the public at large, including knowledge obtained from promotion of the mark.
  • Sec. 123.1(f) – Well-Known Marks Registered in the Philippines (Dissimilar/Unrelated Goods): Identical with, or confusingly similar to, or constitutes a translation of a well-known mark which is registered in the Philippines, even with respect to goods or services which are not similar to those applied for, provided that use of the mark would indicate a connection between those goods/services and the owner of the registered mark, and the interests of the owner of the registered mark are likely to be damaged by such use.
    • Classroom Example: Attempting to register "Apple" for sanitary napkins or "Samsung" for alcoholic beverages will be rejected under Sec. 123.1(f). Even though sanitary napkins and liquor are totally unrelated to electronics, the marks are so famous that consumers would be misled into believing Apple or Samsung expanded into those product lines, damaging the famous brands' reputation.
  • Sec. 123.1(g) – Misleading Marks: Likely to mislead the public, particularly as to the nature, quality, characteristics, or geographical origin of the goods or services.
  • Sec. 123.1(h) – Generic Marks: Consists exclusively of signs that are generic for the goods or services that they seek to identify (e.g., registering the word "Water" for a water refilling station or "Rice" for a rice retail store).
  • Sec. 123.1(i) – Genericized (Customary) Marks: Consists exclusively of signs or of indications that have become customary or usual to designate the goods or services in everyday language or in bona fide and established trade practice (e.g., "Cat's eye" for retroreflective road studs or "Escalator" for moving staircases).
  • Sec. 123.1(j) – Descriptive Marks: Consists exclusively of signs or of indications that may serve in trade to designate the kind, quality, quantity, intended purpose, value, geographical origin, time or production of the goods or rendering of the services, or other characteristics of the goods or services.
  • Sec. 123.1(k) – Shapes Necessitated by Technical or Functional Factors: Consists of shapes that may be necessitated by technical factors or by the nature of the goods themselves or factors that affect their intrinsic value (e.g., registering a plain geometric "Square" or "Triangle" shape).
  • Sec. 123.1(l) – Color Alone: Consists of color alone, unless defined by a given form (e.g., attempting to register the color "Red" or "Blue" in the abstract).
  • Sec. 123.1(m) – Contrary to Public Order or Morality: Any mark that offends public order or morals.

Crucial Caveat: The Combination Exception for Non-Registrable Terms

While generic terms, genericized terms, descriptive indicators, geographical names, basic shapes, and single colors under Sections 123.1(h) to (l) cannot be registered when taken singly or in isolation, a distinctive combination of such terms, colors, or shapes is registrable as a composite mark.

  • Examples of Valid Combinations: "Abanao Square", "Centerpoint Square", and "St. Francis Square" (combining a street/proper name with a geometric shape); "Red Ribbon" (combining a single color with an object); "Blue Eagles" (combining a color with an animal).

IV. TESTS AND JURISPRUDENCE ON CONFUSING SIMILARITY

Comparative Table: Dominancy Test vs. Holistic Test

Parameter Dominancy Test (Prevailing Rule) Holistic / Totality Test (Abandoned)
Current Legal Status Prevailing and mandatory test; codified under Sec. 155.1 of R.A. 8293 (Mang Inasal & Kolin doctrines). Expressly abandoned by recent Supreme Court En Banc jurisprudence.
Analytical Focus Focuses on the similarity of the prevalent, main, essential, or dominant features of the competing marks that might cause confusion or deception. Mandated consideration of the entirety of the marks as applied to the products, including background labels, packaging, font sizes, and price points.
Treatment of Minor Differences Disregards minor variations in background, packaging, or secondary words if the dominant visual, aural, or connotative feature is copied. Allowed infringers to escape liability by pointing to minor distinctions in packaging, company names on the back label, or consumer sophistication.
Standard of Proof Likelihood of confusion is sufficient; actual confusion in the marketplace is not required. Often required a closer showing of overall visual deception when labels were viewed side-by-side in their entirety.

Colorable Imitation and the Principle of Idem Sonans

  • Colorable Imitation (Sec. 155.1): Refers to such a close or ingenious imitation as to be calculated to deceive ordinary purchasers, giving such attention as a buyer usually gives, into purchasing one article for another (e.g., copying the exact color scheme, tomato/banana placement, and visual layout of a competing ketchup bottle like Papa vs. UFC, even if the brand name is slightly altered).
  • Principle of Idem Sonans (Sound-Alike Test): A phonetic test used to resolve confusing similarity whereby a mark with a different spelling is nevertheless ruled confusingly similar to an earlier mark because both sound alike when pronounced, creating a likelihood of public deception as to identical or related goods.
Professor's Illustration: Suppose a merchant manufactures powdered orange juice and applies to register the trademark "TONGUE" (spelled T-O-N-G-U-E). Even though the spelling and literal dictionary meaning differ from the famous powdered juice brand "TANG", the IPO will reject the application under the principle of idem sonans. Because both marks are pronounced identically when spoken by consumers buying powdered juice, the phonetic similarity is likely to deceive the purchasing public. (Similarly, in Aqua Best vs. a hypothetical rival water station across the street named Aqua Fest, the rhyme and dominant features create immediate confusion).

Supreme Court Jurisprudence: Where Confusing Similarity WAS Found

In the following landmark cases discussed in the lecture, the Supreme Court or IPO applied the Dominancy Test and barred registration or found infringement due to confusing similarity over identical or closely related goods:

  1. Standard Brands / Planters Peanuts vs. Philippine Planters Cordial Peanuts:
    • Facts: The owner of the famous "Planters Peanuts" mark opposed the registration of "Philippine Planters Cordial Peanuts" for peanut products.
    • Issue: Whether "Philippine Planters Cordial Peanuts" is confusingly similar to "Planters Peanuts".
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. Applying the Dominancy Test, the prevalent and dominant feature of both marks is the word "Planters" used on identical peanut products, making consumer confusion inevitable despite the addition of the words "Philippine" and "Cordial".
  2. Del Monte Corporation v. Court of Appeals (Sunshine Sauce):
    • Facts: Sunshine Sauce used Del Monte's catsup bottles and adopted a label design with a similar color scheme, font placement, and tomato illustration.
    • Issue: Whether Sunshine's label constituted a colorable imitation of Del Monte's trademark.
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. The court looks at the dominant visual features and overall impression left on an ordinary, casual buyer who does not scrutinize labels side-by-side, finding that the prominent design similarities created a likelihood of confusion.
  3. Converse Rubber Corp. v. Universal Rubber Products (Converse vs. Universal Converse and Device):
    • Facts: Universal Rubber sought to register "Universal Converse and Device" for rubber shoes, which was opposed by Converse Rubber Corp.
    • Issue: Whether the inclusion of the word "Converse" infringed upon Converse Rubber Corp.'s mark and trade name.
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. "Converse" is the dominant word that identifies the footwear in the public mind; appropriating that dominant word for the same class of goods causes likelihood of confusion.
  4. Societe des Produits Nestle v. Court of Appeals (Master Roast / Master Blend vs. Flavor Master):
    • Facts: Nestle, owner of "Master Roast" and "Master Blend" for coffee, opposed CFC Corporation's application to register "Flavor Master" for instant coffee.
    • Issue: Whether "Flavor Master" is confusingly similar to "Master Roast" and "Master Blend".
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. The word "Master" is the dominant feature of the competing coffee marks through which consumers identify the product, rendering "Flavor Master" confusingly similar under the Dominancy Test.
  5. Amigo Manufacturing v. Cluett Peabody (Gold Toe vs. Gold Top):
    • Facts: The owner of "Gold Toe" for men's socks sought to cancel the registration of "Gold Top" for socks.
    • Issue: Whether "Gold Top" is confusingly similar to "Gold Toe".
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. Changing a single letter from "Toe" to "Top" while retaining the dominant word "Gold" and similar visual design on identical hosiery goods is a classic colorable imitation.
  6. McDonald's Corporation v. L.C. Big Mak Burger, Inc. (Big Mac vs. L.C. Big Mak):
    • Facts: McDonald's sued L.C. Big Mak Burger, Inc., which operated fast-food stalls selling hamburgers under the name "L.C. Big Mak".
    • Issue: Whether "L.C. Big Mak" infringed McDonald's registered "Big Mac" mark.
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. Applying the Dominancy Test and idem sonans, "Big Mak" is aurally identical and visually similar to the dominant mark "Big Mac" used for burger products, creating a clear likelihood of confusion as to source and sponsorship.
  7. Prosource International v. Horphag Research (Pycnogenol vs. Picolon) & Dermaline v. Myra Pharmaceuticals (Dermaline vs. Dermalin):
    • Facts: In Prosource, "Picolon" was used for a pharmaceutical supplement competing with "Pycnogenol"; in Dermaline, the applicant sought to register "Dermalin" for skincare products despite the registered mark "Dermaline".
    • Issue: Whether the competing marks are confusingly similar to the senior marks.
    • Ruling / Ratio: Yes in both cases. Under the Dominancy Test and idem sonans, the aural and visual similarities between Pycnogenol/Picolon and the near-identical spelling and pronunciation of Dermaline/Dermalin for skincare products create a strong likelihood of confusion.
  8. Additional Cases Finding Confusing Similarity (Dominancy Test Applied):
    • Gould Brand vs. Gold Brand: Confusingly similar due to phonetic and visual dominance over identical pump/industrial products.
    • W&W vs. W&B: Confusingly similar because the stylized letter "W" served as the dominant feature describing the product's quality.
    • Skechers vs. Strong Rubber: Strong Rubber copied the stylized "S" logo of Skechers on rubber shoes; even with different brand names, copying the dominant stylized "S" device constituted infringement.
    • Mang Inasal vs. OK Hotdog Inasal / Hatok Inasal: The word "Inasal" written in the exact same bold red-and-yellow stylized font with a sun-ray background copied the dominant feature of Mang Inasal.
    • Philips vs. Philight: Confusingly similar for lightbulbs/electrical products due to the dominant prefix "Phil-" and similar visual presentation.
    • Metro Magazine vs. Philippine Daily Inquirer's "Metro": Inquirer's application to register "Metro" for a magazine supplement was denied because "Metro Magazine" was already registered and used for the same periodical class.
    • Ginebra San Miguel vs. Ginebra Kapitan: Use of the dominant word "Ginebra" and similar bottle/label aesthetics on gin products was held confusingly similar.

Supreme Court Jurisprudence: Where Confusing Similarity WAS NOT Found

Conversely, in several historical cases (many decided before the definitive abandonment of the Holistic Test or based on the "discerning buyer" doctrine), the Supreme Court held that no confusing similarity existed:

  • Citigroup, Inc. v. Citystate Savings Bank, Inc. (Citibank vs. City Cash): The registration of "City Cash" for ATM/banking services was allowed over Citibank's opposition because the dominant prefix of Citigroup's marks is "Citi" (spelled with an "i"), whereas "City Cash" was visually and aurally distinct when viewed in relation to Citystate Savings Bank.
  • Emerald Garment Manufacturing Corp. v. Court of Appeals (Lee vs. Stylistic Mr. Lee) & Levi Strauss & Co. (Levi's vs. LSJ): The Supreme Court found no confusing similarity between "Lee" jeans and "Stylistic Mr. Lee" jeans (nor between "Levi's" and "LSJ" customized pants), reasoning that jeans are not inexpensive everyday items and that buyers of pants are more discerning, carefully inspecting the fit, price, and exact brand before purchasing. (Note: The Professor critically noted how unpredictable this reasoning was given the near-identical font styles).
  • Fruit of the Loom, Inc. v. Court of Appeals (Fruit of the Loom vs. Fruit for Eve): Held not confusingly similar for undergarments because the words "of the Loom" and "for Eve" and their respective fruit-cluster vs. single-apple logos were visually distinct.
  • Lorlein vs. Orlane: Held not confusingly similar for high-end cosmetics/perfumes due to distinct visual presentation and discerning luxury consumers.
  • Mark VII vs. Mark Ten (Industrial Hoses): Held not confusingly similar despite sharing the word "Mark" on hoses, as the numerals designated distinct models/brands.
  • Great White Shark Enterprises v. Caralde (Greg Norman Shark Logo vs. Shark): Held not confusingly similar because the composite visual appearance of the competing shark logos and accompanying text were sufficiently distinct.

V. SCOPE OF PROTECTION, DOCTRINE OF UNRELATED GOODS, AND WELL-KNOWN MARKS

Three-Tier Scope of Protection for Trademark Owners

A valid trademark registration protects the trademark owner across three distinct commercial spheres:

1
IDENTICAL GOODS
Absolute Bar
Same product class (e.g. orange juice under "Tang")
2
RELATED GOODS
Shared Trade Channels
Similar nature (e.g. ready-to-drink bottled juice under "Tang")
3
EXPANSION ZONE
Natural Business Growth
Future expansion within normal commercial trajectory
  1. Identical Goods: Absolute protection against unauthorized third parties using an identical or confusingly similar mark on the exact same product (e.g., another merchant selling powdered orange juice under the name "Tang").
  2. Similar or Closely Related Goods: Protection against third parties using the mark on goods that, while not identical in form, belong to the same class or share the same nature and distribution channels (e.g., a competitor selling ready-to-drink bottled liquid juice under the name "Tang").
  3. Zones of Normal Business Expansion: The exclusive right to expand the use of the mark into market areas that fall within the natural or normal expansion of the registered owner's business (e.g., the maker of "Tang" powdered juice expanding into ready-to-drink bottled beverages).

The Doctrine of Unrelated Goods

Under the Doctrine of Unrelated Goods, one who has adopted, used, and registered a trademark on his goods cannot prevent the adoption, use, and registration of the exact same trademark by others on totally unrelated articles of different kinds.

  • Statutory Basis: A Certificate of Registration (Sec. 138) entitles the registrant to exclusive use of the mark only for the goods or services specified in the certificate and those that are related thereto. Where goods are non-competing and wholly unrelated in nature, purpose, and channels of trade, no likelihood of confusion as to source arises.
Professor's Illustration: In a viral social media anecdote, a mother instructed her child to buy "Champion" so she could do the laundry, referring to Champion detergent soap. The child instead bought a "Champion" chocolate/energy drink and poured it into the washing machine. Under the Doctrine of Unrelated Goods (and idem sonans), the manufacturer of Champion laundry detergent cannot prohibit a beverage company from registering "Champion" for a powdered drink because laundry soap and beverages are completely unrelated goods sold in different supermarket aisles for entirely different purposes.

Jurisprudence on the Doctrine of Unrelated Goods

In the following cases discussed in the lecture, the Supreme Court held that the competing goods were unrelated, thereby allowing both marks to co-exist:

  • Faberge, Inc. v. Intermediate Appellate Court (Brut Cologne vs. Brut Briefs): The registered owner of "Brut" for men's toiletries, cologne, and deodorant could not stop another company from registering "Brut 33" for men's briefs and underwear, as cosmetics and garments were deemed unrelated.
  • Hickok Manufacturing Co., Inc. v. Court of Appeals (Hickok Wallets/Belts vs. Hickok Shoes): The owner of "Hickok" for leather wallets, belts, and handkerchiefs could not prevent a local manufacturer from registering "Hickok" for Marikina leather shoes, as the Court ruled they were non-competing goods. (Note: The Professor critiqued this older ruling, noting both are leather fashion accessories).
  • Canon Kabushiki Kaisha v. Court of Appeals (Canon Cameras/Printers vs. Canon Sandals): The famous Japanese manufacturer of "Canon" cameras, copiers, and printers could not bar the registration of "Canon" for rubber sandals and footwear, as electronics and footwear are totally unrelated.
  • Lotus Soy Sauce vs. Lotus Cooking Oil / Products: Held unrelated in historical jurisprudence despite both being kitchen/cooking items.
  • Mighty Corporation v. E. & J. Gallo Winery (Gallo Wines vs. Gallo Cigarettes): The California maker of "Gallo" wines could not enjoin the local use of "Gallo" on cigarettes and tobacco products, as the Court ruled wines and cigarettes are non-competing, unrelated products sold through different trade restrictions.
  • Taiwan Kolin Corp. v. Kolin Electronics Co., Inc. (Kolin Air Conditioners/Home Appliances vs. Kolin Transformers/Inverters/Voltage Regulators): In the earlier Kolin case referenced in the lecture, the Court allowed "Kolin" for television sets and air conditioners despite an existing registration for "Kolin" on automatic voltage regulators, transformers, and converters, classifying consumer home appliances as distinct from power-supply electronic devices.

Well-Known Marks and the Paris Convention

A well-known mark is one considered by the competent authority of the Philippines to be well-known internationally and in the Philippines, whether or not it is registered here, as already being the mark of a person other than the applicant for registration (Sec. 123.1[e]).

  • Protection Under the Paris Convention: Under Article 6bis and Article 8 of the Paris Convention (and Sec. 160 in relation to Sec. 131.1 of the IPC), any foreign national or juridical person from a member state has the legal capacity to sue for the protection of its well-known trademark or trade name even if it is not registered and is not doing business in the Philippines.
  • Remedies Available to Unregistered Well-Known Marks (International or Local):
    1. Opposition: Oppose an application for registration of a mark that is identical with, confusingly similar to, or constitutes a translation of the well-known mark.
    2. Cancellation: File a petition to cancel an existing bad-faith registration of the well-known mark.
    3. Unfair Competition: File an action for unfair competition if the infringer is passing off its goods as those of the well-known mark owner.
Application Example: Suppose Marsha's Bibingka, a famous delicacy maker in Ilocos (or Good Shepherd ube jam in Baguio), has never registered its brand name with the IPO. If a third party attempts to register "Marsha's Bibingka" at the IPO, the original owner can oppose the registration because it is a locally well-known mark/trade name acquired through prior actual use. Furthermore, if a tourist-site vendor sells inferior rice cakes or jam while misrepresenting to buyers that they came from Marsha's or Good Shepherd, the original proprietor can immediately sue for unfair competition without needing a trademark registration.
  • Landmark Cases Applying Well-Known Mark Protection:
    • Fredco Manufacturing Corp. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College: A local garment company was barred from registering "Harvard" for shirts and jeans because "Harvard" is an internationally well-known trade name and mark owned by Harvard University, protected under the Paris Convention even though Harvard only offers educational services abroad.
    • Mirpuri v. Court of Appeals (Barbizon): Local registration of "Barbizon" for brassieres and ladies' undergarments was cancelled because "Barbizon" was an internationally well-known mark for lingerie protected under the Paris Convention.
    • In-N-Out Burger, Inc. v. Sehwani, Inc.: Even though the American burger chain In-N-Out Burger had no physical restaurant branches in the Philippines, it had legal capacity to sue and cancel a local company's unauthorized registration and use of the "In-N-Out" mark and double-arrow logo because it was an internationally well-known mark.

The Theory of Trademark Dilution

Trademark dilution is the lessening of the capacity of a famous mark to identify and distinguish goods or services, regardless of the presence or absence of: (1) competition between the owner of the famous mark and other parties, or (2) likelihood of confusion, mistake, or deception. Dilution protects famous marks against blurring (whittling away the mark's uniqueness) or tarnishment (associating the famous mark with inferior-quality goods that degrade its reputation).

  • Levi Strauss & Co. v. Sevilla / Dockers v. Patos: Levi Strauss, owner of the "Dockers" khaki pants brand featuring a wing-shaped emblem, sought to block the registration of "Patos" for footwear, arguing under the theory of dilution that the Patos duck logo resembled the Dockers wings and that inferior Patos shoes would dilute and tarnish the Dockers brand reputation. The Supreme Court rejected the dilution claim, holding that Dockers failed to prove the rigorous requisites of fame and substantial similarity required to establish dilution against Patos.

VI. GENERIC, DESCRIPTIVE, AND GENERICIZED MARKS & THE DOCTRINE OF SECONDARY MEANING

Generic Marks vs. Descriptive Marks

  • Generic Marks (Sec. 123.1[h]): Refer to the common descriptive name of an article or substance, or the genus of which the particular product is a species (e.g., "Blouse", "Pants", "Shoes"). Generic terms belong to the public domain (publici juris), refer to the basic nature of the wares, and are never legally protectable or registrable when taken alone.
  • Descriptive Marks (Sec. 123.1[j]): Convey an immediate idea of the ingredients, qualities, functions, characteristics, or geographical origin of the goods to a consumer who has never seen the product, without requiring any exercise of imagination or perception (e.g., naming a food product simply "Vanilla" or "Matcha").
  • The Combination Rule in Jurisprudence: While generic, descriptive, or geographical words are non-registrable singly, combining them into a composite mark creates a registrable whole (though the registrant cannot claim exclusive monopoly over the individual generic words):
    • Lyceum of the Philippines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals: The word "Lyceum" (from Greek/Latin, meaning a public hall for lectures or an educational institution) is a generic noun that cannot be exclusively appropriated alone; however, the composite name "Lyceum of the Philippines University" is registrable as a whole.
    • Asia Brewery, Inc. v. Court of Appeals: The phrase "Pale Pilsen" is a generic/descriptive designation for a type of light lager beer and cannot be monopolized singly; hence, both "San Miguel Pale Pilsen" and "Beer Pale Pilsen" (Beer na Beer) were allowed to use the words "Pale Pilsen" in combination with their distinct brand names.
    • Other Valid Combinations: "Master Feeds" (combining generic "Master" and "Feeds"); "Marlboro Cigarettes" (combining a geographical place with a product); "Papa Banana Ketchup" (combining generic "Papa" with product description); "De La Salle" (French generic phrase meaning "of the room/hall" combined with the university designation); "San Francisco Coffee"; "Coffee Match"; and "Sakura Electronics" (combining the Japanese word for cherry blossom with electronics, which is also arbitrary as to electronics).

Genericized Marks (Genericide)

Under Section 123.1(i), a genericized mark is a term that originated as a valid, exclusive, proprietary trademark, but through widespread everyday language or established trade practice, lost its distinctiveness and devolved into the customary generic name for the product category itself (a process known as genericide). Once a mark becomes legally genericized in a jurisdiction, it can no longer be exclusively registered or enforced as a trademark for that product.

  • Colloquial vs. Legal Genericide: In everyday Filipino parlance, consumers habitually use famous brand names to refer to the product genus—such as saying "Colgate" for toothpaste, "Pampers" for baby diapers, or "Lucky Me" for instant pancit canton noodles. While these remain active trademarks in the Philippines because their owners actively police them, several historical marks discussed in the lecture have suffered actual legal genericide in various jurisdictions:
    • Anime Manga (originally a Viz Media trademark that expired in 2016); Air Fryer (originally a Philips trademark invalidated in the US as descriptive); Frigidaire (a brand name used colloquially by older generations to mean any refrigerator); Aspirin (originally a Bayer trademark for acetylsalicylic acid, declared generic in the US); Cat's Eye (originally registered by Percy Shaw / Reflecting Roadstuds Ltd. for retroreflective road safety studs); Cellophane (originally a DuPont trademark for transparent cellulose film); Circline (originally a General Electric trademark for circular fluorescent lamps); Dry Ice (originally a trademark of Dry Ice Corp. of America for solid carbon dioxide); Escalator (originally an Otis Elevator Co. trademark until 1950); Flip Phone (originally a Motorola trademark); Flit Gun (originally a Standard Oil Co. trademark for hand-pumped insecticide sprayers); Heroin (originally a Friedrich Bayer & Co. trademark in 1898); Hovercraft (originally a Saunders-Roe trademark); Kerosene, Lanolin, and Launderette (coin-operated self-service laundry).

The Doctrine of Secondary Meaning (Sec. 123.2)

The Doctrine of Secondary Meaning is a statutory exception (under Section 123.2 of the IPC) to the rule that descriptive, geographical, or shape/color marks (under Secs. 123.1[j], [k], and [l]) cannot be registered.

  • Operative Rule: A word or phrase originally incapable of exclusive appropriation because it is geographical or descriptive may nevertheless be registered if it has been used so long and exclusively by one producer in commerce in the Philippines that, to the purchasing public, the word or phrase has come to mean that the article is exclusively his product.
  • Prima Facie Statutory Period: Under Sec. 123.2, the IPO may accept as prima facie evidence that the mark has become distinctive proof of substantially exclusive and continuous use thereof by the applicant in commerce in the Philippines for five (5) years before the date on which the claim of distinctiveness is made. (Note: The Doctrine of Secondary Meaning rescues descriptive or geographical marks, never purely generic terms).

Jurisprudence on the Doctrine of Secondary Meaning

  1. Ang Tibay (Ana Ang v. Toribio Teodoro):
    • Facts: Toribio Teodoro continuously used the phrase "Ang Tibay" (Tagalog for "how durable" or "strength") for shoes and slippers for decades, and sued Ana Ang for registering "Ang Tibay" for pants and shirts.
    • Issue: Whether the phrase "Ang Tibay" was merely descriptive or protected under the Doctrine of Secondary Meaning.
    • Ruling / Ratio: Protected. Even if considered descriptive of durability (or an exclamation), through Teodoro's long, exclusive, and continuous use in commerce, "Ang Tibay" had acquired a secondary meaning in the mind of the public as identifying footwear and apparel originating exclusively from Teodoro's factory.
  2. Shang Properties Realty Corp. v. St. Francis Development Corp. (St. Francis Square vs. St. Francis Shangri-La Place):
    • Facts: The developer of St. Francis Square sued Shang Properties to stop it from using "St. Francis" in its condo project "The St. Francis Shangri-La Place", both located along St. Francis Street in Ortigas Center.
    • Issue: Whether "St. Francis" had acquired a secondary meaning exclusive to St. Francis Development Corp.
    • Ruling / Ratio: No. Because both real estate projects were located along St. Francis Street, the term "St. Francis" was geographically descriptive of their location, and the complainant failed to prove nationwide or exclusive secondary meaning sufficient to monopolize the street/saint's name against other businesses in the same area.

Descriptively Misleading Marks (Sec. 123.1[g])

A mark is descriptively misleading and barred from registration if it falsely suggests a characteristic, nature, ingredient, or origin that the product does not possess, or misleads the public into confusing a brand with a generic substance.

  • Classroom Case Illustration (Axlod): An application to register the pharmaceutical brand name "Axlod" was rejected as descriptively misleading because its pronunciation and spelling closely mimicked an existing generic drug name (spelled with a double "C"), which would mislead doctors and patients into thinking the branded product was the generic drug itself.

VII. CANCELLATION OF REGISTRATION AND TRADEMARK INFRINGEMENT

Exclusive Rights Conferred by Registration (Sec. 147.1)

The owner of a registered mark has the exclusive right to prevent all third parties not having the owner's consent from using in the course of trade identical or similar signs or containers for goods or services which are identical or similar to those in respect of which the trademark is registered, where such use would result in a likelihood of confusion. In case of the use of an identical sign for identical goods or services, a likelihood of confusion shall be presumed.

Petition for Cancellation of Registration (Sec. 151.1)

Any person who believes that he is or will be damaged by the registration of a mark may file a petition to cancel the registration with the Bureau of Legal Affairs (BLA) of the IPO under the following prescriptive periods and grounds:

  1. Within five (5) years from the date of the registration of the mark under the IPC.
  2. At any time, if the registered mark becomes the generic name for the goods or services (genericide), or has been abandoned, or its registration was obtained fraudulently or contrary to the provisions of the IPC, or if the registered mark is being used by, or with the permission of, the registrant so as to misrepresent the source of the goods or services.
  3. At any time, if the registered owner of the mark, without legitimate reason, fails to use the mark within the Philippines, or to cause it to be used in the Philippines by virtue of a license, during an uninterrupted period of three (3) years or longer.

Landmark Case on Non-Use Cancellation: W Land Holdings, Inc. v. Starwood Hotels and Resorts Worldwide, Inc. (W Hotels Case)

  • Facts: W Land Holdings filed a petition to cancel Starwood's Philippine trademark registration for "W" (W Hotels) on the ground of three years of uninterrupted non-use, arguing that Starwood had no physical "W Hotel" building operating in the Philippines. Starwood countered that its mark was actively used in the Philippines through its interactive website where Filipino residents regularly viewed and booked rooms at W Hotels abroad.
  • Issue: Whether interactive online hotel reservation services accessible to and patronized by consumers in the Philippines constitute valid "actual use in commerce in the Philippines" sufficient to defeat a petition for cancellation for non-use.
  • Ruling / Ratio: Yes. The Supreme Court ruled that in the age of e-commerce, actual use of a service mark does not require a physical brick-and-mortar hotel building within Philippine territory, provided that the interactive website targets and enables Philippine consumers to consummate commercial transactions (room reservations and payments) within the Philippines.

Trademark Infringement (Sec. 155) and Essential Elements

Under Section 155 of the IPC, trademark infringement is committed by any person who, without the consent of the owner of the registered mark:

  • Sec. 155.1: Uses in commerce any reproduction, counterfeit, copy, or colorable imitation of a registered mark or the same container or a dominant feature thereof in connection with the sale, offering for sale, distribution, advertising of any goods or services (including other preparatory steps necessary to carry out the sale) on or in connection with which such use is likely to cause confusion, mistake, or deception; or
  • Sec. 155.2: Reproduces, counterfeits, copies, or colorably imitates a registered mark or a dominant feature thereof and applies such reproduction to labels, signs, prints, packages, wrappers, receptacles, or advertisements intended to be used in commerce.
  • Moment of Infringement: Infringement takes place at the very moment any of the acts in Sec. 155.1 or 155.2 are committed, regardless of whether there is an actual sale of goods or services using the infringing material.

Five Essential Elements of Trademark Infringement:

  1. The trademark being infringed is registered with the IPO (subject to the exception for trade names under Sec. 165.2);
  2. The trademark is reproduced, counterfeited, copied, or colorably imitated by the infringer;
  3. The infringing mark is used in connection with the sale, offering for sale, or advertising of any goods, business, or services (or preparatory steps thereto);
  4. The use or application of the mark is likely to cause confusion or mistake or to deceive purchasers or others as to the goods or services or as to their source or origin; and
  5. The use or application is without the consent of the trademark owner or the assignee thereof.

Remedies, Provisional Reliefs, and Limitations (Secs. 156–159)

  • Civil and Criminal Remedies of the Owner (Secs. 156–157, 170):
    • Recovery of Damages: Reasonable profit the complainant would have made, or the profit the defendant actually made, or a reasonable percentage of the defendant's gross sales. Where there is actual intent to mislead the public or to defraud the complainant, the court may in its discretion double the award of damages (Sec. 156.3).
    • Impounding and Injunction: Impounding of sales invoices and infringing goods during the pendency of the action (Sec. 156.2), and issuance of a preliminary or permanent injunction (Sec. 156.4).
    • Destruction / Disposal Outside Channels of Commerce (Sec. 157.1): The court may order that infringing goods, labels, signs, packages, and plates/molds be disposed of outside the channels of commerce or destroyed without compensation (similar to Optical Media Board raids destroying counterfeit discs).
    • Criminal Action (Sec. 170): Independent of civil and administrative remedies, criminal prosecution may be instituted carrying penalties of imprisonment and fines.
  • Provisional Reliefs and Search & Seizure Rules:
    • Under the Rules of Procedure for Intellectual Property Rights Cases, any order issued by the trial court is immediately executory, unless restrained by the Court of Appeals or Supreme Court, EXCEPT in two instances: (1) an Order of Destruction of infringing goods where a Motion for Reconsideration is filed; and (2) an Order of Release of Seized Goods where a search warrant is quashed.
    • Search and Seizure Governance: Civil search and seizure writs in IP cases are governed by A.M. No. 02-1-06-SC (Rule on Search and Seizure in Civil Actions for Infringement of Intellectual Property Rights), whereas criminal search warrants are governed by Rule 126 of the Rules of Court. A victim of a wrongful search and seizure may file a civil action for damages, or a criminal action with a separate civil action for damages.
  • Statutory Limitations on Actions for Infringement (Sec. 159):
    1. Prior Good-Faith User (Sec. 159.1): A registered mark shall have no effect against any person who, in good faith, before the filing date or the priority date, was using the mark for the purposes of his business or enterprise (provided that his right may only be transferred or assigned together with his enterprise or business).
    2. Innocent Printer (Sec. 159.2): Where an infringer who is engaged solely in the business of printing the mark or other infringing materials for others is an innocent infringer, the owner of the right infringed shall be entitled as against such printer only to an injunction against future printing (no damages or criminal liability).
    3. Innocent Publisher / Media Advertiser (Sec. 159.3): Where the infringement is contained in a paid advertisement in a newspaper, magazine, or electronic communication, the remedies of the owner against an innocent publisher or distributor shall be limited to an injunction against the presentation of such advertising matter in future issues.

VIII. UNFAIR COMPETITION AND THE MADRID PROTOCOL

Unfair Competition (Sec. 168: Passing Off / Palming Off)

Under Section 168 of the IPC, unfair competition consists in passing off (or palming off) or attempting to pass off upon the public the goods or business of one person as the goods or business of another, with the end and probable effect of deceiving the public and defrauding the competitor of his legitimate trade.

  • Statutory Modes of Committing Unfair Competition (Sec. 168.3):
    1. General Appearance Passing Off (Sec. 168.3[a]): Selling one's goods and giving them the general appearance of the goods of another manufacturer or dealer—either as to the goods themselves, or in the wrapping of the packages, devices, or words thereon, or in any other feature of their appearance—which would be likely to influence purchasers to believe that the goods offered are those of a manufacturer or dealer other than the actual manufacturer (e.g., Divisoria stalls selling "Star Box Coffee" with a crocodile logo mimicking Starbucks, "Jag Daniel's" mimicking Jack Daniel's, counterfeit Louis Vuitton or Chanel bags, or bogus LPG dealers painting rusty red tanks blue and affixing fake seals to pass them off as Petron Gasul).
    2. Service Misrepresentation (Sec. 168.3[b]): Employing any artifice, device, or means calculated to induce the false belief that such person is offering the services of another who has identified such services in the mind of the public (e.g., falsely representing oneself as an authorized agent of St. Peter Life Plan to collect money from clients).
    3. False Discrediting Statements (Sec. 168.3[c]): Making any false statement in the course of trade or committing any other act contrary to good faith of a nature calculated to discredit the goods, business, or services of another.

International Registration of Marks: The Madrid Protocol

The Madrid Protocol (governing the Madrid System for the International Registration of Marks) is an international treaty administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) that provides a centralized "one-stop-shop" solution for registering and managing trademarks worldwide.

  • Operative Mechanism: It allows a natural or juridical person who is a national of, domiciled in, or has a real and effective industrial or commercial establishment in a Contracting Party (and who holds a basic application or registration in its home IP Office, such as IPOPHL) to file a single international application, in one language, and pay one set of fees to seek protection in up to 97 (or more) designated member states/contracting parties.
  • Term and Renewal: An international registration under the Madrid Protocol is effective for ten (10) years, and may be renewed for additional periods of ten (10) years upon payment of the prescribed fees.

IX. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF COPYRIGHT AND PROTECTED WORKS

Definition and Statutory Characteristics of Copyright

Copyright is an intangible, incorporeal right granted by statute to the author or originator of certain literary, scholarly, scientific, or artistic productions, whereby he or she is invested for a specific period with the sole and exclusive privilege of multiplying copies of the same and publishing and selling them.

  1. Incorporeal and Distinct from the Material Object (Sec. 181): Unlike patents (which cover physical inventions/processes) and trademarks (which attach to commercial goods), copyright is a purely incorporeal right that exists independently of the physical medium (e.g., buying a physical book or painting does not transfer the copyright over the literary work or artwork).
  2. Originality in Creation: It is granted to the creator or originator of a work that is original (independently created by the author and not copied from another work) in the literary, scientific, and artistic domains.
  3. Definite Statutory Period: Economic rights are not perpetual; they subsist only for a specific statutory duration (generally the author's lifetime plus 50 years).
  4. Strictly Statutory Right: Copyright is not a natural right inherent in an author upon publication, but a creature of statute governed strictly by the Intellectual Property Code.

Principle of Automatic Protection from the Moment of Creation (Sec. 172.2)

Under Section 172.1 and 172.2 of the IPC, literary and artistic works are protected by the sole fact of their creation, irrespective of their mode or form of expression, as well as of their content, quality, and purpose.

  • Effect of Registration and Deposit with the National Library / IPO: A Certificate of Registration and Deposit issued by the National Library or Supreme Court Library serves merely as a notice of recording and registration of the work. It does not confer copyright ownership, is not conclusive proof of ownership (as a non-author might fraudulently register someone else's work first), and is not a condition precedent for filing a copyright infringement suit.

Original Literary and Artistic Works (Sec. 172.1)

Section 172.1 enumerates the fifteen (15) classes of original works protected from the moment of creation:

  • (a) Books, pamphlets, articles, and other writings;
  • (b) Periodicals and newspapers;
  • (c) Lectures, sermons, addresses, dissertations prepared for oral delivery, whether or not reduced in writing or other material form;
  • (d) Letters;
  • (e) Dramatic or dramatico-musical compositions; choreographic works or entertainment in dumb shows;
  • (f) Musical compositions, with or without words;
  • (g) Works of drawing, painting, architecture, sculpture, engraving, lithography, or other works of art; models or designs for works of art;
  • (h) Original ornamental designs or models for articles of manufacture, whether or not registrable as an industrial design, and other works of applied art;
  • (i) Illustrations, maps, plans, sketches, charts, and three-dimensional works relative to geography, topography, architecture, or science;
  • (j) Drawings or plastic works of a scientific or technical character;
  • (k) Photographic works including works produced by a process analogous to photography; lantern slides;
  • (l) Audiovisual works and cinematographic works;
  • (m) Pictorial illustrations and advertisements;
  • (n) Computer programs; and
  • (o) Other literary, scholarly, scientific, and artistic works.

Useful Articles and the Denicola Test of Conceptual Separability

A useful article is an object having an intrinsic utilitarian function that is not merely to portray the appearance of the article or to convey information (e.g., an ordinary chair, table, bed, or lamp).

  • General Rule: Purely useful articles are excluded from copyright eligibility because functional utility is the domain of patents/utility models, not copyright.
  • Exception – The Denicola Test (Physical or Conceptual Separability): A useful article becomes eligible for copyright protection as a work of applied art only to the extent that it incorporates a design element that is physically or conceptually separable from the utilitarian aspect of the underlying product.
Professor's Illustration: An ordinary lighting lamp, stool, or bed is a utilitarian object and cannot be copyrighted. However, consider three classroom designs: (1) a lamp sculpted into a life-sized standing horse with a lightbulb on its head; (2) a woven rattan chair by Kenneth Cobonpue featuring intricate sculptural fringes at the back; or (3) a bed topped with a sculptural cloud canopy. Under the Denicola Test, because the artistic horse sculpture, the woven sculptural backrest, and the cloud design can be conceptually separated from the utilitarian function of providing light or a surface to sit/sleep on, those separable artistic design elements are protectable by copyright.

Derivative Works (Sec. 173) and Multiple Copyrights in a Single Item

  • Derivative Works (Sec. 173.1): Include: (a) dramatizations, translations, adaptations, abridgments, arrangements, and other alterations of literary or artistic works (e.g., adapting original movies like One More Chance, ZsaZsa Zaturnnah, or Bar Boys into stage musicals/theater plays, or recording a musical revival/new arrangement of an old song); and (b) collections of literary, scholarly, or artistic works, and compilations of data which are original by reason of the selection, coordination, or arrangement of their contents.
  • Treatment as New Works (Sec. 173.2): Derivative works are protected as new works, provided that such new work shall not affect the force of any subsisting copyright upon the original work employed, nor be construed to imply any right to such use of the original work without the original author's consent, nor extend the copyright term of the original work.
  • Co-Existing Copyrights in a Single Published Book: Multiple distinct copyrights can subsist in a single physical item. In a published book, the author owns the copyright over the literary content/text, whereas the publisher (under Sec. 174) owns a separate copyright consisting of the exclusive right to authorize the reproduction of the typographical arrangement, layout, cover composition, and general page appearance of the published edition.

Non-Copyrightable Subject Matter (Secs. 175–176)

  1. Unprotected Subject Matter (Sec. 175): No copyright protection shall extend to:
    • Any idea, procedure, system, method or operation, concept, principle, discovery, or mere data as such, even if they are expressed, explained, illustrated, or embodied in a work (Idea-Expression Dichotomy);
    • News of the day and other miscellaneous facts having the character of mere items of press information (because nobody "creates" factual events; however, the specific audiovisual broadcast clip or journalist's written article expressing the news is copyrightable);
    • Any official text of a legislative, administrative, or legal nature, as well as any official translation thereof (e.g., statutes, administrative rules, court decisions, and speeches/dissertations pronounced in courts of justice, administrative agencies, or deliberative assemblies).
  2. Works of the Government of the Philippines (Sec. 176.1): No copyright shall subsist in any work of the Government of the Philippines. However, prior approval of the government agency or office wherein the work is created shall be necessary for exploitation of such work for profit, and such agency may impose the payment of royalties as a condition.
  3. TV Game Show Formats and Mechanics: In television network rivalries (e.g., Eat Bulaga vs. It's Showtime segments like Super Sireyna vs. That's My Tomboy or similar game show mechanics), the general format and game mechanics are unprotectable ideas/systems under Sec. 175; only the actual audiovisual recording (video clips) and specific scripts are protected by copyright.

X. ECONOMIC RIGHTS, MORAL RIGHTS, AND RULES ON OWNERSHIP

Economic Rights (Sec. 177) and the Test of Substantiality

Under Section 177 of the IPC, economic rights grant the author the exclusive right to carry out, authorize, or prevent the following acts (often in exchange for royalties):

  1. Reproduction of the work or a substantial portion of the work;
  2. Dramatization, translation, adaptation, abridgment, arrangement, or other transformation of the work (the right to authorize derivative works—e.g., the heirs of Mars Ravelo licensing Darna or Captain Barbell comics to ABS-CBN or GMA for TV/movie adaptations during the subsistence of the copyright term);
  3. First public distribution of the original and each copy of the work by sale or other forms of transfer of ownership;
  4. Rental of the original or a copy of an audiovisual/cinematographic work, sound recording, computer program, compilation of data, or musical work in graphic form, regardless of ownership of the physical copy;
  5. Public display of the original or a copy of the work (displaying at a place open to the public or where a substantial number of persons outside a normal circle of family and social acquaintances are gathered);
  6. Public performance of the work; and
  7. Other communication to the public of the work.
  • The Test of Substantiality: To constitute unlawful reproduction or infringement, it is NOT necessary that the whole or even a large portion of the work shall have been copied. Infringement exists if so much is taken that the value of the original work is sensibly diminished, or the labors of the original author are substantially and to an injurious extent appropriated by another.

The First-Sale Doctrine vs. Rental Right & Droit de Suite

  • First-Sale / Exhaustion Doctrine (Sec. 177.3): Once the copyright owner consents to the first public sale or distribution of a particular lawful copy of a work, the copyright owner's exclusive right to control the subsequent resale of that specific physical copy is exhausted. An individual who lawfully purchases an authentic copy of a book (e.g., importing several genuine copies of Fifty Shades of Grey from abroad and reselling them in the Philippines) has the right to resell, display, or dispose of that particular copy without committing copyright infringement.
  • Exception – Rental Right Not Exhausted (Sec. 177.4): The First-Sale Doctrine covers only subsequent sales/dispositions, NOT commercial rentals of audiovisual works, sound recordings, computer programs, or sheet music. A buyer of an original DVD or software disc cannot open a commercial rental shop and rent it out without the copyright owner's consent.
  • Droit de Suite / Right to Follow (Sec. 200): In every sale or lease of an original work of painting or sculpture, or of the original manuscript of a writer or composer, subsequent to the first disposition thereof by the author, the author or his heirs shall have an inalienable right to participate in the gross proceeds of the sale or lease to the extent of five percent (5%). This right subsists during the lifetime of the author and for fifty (50) years after his death (excluding prints, etchings, engravings, works of applied art, or similar works wherein the author primarily derives gain from reproductions).

  1. Right of Attribution / Paternity (Sec. 193.1): To require that the authorship of the works be attributed to him, in particular, the right that his name, as far as practicable, be indicated in a prominent way on the copies and in connection with the public use of his work. Under Section 198.1, the Right of Attribution is PERPETUAL (lasts forever, even after the 50-year post-mortem economic copyright expires).
  2. Right of Alteration / Withdrawal (Sec. 193.2): To make any alterations of his work prior to, or to withhold it from, publication.
  3. Right of Integrity (Sec. 193.3): To object to any distortion, mutilation, or other modification of, or other derogatory action in relation to, his work which would be prejudicial to his honor or reputation (e.g., Mars Ravelo's heirs objecting to an adaptation's art direction if it distorts and degrades the creator's artistic reputation).
  4. Right Against False Attribution (Sec. 193.4): To restrain the use of his name with respect to any work not of his own creation or in a distorted version of his work.
  • Rules on Waiver of Moral Rights (Sec. 195): Moral rights cannot be assigned or licensed, though an author may waive his moral rights by a written instrument. However, no such waiver shall be valid if its effect is to permit another:
    1. To use the name of the author, or the title of his work, or otherwise to make use of his reputation with respect to any version or adaptation of his work which, because of alterations therein, would substantially tend to injure the literary or artistic reputation of another author; or
    2. To use the name of the author with respect to a work he did not create.

XI. STATUTORY LIMITATIONS, DOCTRINE OF FAIR USE, AND COPYRIGHT INFRINGEMENT

Statutory Limitations on Copyright (Sec. 184)

Under Section 184.1 of the IPC, the following acts do NOT infringe copyright:

  • (a) Private or Charitable Performance: The recitation or performance of a work, once it has been lawfully made accessible to the public, if done privately and free of charge or if made strictly for a charitable or religious institution or society;
  • (b) Quotations (Fair Use Compatible): The making of quotations from a published work if they are compatible with fair use and only to the extent justified for the purpose (including press summaries), provided that the source and the name of the author, if appearing on the work, are mentioned (e.g., academic footnotes; merely typing "CTTO" on social media without naming the known author/source does not satisfy this statutory proviso);
  • (c) Public Lectures & Media Articles on Current Topics: Reproduction or communication to the public by mass media of articles on current political, social, economic, scientific, or religious topics, or public lectures/addresses delivered in public, if for information purposes only and not expressly reserved, provided the source is clearly indicated;
  • (d) Reporting of Current Events: Reproduction and communication to the public of literary, scientific, or artistic works as part of reports of current events by means of photography, cinematography, or broadcasting to the extent necessary for the purpose (e.g., Kapuso Mo, Jessica Soho briefly showing a painting by Juan Luna while reporting on an art exhibition);
  • (g) Ephemeral Recordings by Broadcasters: The making of ephemeral recordings by a broadcasting organization by means of its own facilities and for use in its own broadcasts; and
  • (k) Judicial or Administrative Proceedings: Any use made of a work for the purpose of any judicial proceedings or for the giving of professional advice by a legal practitioner (e.g., copying arguments or authorities in court pleadings).

The Doctrine of Fair Use (Sec. 185) and the Four-Factor Test

Fair Use is a statutory privilege to use copyrighted material in a reasonable manner without the consent of the copyright owner for criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship, research, and similar purposes (Sec. 185.1). It serves as an exemption to the author's monopoly to avoid stifling the very creativity and learning that copyright law is designed to foster.

Landmark Case: ABS-CBN Corporation v. Gozon (GMA-7 Live Feed Case)

  • Facts: During the live arrival of overseas Filipino worker Angelo dela Cruz at the airport, GMA-7 aired a 5-second live video feed showing ABS-CBN's reporter on the ground, which GMA-7 had received via a Reuters international video subscription without realizing it originated from ABS-CBN's exclusive live broadcast. ABS-CBN filed a criminal complaint for copyright infringement against GMA-7's news executives, who invoked good faith and fair use.
  • Issue: Whether the unauthorized 5-second re-airing of ABS-CBN's live broadcast footage is copyrightable subject matter and whether lack of intent or fair use bars probable cause for infringement.
  • Ruling / Ratio: While "news of the day" as a factual event is not copyrightable under Sec. 175, the actual broadcast footage and audiovisual expression of the news event is protected by copyright. Furthermore, copyright infringement under the IPC is malum prohibitum, meaning good faith or lack of knowledge that the Reuters feed belonged to ABS-CBN is not a defense to direct infringement, while Fair Use is an affirmative defense that must be proven during trial using the Four-Factor Test.

Copyright Infringement (Piracy): Modes, Elements, Remedies, and Defenses

Copyright infringement (colloquially known as piracy) is a trespass on a private domain owned and occupied by the owner of the copyright.

  • Three Modes of Committing Infringement (Sec. 216.1): A person infringes copyright when he:
    1. Directly commits an infringement (Direct Infringer);
    2. Benefits from the infringing activity of another person who commits an infringement if the person benefiting has been given notice of the infringing activity and has the right and ability to control the activities of the other person (Vicarious Infringer / Benefiting from Piracy); or
    3. With knowledge of infringing activity, induces, causes, or materially contributes to the infringing conduct of another (Contributory Infringer).
  • Essential Elements of Piracy: (1) Ownership of a validly copyrighted material by the complainant; and (2) Infringement (unauthorized exercise of an economic or moral right) of the copyright by the respondent.
  • Remedies (Secs. 216–217): Injunction, actual or statutory damages, impounding and destruction of infringing copies and master plates/molds, delivery of profits, and criminal prosecution.
  • Valid Defenses to Copyright Infringement: (1) The work is not copyrightable subject matter (Sec. 175–176); (2) The term of copyright has already expired (work is in the public domain); (3) The act falls under statutory limitations (Sec. 184) or Fair Use (Sec. 185); (4) The plaintiff is not the true owner of the copyright; (5) Non-participation / lack of notice or control over the infringing activity (for vicarious liability); (6) Prescription; or (7) Lack of evidence.

Comprehensive Classroom Applications & Bar Exam Hypotheticals

The Professor concluded the lecture with rapid-fire bar exam hypotheticals testing whether Copyright Infringement exists in each scenario:

  1. Making or distributing fake/cracked Antivirus Computer Programs: YES (Infringement). Computer programs are expressly protected original works under Sec. 172.1(n).
  2. Keeping a personal collection of imported original DVDs at home: NO. Private ownership and private viewing of lawfully purchased authentic copies do not violate any economic right.
  3. Unauthorized public display of original artworks: YES (Infringement). Public display is an exclusive economic right of the copyright owner under Sec. 177.5 (unless ownership of the physical copy was transferred and the display falls under the statutory exception in Sec. 184.1[i] for works not produced by photography or applied art).
  4. Reproducing a single copy of a thesis strictly for personal academic research (not for sale or mass distribution): NO. Covered by the Doctrine of Fair Use (Sec. 185) for non-profit scholarship and research.
  5. Printing copyrighted artworks or logos onto T-shirts and mugs via silkscreen for commercial sale: YES (Infringement). Reproducing an artistic work onto commercial merchandise violates the author's exclusive reproduction and distribution rights.
  6. Jailbreaking a personal iPhone (overriding factory security restrictions on a phone you bought): NO Copyright Infringement (as ruled discussed in class because the user owns the physical device and does not distribute pirated software, though it may breach warranty or other cyber/telecom regulations).
  7. A composer assigns his copyright; 50 years after the composer's death, the assignee changes the tempo and claims to be the original author of the song: YES (Violation of Moral Rights). While the economic copyright expired 50 years after the composer's death (allowing anyone to adapt the song without consent), the Moral Right of Attribution is PERPETUAL (Sec. 198.1)—nobody can ever claim authorship over the deceased composer's work.
  8. Importing authentic books from abroad and reselling them in a local book sale: NO. Protected by the First-Sale / Exhaustion Doctrine (Sec. 177.3).
  9. Unauthorized commercial remixing or sampling of substantial portions of a copyrighted song: YES (Infringement) if a substantial portion is taken without a license, as arrangements and adaptations are exclusive derivative rights under Sec. 177.2.
  10. Uploading song covers on YouTube: Generally NO direct suit against the uploader IF the platform (YouTube) has blanket Content ID / licensing agreements with the music publishers/composers covering user-generated covers (though without a synchronization/mechanical license, unauthorized public communication technically implicates copyright).
  11. Paying ₱70 to burn a compilation CD of 18 MP3 songs at a computer shop: YES (Infringement). Unauthorized commercial reproduction of sound recordings and musical compositions.
  12. Copying legal arguments and jurisprudence in court pleadings: NO. Expressly exempted under Sec. 184.1(k) (use for judicial proceedings) and Sec. 175 (ideas/legal texts).
  13. Rebroadcasting a radio program 21 years after the original broadcast without the radio station's consent: NO. Under Sec. 215.2, the term of protection for broadcasts is only twenty (20) years from the date the broadcast took place; after 20 years, the broadcast protection has lapsed.
  14. A photographer reproduces a photograph he took without the consent of the person (subject) in the photo: NO Copyright Infringement. Under Sec. 178.1 and Sec. 172.1(k), the photographer owns the copyright over the photograph, not the subject. However, the photographer may be held liable for violating the Data Privacy Act (R.A. 10173) if the photo was taken in private and processed without the data subject's consent.
