I. CONSTITUTIONAL FOUNDATIONS AND STATUTORY CONCEPT OF BAIL

Why is bail necessary if the State has already arrested the accused?

Imagine a man charged with homicide. His trial might last years, but the prosecution has not yet proved him guilty beyond reasonable doubt. Should he necessarily spend those years in jail? Bail is the law's compromise: the accused may enjoy provisional liberty, while the court receives security to ensure he returns when required. Bail is not an acquittal, a declaration that the prosecution is weak, or permission to ignore court processes.

The foundation is Article III, Section 13 of the 1987 Constitution, read with the presumption of innocence in Article III, Section 14(2). In general, before conviction, a person is bailable except when charged with an offense punishable by reclusion perpetua and the evidence of guilt is strong. Rule 114, Section 7 also addresses capital offenses and offenses punishable by life imprisonment. Bail is protected even during suspension of the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and excessive bail is prohibited. Do not equate an accusation with a conviction: the former starts the prosecution; the latter results from adjudication.

Rule 114, Section 1 defines bail as the security given for the release of a person in custody of the law, furnished by that person or a bondsman, guaranteeing appearance before any court when required. Three phrases carry the rule: security, custody of the law, and appearance. The court holds security because it cannot simply rely upon the accused's promise; custody matters because bail releases someone from legal restraint; appearance is the performance secured by the undertaking. The accused retains obligations after release.

1. Security

⚖️

Custody of the law versus jurisdiction over the person

Professor's Illustration: Accused A is abroad. His lawyer appears with sufficient cash and offers to post bail without producing A. Accused B is seriously ill in a hospital but, through counsel, discloses his whereabouts and unequivocally submits to the court. The professor contrasts the two situations to show why custody cannot be bypassed by money alone.

Ordinarily custody arises from actual arrest under a warrant or a lawful warrantless arrest, or voluntary surrender to competent authorities. A fugitive who remains beyond the court's reach cannot simply purchase release from custody never acquired. However, constructive custody may exist even without physical presence inside the courtroom. In Paderanga v. Court of Appeals, the accused's hospital confinement, disclosure of his location, and unmistakable submission to the court's jurisdiction were material. These are fact-sensitive requirements, not a standing rule that every bedridden applicant can merely send counsel to pay cash. [See the Supreme Court's discussion of Paderanga in this judicial decision.]

Do not conflate jurisdiction over the person (authority to bind the accused) with custody of the law (restraint from which bail may release him). Arrest or voluntary surrender may establish both, but counsel must still demonstrate custody to support release.

Concept Legal Definition & Scope Key Distinction for Bail
Jurisdiction Over the Person Court's legal power and authority to try, bind, and render judgment over the individual accused. Can be acquired by voluntary appearance or pleadings, but is insufficient alone to grant bail without custodial submission.
Custody of the Law Actual restraint of the person (arrest/surrender) or constructive submission (e.g. bedridden hospital confinement in Paderanga). Indispensable condition precedent for bail; a fugitive cannot purchase provisional liberty from abroad.

Who puts up the security—and who bears the risk?

Section 1 expressly permits the accused or a bondsman to furnish bail. Thus, a spouse, relative, or unrelated person may provide the money, and a corporate surety may undertake the obligation under the rules. The bondsman is sometimes called the accused's "jailer" because the bondsman undertakes to produce the accused whenever the court requires him. This is a legal metaphor: the bondsman does not receive police powers or physically imprison the accused.

Professor's Illustration: A lawyer advances ₱100,000 in bail for a client and has the deposit made in the lawyer’s own name as security for unpaid legal fees. If the accused absconds, the bond may be forfeited. But counsel must not treat client funds or a bail refund as an automatic fee collection device; professional-responsibility rules and the actual deposit arrangement matter.

Excessive bail and the prosecutor's recommended amount

Professor's computation: A crime carries a maximum of six years' imprisonment. The lecture uses an illustrative ₱6,000 per year × 6 years = ₱36,000 prosecutorial recommendation. Understand the arithmetic, but do not recite ₱6,000 per year as a timeless statutory formula. Bail guides, prosecutorial policies, and amounts can be changed. More importantly, a recommendation on the Information is not the judge's independent determination under Rule 114, Section 9.

The constitutional ban on excessive bail requires reasonableness in light of the person and circumstances, not unquestioning adoption of the fiscal’s suggested amount. Recitation: Bail secures appearance without unnecessarily sacrificing provisional liberty.

II. THE RIGHT TO BAIL IN SPECIAL AND NON-JUDICIAL PROCEEDINGS

The presumption-of-innocence explanation fits an ordinary criminal prosecution. But what if the person is detained under military discipline, an extradition request, or immigration law? First ask what proceeding is involved. Applying ordinary Rule 114 automatically to every form of detention gives the wrong answer.

Military courts-martial

Professor's discussion: Soldiers facing service-related court-martial charges do not enjoy the ordinary constitutional bail guarantee in the same manner. The professor connects this distinction with military discipline and security, particularly access to troops or weapons. The lecture identifies Comendador v. De Villa as an assumed reference. Identify the forum and charge; the doctrine does not turn every civilian criminal case against a soldier into a court-martial. The right to speedy disposition remains relevant.

Extradition: surrender to another State

Imagine a foreign government asking the Philippines to surrender a person wanted abroad. An extradition court is not determining guilt; it is deciding whether surrender is authorized by the applicable treaty and law. Therefore, bail in extradition is discretionary, not the same entitlement as ordinary pre-conviction criminal bail.

The current Rules on Extradition Proceedings, A.M. No. 22-03-29-SC, effective November 10, 2025, expressly provide in Rule 3, Section 1 that the extraditee must prove by clear and convincing evidence that (1) the person is not a flight risk, and (2) the person will obey the extradition court's orders and processes. Clear and convincing is stronger than preponderance but less than proof beyond reasonable doubt. An important additional restriction: bail shall not be granted to an extraditee sought to serve a prior sentence or already convicted abroad but not yet sentenced. This qualification is absent from the lecture's simplified twin-requisite account. [Supreme Court E-Library: A.M. No. 22-03-29-SC.]

Professor's Illustration: The lecture mentions Jose Maria Sison in the Netherlands to explain extradition treaties and the political-offense exception. Its historical claims require case- and treaty-specific verification. The principle to retain is that extradition depends on the applicable agreement, law, and charge, not a foreign government’s unilateral demand.

Deportation: removal under immigration law

Deportation concerns an alien's immigration status, not a criminal judgment on the underlying facts. The professor explains that release pending deportation is not constitutional bail as a matter of right. Rather, provisional release depends on immigration authority and the governing law; the discussion cites Harvey v. Defensor-Santiago as a possible authority. Remember the distinction: a criminal trial adjudicates criminal liability; an extradition case evaluates a request for surrender; deportation administers immigration law.

Proceeding Governing bail idea What to remember
Ordinary criminal case Right, discretion, or denial depending on charge and stage Rule 114 + presumption of innocence
Court-martial Ordinary constitutional bail guarantee does not apply in the same manner Military forum and discipline
Extradition Discretionary, subject to the 2025 rules and exclusions No flight risk + obey orders, clear and convincing proof
Deportation Administrative provisional release, not Rule 114 bail as of right Immigration-law discretion

III. FOUR FORMS OF BAIL AND RELEASE ON RECOGNIZANCE

A client asks, "Do I have to deposit the entire ₱100,000?" Your first answer should not be yes or no. Section 1 recognizes four forms: corporate surety, property bond, cash deposit, and recognizance. All pursue appearance, but their financial consequences differ.

Sec. 14: Full amount deposited; refundable upon cancellation less fines/costs
3
Property Bond
Secs. 11–13: Real property encumbrance; mandatory annotation within 10 days

Recognizance
Sec. 1 / RA 10389: Non-monetary release to qualified custodian authorized by law

Corporate surety: pay a premium, not the face amount

Under Rule 114, Section 10, a duly authorized and Supreme Court-accredited corporate surety undertakes the prescribed liability. The accused typically pays a premium, not the entire stated bond. Professor's practical warning: Premiums are generally nonrefundable, and the surety agreement may require renewals or additional payments during a prolonged case. Annual renewal is a matter of the particular contract and administrative requirements—not a clause saying all bonds expire annually under Rule 114. If the accused fails to appear, the company faces liability and may pursue contractual recourse.

Cash deposit: full amount now, possible refund later

Under Section 14, the accused or another person deposits the required cash and files the undertaking. This can be attractive when the depositor has liquidity: unlike a surety premium, the principal may ultimately be returned after cancellation, subject to the rules. Important qualification: Section 14 permits cash to be applied to fines and costs, with any excess returned to the accused or the depositor. Thus "always refunded in full" is too broad.

Professor's practical example: Counsel arranges transport for a detainee, but the payment portal is unavailable. Coordinate the detainee’s presentation, bond documents, and authorized payment procedure. Legal clarification: The official platform is JePS (Judiciary Electronic Payment Solution), not "JudEx"; Supreme Court guidance makes JePS optional for cash bail and bond fees, regardless of amount. Thus mandatory electronic payment and guaranteed manual payment during outages must not be stated as universal requirements. [Supreme Court guidance.]

Property bond: the land secures someone else's appearance

Under Sections 11–13, the accused offers sufficient real property as security, with necessary proof of ownership, value, and encumbrances. After approval, the lien must be properly annotated within ten days in the appropriate land records, or in the applicable tax declaration for unregistered land. The title is not instantly transferred to the State. Rather, the bond creates an encumbrance, and subsequent noncompliance may lead to execution after the prescribed forfeiture procedure.

Professor's Illustration: A relative allows an accused to use family land. The accused later disappears. The risk to the relative is real: a judgment on the bond may expose the property to levy and public auction. This is why a property bond often involves more paperwork and risk than the word "no cash" suggests.

Recognizance: release based on a lawful undertaking

Recognizance is usually the easiest form to misunderstand. It is not simply "free bail" chosen whenever cash is unavailable. It is release without monetary security in cases authorized by law or the Rules, normally with a responsible person or qualified custodian undertaking to ensure appearance. The lecture mentions light offenses, minor ordinance violations, preventive detention, youthful offenders, certain summary-procedure situations, and pending probation applications. Those routes arise under different legal provisions, so their eligibility requirements must not be blended together.

R.A. No. 10389 governs qualified indigent accused. Section 5 speaks of recognizance as a matter of right in specified circumstances, but Section 6 requires a motion, proof of indigency, social-welfare certification, arraignment, and sanggunian notice. Section 8 qualifies the custodian, including restrictions on relatives and required affiliations. Thus a barangay official or lawyer does not automatically qualify merely because the professor gives that example. [R.A. No. 10389.]

The law's arraignment condition for this particular recognizance scheme should not be confused with the general rule that ordinary bail may not be conditioned upon prior arraignment. They are distinct routes with different statutory requirements. Memory aid: Surety = premium; Cash = deposit; Property = lien; Recognizance = qualified undertaking without monetary security.

IV. FIXING BAIL, CONDITIONS, DURATION, AND CUSTODIAL TRANSFERS

How a judge determines a reasonable amount

The judge, not the prosecutor, fixes judicial bail. Section 9 gives ten primary considerations. Instead of memorizing them as disconnected words, group the questions the judge asks:

  1. What is the charge? Nature and circumstances of the offense; penalty; and weight of the evidence.
  2. Who is the accused? Financial ability, character and reputation, and age and health.
  3. Will the accused return? Probability of appearance, other bail previously forfeited, fugitive status when arrested, and other pending cases in which the accused is on bail.

Those groups contain all ten statutory factors. No single factor—wealth, notoriety, or seriousness of the charge—automatically fixes the amount. In the professor's comparison, a theft defendant may ask to reduce an unaffordable ₱16,000 recommendation, while a murder defendant who succeeds in a bail hearing might receive a substantially higher bond. The illustrative ₱500,000–₱1,000,000 range is not a statutory floor for murder and petitioning for bail does not prove wealth.

A motion to reduce bail asks the judge to exercise discretion, commonly after notice to the prosecution. Professor's courtroom observation: A fiscal sometimes writes no objection to a reduction to 50% of the recommended amount. Such concurrence can help but does not compel a 50% reduction or replace the judge's responsibility to determine reasonableness. The court may also increase bail on good cause under Section 20.

What the bond continues to require

Under Section 2, the approved undertaking remains in force, unless cancelled, through the stages specified in the Rule until promulgation of the RTC judgment; this wording matters when a first-level case is appealed. The accused must appear whenever required. Failure to attend trial without justification and despite due notice may constitute waiver of presence for that hearing and permit trial in absentia, subject to its constitutional requisites. The bondsman must surrender the accused for execution of final judgment.

Section 3 provides that a detainee held under legal process shall not be released or transferred except by court order or admission to bail. Professor's Illustration: A warden wants to transfer an accused to another jail and allow an escorted visit to the mother’s wake. Proper judicial authority must be secured; a humanitarian furlough is discretionary, not automatic. Potential liability for unauthorized acts depends on the law and facts, not merely the occurrence of a transfer.

Part 1 understanding check: Why can an accused in hospital sometimes seek bail while one hiding abroad cannot? Why can the same nominal ₱100,000 bail produce different financial risks under cash and corporate surety? The answers turn on custody/submission and deposit versus nonrefundable premium.

V. WHEN BAIL IS NOT REQUIRED VERSUS WHEN BAIL IS NOT ALLOWED

These statements sound alike but describe opposite legal conditions. If bail is not required, the person generally need not secure release with a bond. If bail is not allowed, the person cannot obtain provisional liberty through bail at that stage. First identify whether the accused is in custody, then the offense and procedural stage.

Cases in which bail is not required

Section 16 of Rule 114 begins with the governing principle: no bail is required when the law or the Rules so provide. The lecture illustrates this through offenses punished by fine only, ordinary summary-procedure filings, and offenses with destierro as the relevant penalty. The point is not that all minor criminal cases cease to be criminal. It is that arrest or preventive detention may not be necessary when the applicable rules provide another procedure.

Professor's Illustration: In concubinage, the woman charged as the concubine faces destierro under Article 334 of the Revised Penal Code. That penalty restricts residence within designated limits; it is not ordinary imprisonment. The lecture therefore contrasts a regularly filed case—where arrest may not be warranted solely to secure detention—with a scenario in which the persons are allegedly apprehended while committing the offense. But remember two qualifications: concubinage requires the offended spouse's complaint, and a purported in flagrante delicto arrest must still satisfy the legal elements of Rule 113, Section 5(a). Being discovered in suspicious circumstances alone does not conclusively establish lawful arrest.

A lawfully arrested person may need bail or recognizance for release during inquest. An accused in a summary-procedure case may also face arrest for unjustified nonappearance when the rules authorize it. But neither arrest nor a cash-bond requirement follows automatically from police presentation; verify the legal arrest basis and applicable special rules.

Section 16 contains three distinct preventive-detention thresholds:

  1. Detention equals or exceeds the possible maximum imprisonment for the charge: immediate release, without preventing continuation of the case or appeal.
  2. Maximum penalty is destierro: release after 30 days of preventive imprisonment, according to the Rule.
  3. Detention equals or exceeds the minimum principal penalty (without the Indeterminate Sentence Law or modifying circumstances): reduced bail or own recognizance, at the court's discretion. Under R.A. No. 10389, assess separately whether its special recognizance conditions apply.

The distinction is maximum exhausted → immediate release; minimum reached → reduced bail or recognizance under the governing rule.

Cases in which bail is legally unavailable

Now imagine the opposite: the State has a reason to keep the accused in custody and bail cannot be granted. The lecture emphasizes four situations:

  • Before conviction, a capital offense or one punishable by reclusion perpetua or life imprisonment when evidence of guilt is strong (Section 7).
  • After RTC conviction of an offense punishable by reclusion perpetua or life imprisonment, the ordinary pending-appeal discretion of Section 5 does not apply.
  • After a judgment of conviction becomes final (Section 24).
  • Once the accused has begun serving the sentence, no bail may be allowed (Section 24).

Legal clarification: An RTC conviction of an offense punishable by reclusion perpetua does not mean Rule 114 automatically transfers the accused to New Bilibid Prison during appeal. Detention location follows the proper custody and commitment orders; the relevant point here is ineligibility for ordinary pending-appeal bail.

Section 24’s probation qualification: An application for probation before finality may permit temporary liberty on existing bail or, when appropriate, recognizance. This does not authorize ordinary bail after finality or once sentence service begins.

The 'other lawful cause' limitation

Suppose an accused faces graft and a separate plunder charge. Even if he posts valid bail for graft, that does not release him from lawful detention for plunder. This is the reason court release orders commonly contain the qualification "unless detained for some other lawful cause."

Question Bail not required Bail not allowed

Recitation trap: Never answer "non-bailable" merely because bail is unnecessary. One means no bond needed; the other means no provisional release on bond.

VI. BAIL AS A MATTER OF RIGHT AND PRACTICAL EARLY SURRENDER

Three situations governed by Section 4

When bail is a matter of right, the court is not free to refuse reasonable bail to an eligible accused who complies with the Rules. This differs from a discretionary bail petition, where the court must assess whether release should be allowed at all.

Rule 114, Section 4 covers: (1) before conviction in a first-level court (MeTC, MTCC, MTC, MCTC); (2) after conviction by such a court while the case proceeds on appeal; and (3) before conviction by the RTC, where the offense is not punishable by death, reclusion perpetua, or life imprisonment. Do not group all RTC cases into the same category: a homicide charge punishable by reclusion temporal is different from a murder charge carrying reclusion perpetua.

The professor's pre-warrant strategy

Professor's Illustration: When attempted-homicide charges are filed, counsel promptly accompanies the client to court for voluntary surrender and bail, rather than awaiting physical arrest. This is a practical strategy to avoid unnecessary detention, not a guarantee that an e-warrant will never issue.

Voluntary surrender may create custody of the law, allowing bail, but the judge retains the duty to determine judicial probable cause. Any outstanding warrant requires the proper judicial handling. The lecture’s e-warrant discussion describes a practical possibility, not a guaranteed legal outcome.

Important: Voluntary submission for bail does not automatically establish mitigating voluntary surrender under Article 13(7), Revised Penal Code. The mitigating circumstance independently requires its legal elements, including spontaneity, and is decided at sentencing.

Professor's practical checklist: Prepare identification photographs, proof of residence and any required sketch, signed bond undertaking, fingerprints, and proof of lawful payment. Court-specific forms and charges must be verified with the clerk; the lecture’s ₱500 figure is not a universal statutory amount.

Most importantly, posting bail does not itself waive timely challenges to illegal arrest or missing preliminary investigation. We will return to Section 26 in Part 3.

VII. PETITION FOR BAIL WHEN THE CHARGE CARRIES THE MOST SEVERE PENALTIES

'Non-bailable offense' is often shorthand, not the whole test

Imagine a murder Information. A student says: "Murder is non-bailable, so the judge must automatically deny bail." That is incomplete. Under Section 7, the critical pre-conviction question is not simply the name of the charge. It is whether the evidence of guilt is strong. If the prosecution cannot meet that test, the accused may be admitted to bail notwithstanding the severe charge.

The court must give the prosecutor notice and an opportunity to present evidence at a bail hearing. Under Section 8, the prosecution has the burden of proving the evidence of guilt is strong. This differs from proof beyond reasonable doubt at trial; the bail ruling does not decide guilt.

What happens at the hearing?

Professor's Illustration: An accused charged with murder seeks bail. The prosecution calls an eyewitness, introduces relevant video evidence, or develops circumstantial evidence. Defense counsel cross-examines and may point out contradictions or present proof supporting self-defense. The judge then assesses the record for the specific bail question: Is the prosecution's evidence of guilt strong?

The hearing previews trial evidence, but denial does not guarantee conviction. The trial may present additional evidence and applies a different standard.

Section 8 supplies another easily overlooked consequence: evidence presented at the bail hearing is automatically reproduced at trial, subject to the Rule's provisions permitting the recall of a witness. This makes the hearing procedurally important beyond immediate release.

A bail order must summarize material prosecution evidence and independently explain why it is or is not strong. The source associates this with Cortes v. Catral (unverified reference). A bare formulaic conclusion is inadequate.

Illustration of the judge's legal reasoning

Application Example: If a distant eyewitness cannot identify the accused reliably and video lacks a clear face, the judge may find the prosecution’s proof insufficiently strong and grant bail. Multiple credible, consistent witnesses may justify denial. Neither bail ruling determines guilt beyond reasonable doubt.

Recitation line: For a capital or reclusion perpetua/life-imprisonment charge, the prosecution must show at a properly noticed bail hearing that the evidence of guilt is strong; the judge must evaluate and explain the result.

Remedy against an erroneous bail order

A bail order ordinarily decides provisional liberty, not final criminal liability, and is thus interlocutory. There is generally no ordinary appeal from it. Rule 65 certiorari may lie for grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction, subject to its procedural requirements.

Professor's Illustration: An RTC bail ruling is ordinarily challenged by Rule 65 certiorari in the Court of Appeals; a Sandiganbayan bail ruling may be taken to the Supreme Court. The point is jurisdictional grave abuse, not an ordinary merits appeal. The lecturer references Senators Revilla and Estrada.

VIII. BAIL AFTER RTC CONVICTION: DISCRETION AND THE LEVISTE TEST

Why conviction changes the legal position

A man charged with homicide received bail as a matter of right before RTC conviction. The RTC subsequently finds him guilty. Can he retain liberty simply because the judgment is on appeal? No automatic entitlement follows. Under Section 5, following RTC conviction of an offense not punishable by death, reclusion perpetua, or life imprisonment, bail is now discretionary. Conviction has displaced the pre-conviction presumption of innocence for this purpose; provisional liberty requires the court's careful judgment.

The court may allow continued liberty under the same bond with the bondsman's consent, or impose an appropriate different arrangement. The professor mentions increasing a ₱100,000 bond to ₱200,000 to reflect increased flight risk; doubling is not a mandatory formula. The court must still act reasonably under the Rules.

The five listed bail-negating circumstances

The third paragraph of Section 5 becomes especially important when the RTC imposes imprisonment exceeding six years. After showing by the prosecution and notice to the accused, bail must be denied or cancelled when any listed circumstance or another similar circumstance is established. The five specified circumstances concern:

  1. Recidivism, quasi-recidivism, habitual delinquency, or reiteracion. They reflect prior criminality under legally defined categories; they are not interchangeable labels.
  2. Prior escape, evasion of sentence, or unjustified violation of bail conditions. History matters because bail depends on future attendance.
  3. Commission of the offense while on probation, parole, or conditional pardon. This indicates alleged misconduct while enjoying conditional liberty.
  4. Probability of flight shown by the accused's circumstances or the case.
  5. Undue risk of committing another crime while the appeal is pending.

It is not enough for the prosecutor merely to repeat these phrases. The prosecution must establish the circumstances it invokes, with the accused receiving notice. Also read the words "or other similar circumstances": the five listed categories are not necessarily exhaustive.

Leviste v. Court of Appeals — the central post-conviction case

Facts: Former Batangas Governor Jose Antonio Leviste was charged with murder in a fatal shooting but convicted by the RTC of homicide. He sought bail while appealing. He argued that because none of the Section 5 bail-negating circumstances was proved, bail should follow automatically.

Issue: Whether the absence of a listed bail-negating circumstance converts post-RTC-conviction bail into a matter of right.

Ruling: No. In Leviste v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 189122, March 17, 2010, the Supreme Court described a two-stage analysis. Stage 1: determine whether any enumerated or similar circumstance exists, deciding whether the court operates under more stringent or ordinary sound discretion. If one exists, denial or cancellation follows under the Rule. Stage 2: if none exists, the court still exercises sound judicial discretion on all relevant circumstances; bail does not become automatic. The decision also emphasized that the appeal should raise a substantial, fairly debatable issue rather than be merely pro forma. [Official decision.]

Application: An appellant sentenced to eight years has no prior escape, other cases, or demonstrated flight risk. He nonetheless cannot simply say, "The prosecution proved none of the five; therefore release me." The appellate court examines the entire record and the credibility of the application. Bar trap: Absence of grounds for mandatory denial ≠ affirmative entitlement to bail.

Which court hears the post-conviction request?

Generally, after RTC conviction, the trial court may act even after notice of appeal, provided it has not transmitted the original records. Once the records are transmitted, the appellate court acts. But there is a crucial exception: if conviction changes the nature of the offense from non-bailable to bailable, the application must be resolved by the appellate court, regardless of where the records remain.

Professor's Illustration: Murder was charged, but the RTC convicted only of homicide. The accused moves for bail before the RTC because the records are still there. Wrong court. Since the conviction has changed the offense's nature from non-bailable to bailable for purposes of the Rule, the Court of Appeals must act. This is exactly the procedural feature emphasized in Leviste.

IX. VENUE AND HIERARCHY OF COURTS IN FILING BAIL

Begin with one simple question: where is the case pending?

Suppose the RTC in San Fernando City, La Union issued a warrant for an accused later arrested in Dagupan City, Pangasinan. The client asks whether bail must be paid in La Union or may be posted in Pangasinan. Rule 114, Section 17 answers that question, but the answer changes depending on where arrest occurred, whether the case is already filed, and whether approval requires discretion.

The general venue is the court where the criminal case is pending. For bail in the amount already fixed, Section 17(a) recognizes substitute judges when the judge of that court is absent or unavailable. The professor describes an intra-territorial hierarchy, preferring another RTC judge within the relevant province or city, and then an available first-level judge. The governing text permits a judge within the province, city, or municipality, subject to the conditions in Section 17(a). An unavailable judge does not justify crossing a provincial boundary just to shop for a convenient forum.

A substitute judge accepts the fixed bond, not an application requiring fresh discretion. Courts have disciplined judges for disregarding Section 17's territorial limits. The professor's geographic illustrations are not permission to file in any convenient courthouse. [Section 17, Rule 114.]

Four commonly confused local court names

A MeTC is a Metropolitan Trial Court, operating in metropolitan Manila; an MTCC is a Municipal Trial Court in Cities, such as a first-level court of a city outside Metro Manila; an MTC serves a municipality; and an MCTC serves a circuit of multiple municipalities. All are first-level courts. For Rule 114, the essential comparison is usually RTC versus first-level court, not which abbreviation is longer.

Professor's Illustration: The MTC of Bacnotan judge is unavailable. Counsel seeks a lawful substitute within La Union, not another province merely because the courthouse is open. A substitute’s acceptance of the fixed amount does not authorize a reduction.

Arrest outside the place where the case is pending

Section 17(a) makes a specific exception. When the accused is arrested elsewhere, bail in the amount fixed may also be filed with an RTC at the place of arrest. If no RTC judge is available there, a first-level judge at that place may act. Here, unlike an ordinary choice of substitute judge within the place of the pending case, the place of arrest itself creates the additional venue.

Professor's Illustration: RTC San Fernando fixes estafa bail at ₱40,000. When the accused is arrested in Dagupan, the bond may be filed at a Dagupan RTC, with a first-level Dagupan court as fallback only if no RTC judge there is available. This alternative arises from arrest there, not counsel’s convenience.

Inquest custody before an Information is filed

Section 17(c) speaks differently: a person in custody who is not yet charged in court may apply for bail with any court in the province, city, or municipality where held. This is useful in inquest proceedings, including a legally detained person who has requested preliminary investigation. Because the provision says "any court", it does not itself establish the same RTC-first requirement as the place-of-arrest exception applicable when the case is already pending elsewhere.

The distinction concerns the filing stage, not the seriousness of the charge alone. Ask: Has the Information been filed? If not, apply Section 17(c) to the appropriate custodian location. If filed, consult Section 17(a) and any applicable special provisions.

When the court must exercise discretion

Under Section 17(b), discretionary bail and release on recognizance may be applied for only in the court where the case is pending, whether in preliminary investigation, trial, or appeal. A motion to reduce bail likewise requires judicial consideration by the competent court handling the case; it is not an invitation for the arrest-place judge to revise another court's amount.

Professor's Illustration: RTC San Fernando fixes bail at ₱120,000. The accused is arrested in Baguio. The lawyer may tender the fixed amount before a proper Baguio judge under Section 17(a), but cannot use that judge to obtain a reduction to ₱60,000. The reduction must be sought in the proper court, and a substitute judge is not free to displace the court of origin's exercise of discretion.

X. REQUIRED PRESENCE, FORFEITURE, AND CANCELLATION OF BAIL

Must an accused attend every hearing?

The accused’s right to attend does not automatically make every absence a ground for forfeiture. The professor distinguishes arraignment and plea, identification when required, and promulgation (subject to exceptions) from proceedings where presence may be waived. Proper court orders requiring appearance—including pre-trial directives—still bind the accused.

Professor's Illustration: An eyewitness is about to identify the accused in a physical-injuries case, and the court has required the accused's presence. Absence without justification matters because the accused's identity is directly at issue. Later, a billing clerk testifies about hospital expenses, with no order requiring the accused's personal presence. A properly waived or excused absence at that later hearing should not mechanically produce forfeiture.

Legal clarification: Lavides describes arraignment, required identification, and promulgation as the classic compulsory stages. Pre-trial and other hearings may also involve specific court orders requiring presence under Section 2(b). Therefore, neither "every absence forfeits bail" nor "attendance at all other hearings is always optional" is correct.

The four-stage forfeiture procedure

Forfeiture is not an immediate transfer of money or land to the State whenever an accused misses court. The procedure under Section 21 protects the bondsman from summary execution without the opportunity to produce the accused and explain the absence.

Stage 1 — Required appearance, notice, and declaration of forfeiture. The court or Rules require the accused's presence. The bondsmen are notified to produce the accused on the designated date and time. If the accused fails to appear, the court declares bail forfeited. The court may also issue the appropriate warrant or coercive order concerning the accused. This preliminary forfeiture is sometimes called an order nisi or confiscation of bond. It is not yet a final monetary judgment against the bondsmen.

Stage 2 — Thirty-day opportunity to comply. From the declaration, the bondsmen have 30 days to comply with two separate obligations: (a) produce the accused's person or give an adequate reason for nonproduction; and (b) explain why the accused failed to appear when first required. Both matter. Simply saying "we cannot find him" does not automatically excuse nonappearance, and producing him does not necessarily excuse the original breach without the necessary showing.

Stage 3 — Judgment on the bond. If the bondsmen fail the prescribed requirements, the court renders a separate judgment holding them jointly and severally liable for the amount. Joint and several means the law permits recovery of the full obligation from a liable bondsman, subject to applicable recourse between them. The court generally may not reduce or mitigate liability, unless the accused has been surrendered or is acquitted, as Section 21 expressly provides.

Stage 4 — Execution after an enforceable judgment. After the judgment on the bond becomes final and enforceable, a writ of execution may issue to collect cash or levy and auction bonded property. The professor mentions a 15-day finality period: verify applicable procedural rules and service, rather than treating issuance of the forfeiture order as immediate authority to sell property. The controlling distinction is forfeiture order ≠ judgment on the bond.

Mendoza v. Alarma: why a premature auction was void

Facts: Spouses Fernando and Fausta Alarma posted 11.7 hectares in Iba, Zambales for Joselito Mayo. He failed to appear; the trial court declared forfeiture and gave 30 days to show cause. The court then ordered an auction without rendering judgment on the bond, and purchasers Mendoza and Miclat acquired the property.

Issue: Whether a declaration of bail forfeiture alone supports an execution sale of the property given as bond.

Ruling: No. In Mendoza v. Alarma, G.R. No. 151970, May 7, 2008, the Supreme Court explained that an order of forfeiture is conditional and interlocutory. Because the statutorily required judgment on the bond was missing, the writ of execution and the subsequent auction sale could not stand. This is a due-process rule for bondsmen, not a technicality excusing the accused's unexplained disappearance. [Official decision.]

Memory trigger: "Mendoza = Missing judgment." If a Bar problem gives you an order declaring bail confiscated, a 30-day directive, and then a public auction without a judgment on the bond, identify the fatal missing stage.

Cancellation is different from forfeiture

Section 22 provides two ways bail may be cancelled on application by the bondsmen with notice to the prosecutor: surrender of the accused or proof of death. It also provides automatic cancellation upon acquittal, dismissal of the case, or execution of the judgment of conviction. However, cancellation is without prejudice to liability on the bond already incurred.

For example, if the case is dismissed after a bondsman previously breached the undertaking, do not assume all past liabilities vanish retroactively. The cancellation ends the continuing undertaking as the Rule directs; it does not magically erase a liability validly incurred before cancellation.

Remember the order: Forfeiture declares a breach; judgment on the bond fixes liability; execution collects it; cancellation ends the undertaking.

XI. BAIL DOES NOT WAIVE OBJECTIONS TO ARREST OR PRELIMINARY INVESTIGATION

The apparent dilemma before Rule 114, Section 26

A client seeks bail while challenging a warrantless arrest. Must counsel choose between obtaining liberty and preserving that challenge? Section 26 avoids that dilemma: a bail application need not equal waiver, provided the accused objects in time.

Section 26 expressly resolves that problem: application for or admission to bail does not bar the accused from questioning the legality of the arrest or arrest warrant, or the regularity or absence of preliminary investigation, provided these objections are raised before entering a plea. The court should resolve them as early as practicable, not later than the start of trial. The time requirement matters as much as the protection.

The old broad proposition—"posting bail automatically waives an illegal-arrest objection"—is therefore not the rule under Section 26. But the Section is not a universal permission to defer procedural objections indefinitely. Counsel must raise the appropriate objection in time; the bond does not itself operate as a substitute for a motion.

Lavides: the court may not force arraignment as a bail condition

Professor's Illustration: A shoplifting accused is eligible for bail, but the judge refuses approval until arraignment, hoping to prevent disappearance. This is impermissible: it pressures a plea before the accused can raise pre-plea objections, including a motion to quash.

In Lavides v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 129670, the Supreme Court explained why making arraignment a condition of granting bail undermines protected procedural rights. The court can secure appearance at arraignment through the conditions of bail itself. It need not use detention as leverage to accelerate plea. [Decision.]

Be careful: the special statutory recognizance procedure under R.A. No. 10389 has its own arraignment condition, as discussed in Part 1. Do not flatten that distinct statute into a universal rule about the timing of ordinary bail.

Borlongan v. Peña: waiver requires more than paying the bond

Facts: Accused in an estafa case posted bail while expressly reserving objections to their arrest warrants, and pursued pre-arraignment relief. The opposing party argued that merely posting bail waived those objections.

Issue: Whether posting bail by itself waives objections to arrest or defects in the preliminary investigation.

Ruling: No. In Borlongan v. Peña, G.R. No. 143591, May 5, 2010, the Supreme Court applied Section 26 and rejected the automatic-waiver theory. A waiver may arise from voluntarily pleading and participating in trial without timely invoking the objection; it is not presumed merely from the bail deposit. [Decision.]

Final application drills

Problem A. A murder accused applies for bail and the prosecutor offers no evidence after proper opportunity. Answer: The court cannot deny bail solely on the label "murder"; the prosecution must establish strong evidence of guilt under Sections 7–8.

Problem B. An RTC homicide appellant sentenced to eight years proves no prior escape or pending case and insists release is automatic. Answer: No. Under Leviste, absence of bail-negating circumstances leaves sound judicial discretion, not bail as a matter of right.

Problem C. A property bondsman receives a forfeiture order, but the court immediately auctions the property without a separate judgment on the bond. Answer: The process violates Section 21; Mendoza requires the missing judgment before execution.

Problem D. An accused posts bail but files a challenge to illegal arrest before entering his plea. Answer: Section 26 preserves the objection; bail alone is not waiver.
