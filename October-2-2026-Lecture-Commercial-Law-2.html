I. FUNDAMENTALS & SCOPE OF MARINE INSURANCE

A. Concept and Classifications of Marine Insurance

Under Section 101 of R.A. 10607 (The Amended Insurance Code), marine insurance is broadly classified as transportation insurance covering loss or damage to vessels, craft, aircraft, vehicles, goods, freights, cargoes, merchandise, and all other kinds of property and interests therein, in connection with any and all risks or perils of navigation, transit, or transportation.

  • Two Principal Divisions:
    1. Inland Marine Insurance: Covers primarily the land or over-the-land transportation perils of property shipped by railroads, motor trucks, airplanes, and other means of inland transit, as well as bridges, tunnels, and piers.
    2. Ocean Marine Insurance: Covers property and interests exposed to maritime perils over the high seas or navigable waters, encompassing the hull (vessel), cargo, freightage (expected earnings/rent of the vessel), and liability to third persons.

B. Risks Insured Against: Perils of the Sea vs. Perils of the Ship

In a standard ocean marine insurance contract, the insurer undertakes to indemnify the insured only against perils of the sea and not perils of the ship, unless the parties expressly stipulate an "All-Risks" policy.

Basis of Comparison Perils of the Sea Perils of the Ship
Nature of Risk Extraordinary, fortuitous, and unusual casualties and violence of the elements (wind, waves, tempest, lightning, rocks, piracy) connected with navigation. Ordinary, natural, and inevitable action of the sea, or losses resulting from the ordinary wear and tear of the vessel or its inherent vice/unseaworthiness.
Proximate Cause External, accidental, and unexpected maritime hazards that could not be guarded against by the ordinary exertion of human skill and prudence. Internal defects of the hull, defective equipment, improper stowage, or negligence of the master and crew without an intervening fortuitous maritime event.
Standard Coverage Covered as a general rule under an ordinary marine insurance policy. Not covered under an ordinary marine policy; the shipowner or insured bears the loss.
Effect of "All-Risks" Policy Covered automatically unless expressly excluded by the terms of the policy. Covered by exception, provided the loss is fortuitous and not caused by the insured's willful misconduct or fraud.
  • Rule on "All-Risks" Marine Policies: By way of exception, when a marine policy is issued on an "all-risks" basis, it covers all losses during the voyage whether arising from a peril of the sea or a peril of the ship, except when the loss is caused by the willful destruction, fraud, or intentional misconduct of the insured. The burden of proof shifts to the insurer to establish that the loss falls cleanly within an express exclusion in the policy.

C. Vessel Covered by Marine Insurance vs. Fire Insurance

Because a vessel may be damaged by fire, determining whether the governing law is Marine Insurance or Fire Insurance depends on the operational status of the craft at the time of the contract:

  • When Governed by Fire Insurance: If the subject matter of the policy is an unfinished vessel still under construction in a shipyard, drydock, or port, has never been afloat for a voyage, or consists merely of raw materials in a shipyard intended for constructing vessels, the contract is strictly a fire insurance policy.
  • When Governed by Marine Insurance: If the policy covers a vessel engaged in navigation (or afloat and ready for maritime service), it is governed by the rules of ocean marine insurance, even if the policy insures the vessel against fire risk only.

II. CONCEALMENT AND REPRESENTATION IN MARINE INSURANCE

A. Concept and Rationale of Marine Concealment

Under Section 26, concealment is the neglect to communicate that which a party knows and ought to communicate. While this definition applies to both ordinary and marine insurance, Section 109 imposes a much stricter standard of uberrimae fidei (utmost good faith) in marine insurance: each party is bound to communicate all the information which he possesses, material to the risk, except such as is mentioned in Section 30, and to state the exact and whole truth in relation to all matters that he represents, or upon inquiry discloses or assumes to disclose.

  • Rationale for Unique Marine Concealment Rules: Unlike ordinary property or life insurance where the insurer can readily inspect the building or subject the applicant to a medical examination, a vessel or cargo may be in a foreign port or on the high seas. Moreover, marine insurance involves massive aggregate valuations (hull, multi-party cargoes, expected freightage, and charter hire) and multiple intersecting commercial parties.

B. Disclosure of Information, Belief, or Expectation of Third Persons

Under Section 35 governing ordinary insurance, neither party is bound to communicate information of his own judgment or opinion upon the matters in question, even if inquired into. However, Section 110 establishes a critical exception in marine insurance: In marine insurance, information of the belief or expectation of a third person, in reference to a material fact, is material and must be communicated.

  • Rationale for Requiring Disclosure of Third-Party Beliefs: Persons operating within the maritime port environment—such as harbor pilots, stevedores, shipwrights, and officers or seamen of neighboring vessels—are not ordinary laypersons. They are maritime experts within the same industry whose observations and expectations regarding a vessel's condition carry technical weight and materially affect the insurer's estimation of the risk.

Professor's Illustration
While Vessel X is docked at the port being loaded with cargo, seamen and officers from an adjacent vessel observe the loading process and remark that Vessel X is listing heavily to one side ("lumaladlad o bumabanking ang barko") because the cargo is not properly stowed (not properly laden). Even though these observers are third persons unconnected to the shipowner, crew, or cargo owner of Vessel X, their belief or expectation that the vessel is improperly laden is a material fact formed by industry experts. Under Section 110, the insured must disclose this third-party observation to the marine insurer.

C. When Concealment Does Not Vitiate the Entire Contract (Qualified Concealment)

In ordinary insurance contracts, any material concealment entitles the injured party to rescind the entire contract, even if the insured dies or suffers a loss from a cause completely unrelated to the concealed fact. In contrast, Section 112 provides a specialized rule for marine insurance where concealment regarding five specific matters does not vitiate the entire contract, but merely exonerates the insurer from a loss resulting from the risk concealed:

  1. The national character of the insured;
  2. The liability of the thing insured to capture and detention;
  3. The liability to seizure from breach of foreign laws of trade;
  4. The want of necessary documents; and
  5. The use of false and simulated papers.
  • Operative Rule of Causation under Section 112: If the insured conceals any of the five enumerated matters above, the marine insurance contract remains valid and subsisting. The insurer is exonerated from liability only if there is a causal connection ("nagtama ang rason at ang fact concealed") between the concealed matter and the actual cause of the loss. If the vessel is lost due to an independent peril of the sea or a different risk, the insurer remains fully liable.

Professor's Illustration
A shipowner applies for marine insurance and conceals the fact that the vessel carries high-value contraband that could attract pirates along a pirate-infested route (concealing liability to capture and detention). However, during the voyage, no pirates attack the ship; instead, the vessel suffers a loss because port authorities detain it for lacking required customs clearances (want of necessary documents), or it sinks in a typhoon. Because the concealed fact (piracy/capture risk) did not match the actual cause of the loss, the contract is not vitiated under Section 112, and the insurer cannot avoid liability on the ground of the piracy concealment.

E. Representation in Marine Insurance

Under Sections 113 to 115, representations in marine insurance are governed by whether the falsity is intentional or unintentional:

  • Intentional Falsity (Sec. 113): If a representation by the insured is intentionally false in any material respect, or in respect of any fact on which the character and nature of the risk depends, the insurer may rescind the entire contract.
  • Unintentional Falsity / Eventual Falsity (Sec. 114): The eventual falsity of a representation as to expectation does not, in the absence of fraud, avoid a contract of marine insurance. However, if an unintentional representation of a material fact is false, the insurer may rescind the contract from the time the representation becomes false (Sec. 45 in relation to Sec. 113).

III. IMPLIED WARRANTIES IN MARINE INSURANCE: SEAWORTHINESS AND DEVIATION

A. Concept and Enumeration of Implied Warranties

In ordinary insurance contracts, all warranties (whether affirmative or promissory) must be expressly contained in the policy itself or in another instrument signed by the insured and referred to in the policy as making a part of it (Sec. 70). In Marine Insurance, however, the law reads Implied Warranties into every contract by operation of law. Even if the policy is completely silent and the parties did not expressly stipulate them, these warranties are conclusively presumed to exist:

  1. Implied Warranty of Seaworthiness (Sec. 115);
  2. Implied Warranty Against Improper Deviation (Secs. 123–128);
  3. Implied Warranty of Neutrality / National Character—that the ship will carry the requisite documents to show its nationality or neutrality and will not carry documents casting reasonable suspicion thereon (Sec. 122);
  4. Implied Warranty of Legality of the Venture—that the adventure insured is lawful and does not violate domestic law; and
  5. Implied Warranty of Insurable Interest—that the insured possesses an insurable interest in the vessel, cargo, or freightage.

B. The Implied Warranty of Seaworthiness

Under Section 115, in every marine insurance upon a ship or freight, or freightage, or upon anything which is the subject of marine insurance, a warranty is implied that the ship is seaworthy.

  • Definition and Test of Seaworthiness (Sec. 116): A ship is seaworthy when it is reasonably fit to perform the service and to encounter the ordinary perils of the voyage contemplated by the parties to the policy.
  • Relativity of Seaworthiness: Seaworthiness is not an absolute or fixed engineering standard; it is a relative term that depends entirely upon the nature of the ship, the specific voyage to be undertaken, the season, the service to be performed, and the nature of the goods carried. A vessel that is perfectly seaworthy for inland river navigation or a calm coastal passage may be completely unseaworthy for a trans-Pacific ocean voyage.
  • Comprehensive Scope of Seaworthiness (Sec. 119): A warranty of seaworthiness extends not only to the condition of the structure of the ship itself, but requires that:
    1. It be properly laden (cargo must be properly stowed, balanced, and secured so the vessel will not list or capsize, and not overloaded beyond its Plimsoll mark);
    2. It be provided with a competent master;
    3. It be manned by a sufficient number of competent officers and seamen; and
    4. It be furnished with the necessary appurtenances and equipment (such as anchors, cables, navigation instruments, radar, fuel, medical supplies, and stores), as well as being seaworthy in reference to the specific kind of cargo taken on board (Sec. 121).

C. When Seaworthiness Must Exist: General Rule vs. Statutory Exceptions

Under Section 117, the General Rule is that an implied warranty of seaworthiness is complied with if the ship be seaworthy at the time of the commencement of the risk. Prior unseaworthiness (cured before sailing) or subsequent unseaworthiness arising during the voyage is not a breach of the warranty, nor is it material, provided the insured does not unreasonably delay repairing defects when the ship becomes unseaworthy during the voyage (Sec. 120).

However, Section 117 enumerates Three Statutory Exceptions where seaworthiness must be determined at multiple points rather than once:

Type of Policy Statutory Rule on When Seaworthiness Must Exist (Secs. 117–118) Professor's Analytical Observation
1. Time Policy When the insurance is made for a specified length of time (e.g., 1 year), the implied warranty is not complied with unless the ship be seaworthy at the commencement of every voyage it undertakes during that time (Sec. 117[a]). In truth, these three "exceptions" are merely strict applications of the general rule to multi-part operations: every time a new voyage, a new vessel transfer, or a new voyage segment commences, the risk recommences, requiring seaworthiness at each starting point.
2. Cargo / Transshipment Policy When the insurance is upon cargo which, by the terms of the policy, description of the voyage, or established custom of the trade, is to be transshipped at an intermediate port, the warranty is not complied with unless each vessel upon which the cargo is shipped or transshipped be seaworthy at the commencement of each particular voyage (Sec. 117[b]). Applies when a balikbayan box or commercial cargo is transferred across multiple vessels at stopover ports (e.g., New York to Guam, Guam to Singapore, Singapore to Manila); each receiving vessel must be seaworthy when it departs.
3. Voyage in Stages Policy Where different portions or segments of the voyage contemplated by a policy differ in respect to the things requisite to make the ship seaworthy therefor (e.g., river navigation followed by open ocean navigation), the warranty is not complied with unless, at the commencement of each portion, the ship is seaworthy with reference to that portion (Sec. 118). Every time the vessel completes one segment and prepares to commence the next segment of a staged voyage, its fitness for that specific upcoming segment must be verified prior to departure.

D. Implied Warranty of Seaworthiness of Cargo Owners

Under Section 115 and settled Supreme Court jurisprudence (Roque v. Intermediate Appellate Court, G.R. No. L-66935; Delsan Transport Lines, Inc. v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 127897), the implied warranty of seaworthiness attaches even when the policy is procured by the cargo owner rather than the shipowner.

  • Doctrine: A cargo owner cannot excuse the unseaworthiness of the carrying vessel by claiming he neither owns nor controls the ship. While the shipper has no physical control over the management of the vessel, he has complete control in the choice of the common carrier that will transport his goods. It is the legal obligation of the cargo owner to look for a reliable common carrier that keeps its vessels in seaworthy condition. If the vessel is unseaworthy, the cargo insurer is discharged from liability, and the cargo owner's recourse is to sue the common carrier for breach of contract of carriage, not the marine insurer.

E. The Implied Warranty Against Improper Deviation

Under Sections 123 to 125, in every marine insurance contract, the vessel must sail along the course of sailing fixed by mercantile usage (or the most natural, direct, and advantageous route if none is fixed by usage).

  • Four Forms of Deviation (Sec. 125): Deviation is defined as:
    1. A departure from the course of the voyage insured;
    2. An unreasonable delay in pursuing the voyage;
    3. The commencement of an entirely different voyage (e.g., sailing from Point A to Point C instead of the agreed route from Point A to Point B); or
    4. In the case of a time policy, any departure from the limits of navigation specified.
  • Four Instances of Proper Deviation (Sec. 126): A deviation does not vitiate the policy and is deemed proper only in four specific instances:
    1. When caused by circumstances over which neither the master nor the owner of the ship has any control (e.g., violent storms, strong ocean currents, icebergs, or heavy maritime traffic);
    2. When necessary to comply with a warranty, or to avoid a peril, whether or not the peril is insured against;
    3. When made in good faith, and upon reasonable grounds of belief in its necessity to avoid a peril; or
    4. When made in good faith, for the purpose of saving human life or relieving another vessel in distress.
  • Strict Rule on Improper Deviation (Secs. 127–128): Under Section 127, every deviation not specified in Section 126 is improper. Under Section 128, an improper deviation exonerates the insurer from any loss happening to the thing insured subsequent to the improper deviation, whether or not the deviation increased the risk or caused the loss.

Professor's Illustration

  1. The Captain's Dream (Bar Exam Problem): During a voyage, the ship captain dreams of his deceased grandfather (also a former seaman), who warns him in the dream to change the ship's route to avoid an impending disaster. Upon waking, the captain alters the vessel's course based solely on this dream, and the ship hits a reef and sinks along the new route. Application: The deviation is improper, and the insurer is exonerated from liability. Under Section 126(c), good faith alone is insufficient; the deviation must rest upon reasonable grounds of belief based on objective maritime standards, radar, or weather advisories, not superstition or dreams.
  2. Medical Emergency at Sea: During an ocean voyage, the vessel's Third Officer suffers an acute, debilitating wisdom tooth infection after a week without sleep despite taking all onboard antibiotics, as the ship has no licensed doctor. The captain deviates for two days toward the nearest port in Malaysia for emergency medical relief. Application: Under Section 126(d), deviating in good faith to preserve the health or life of a crew member in severe medical distress qualifies as a proper deviation; hence, the marine policy remains in full force.

IV. LOSS, ABANDONMENT, AVERAGES, AND CO-INSURANCE IN MARINE POLICIES

A. Classification of Losses in Marine Insurance

Under Sections 130 and 131, a loss in marine insurance may be either total or partial (any loss that is not total is partial). Under Section 132, a total loss may be either:

  1. Actual Total Loss; or
  2. Constructive Total Loss (also known as Technical Total Loss or Quasi-Total Loss).
  • Exam Terminology Note: When answering bar or classroom problems on marine losses, always use the complete statutory three-word phrases: "Actual Total Loss" and "Constructive Total Loss" (never merely "actual loss" or "constructive loss").

B. Actual Total Loss vs. Constructive Total Loss

Under Section 133, an Actual Total Loss occurs when the subject matter is completely destroyed or rendered valueless to the owner for the purpose for which he held it, caused by:

  1. A total destruction of the thing insured;
  2. The irretrievable loss of the thing by sinking, or by being broken up (e.g., sinking into an unrecoverable ocean trench);
  3. Any damage to the thing which renders it valueless to the owner for the purpose for which he held it; or
  4. Any other event which effectively deprives the owner of the possession, at the port of destination, of the thing insured (including insurmountable legal obstacles or permanent seizure).

Under Section 134 in relation to Section 141, a Constructive Total Loss (CTL) is one which gives to a person insured a right to abandon under Section 141 because, although the property is not completely destroyed, the commercial cost of saving or repairing it makes a total loss inevitable or economically impractical. Under Section 141 (The Three-Fourths [3/4] Rule), a person insured by a contract of marine insurance may abandon the thing insured and claim for a Constructive Total Loss when more than three-fourths (3/4) thereof in value is actually lost, or would have to be expended to recover it from the peril, specifically:

  1. If more than three-fourths (3/4) in value is actually lost or would have to be expended to recover it;
  2. If it is injured to such an extent as to reduce its value more than three-fourths (3/4);
  3. If the thing insured is the ship, and the contemplated voyage cannot be lawfully performed without incurring either an expense to the insured of more than three-fourths (3/4) the value of the thing abandoned or a risk which a prudent man would not take under the circumstances; or
  4. If the thing insured is cargo or freightage, and the voyage cannot be performed, nor another ship procured by the master (expenses of transshipment), within a reasonable time and with reasonable diligence, to forward the cargo, without incurring the like expense of more than three-fourths (3/4) of the value of the cargo or risk.

C. The Doctrine of Abandonment

Under Section 140, Abandonment is the act of the insured by which, after a constructive total loss, he declares the relinquishment to the insurer of his interest in the thing insured.

  • Exclusive Applicability to Constructive Total Loss: The right of abandonment can only be exercised in the case of a Constructive Total Loss. In an Actual Total Loss, abandonment is neither necessary nor possible (Sec. 137) because there is nothing left to abandon, and the insured simply claims the full indemnity outright. Conversely, in the absence of a Constructive Total Loss (i.e., when the loss is less than 3/4), the insured has no right to abandon.
  • Two Options of the Insured in a Constructive Total Loss: When a Constructive Total Loss occurs, the insured is not compelled to abandon; he has two distinct options:
    1. Option 1 (Abandon and Claim Total Loss): Exercise the right of abandonment by relinquishing all rights and title over the remaining salvage of the vessel or cargo to the insurer, and recover the entire insured value as for an actual total loss; or
    2. Option 2 (Retain Ownership and Claim Partial/Actual Loss): Decline to abandon, retain ownership and possession of the damaged vessel or cargo, and recover from the insurer only the actual amount of the partial loss sustained.
  • Statutory Requisites for a Valid Abandonment (Secs. 142–147):
    1. There must be an actual Constructive Total Loss exceeding the 3/4 threshold (Sec. 141);
    2. The abandonment must be neither partial nor conditional—it must be absolute and unconditional (Sec. 142);
    3. It must be made within a reasonable time after receipt of reliable information of the loss (Sec. 143);
    4. It must be factual—if the information upon which it is made proves incorrect, or the thing insured was restored before the abandonment was made, the abandonment is ineffectual (Sec. 144);
    5. It must be made by giving explicit notice thereof to the insurer, which may be done orally or in writing; however, if done orally, a written notice must be submitted within seven (7) days from such oral notice (Sec. 145); and
    6. The notice must specify the true particular cause of the loss showing a constructive total loss (Secs. 146–147).
  • Legal Effects of a Valid Abandonment (Secs. 148–149):
    1. Transfer of Interest (Sec. 148): It is equivalent to a transfer by the insured of his entire interest in the thing insured to the insurer, with all the chances of recovery and indemnity (subrogation/salvage rights).
    2. Transfer of Agency (Sec. 149): Upon a valid abandonment, the acts of the insured's agents done in good faith subsequent to the loss are at the risk of the insurer, and for its benefit.
  • Effect of Insurer's Refusal to Accept a Valid Abandonment (Sec. 151): Acceptance by the insurer is not necessary to validate an abandonment that complies with all statutory requisites. If the insurer refuses to accept a valid abandonment, the insurer is nonetheless liable as upon an actual total loss, deducting from the amount any proceeds of the thing insured which may have come to the hands of the insured.

D. Measure of Indemnity and Marine Averages

  • Measure of Indemnity (Secs. 158–166):
    • Valued Policy (Sec. 158): A valuation in the policy is conclusive between the parties in adjusting either a partial or a total loss, if the insured has some interest at risk and there is no fraud.
    • Open Policy (Sec. 163): When the policy does not fix the value, the value is estimated at the commencement of the risk (e.g., value of the ship at the outset of the voyage, prime cost of the cargo at the port of loading plus expenses of lading and cost of insurance, and gross freightage exclusive of primage).
  • Concept and Classification of Averages (Sec. 138; Arts. 806–818, Code of Commerce):
    An Average refers to any extraordinary or accidental expense incurred during the voyage for the preservation of the vessel, cargo, or both, and all damages or deterioration suffered by the vessel from departure to arrival, or by the cargo from loading to unloading. Ordinary operating projections (such as routine fuel/bunker oil, crew wages, and standard port dues) are not averages.

Professor's Illustration

  1. General Average (Jettison / "The Boat is Sinking"): During a violent storm at sea, a cargo vessel begins listing dangerously and is about to sink under its heavy load. To save the ship, the crew's lives, and the remaining cargo, the captain deliberately jettisons (throws overboard) Cargo A belonging to Shipper X. Because this deliberate sacrifice saved the vessel and the cargoes of Shippers Y and Z, it inured to the common benefit of all parties and constitutes a General Average. The shipowner, Shipper Y, and Shipper Z (and their respective marine insurers) must contribute proportionately to indemnify Shipper X for the sacrificed Cargo A.
  2. Particular Average: During a calm voyage, crew members get into a brawl on deck and accidentally knock Cargo B overboard, or seawater leaks into a single hold and spoils only Cargo B. Because this loss was not a deliberate sacrifice and did not inure to the common benefit of the vessel and other cargo owners, it is a Particular Average borne solely by the owner of Cargo B (or his insurer, subject to any fault of the carrier).

E. Automatic Co-Insurance in Marine Insurance

Under Section 159, a marine insurer is liable upon a partial loss, only for such proportion of the amount insured by him as the loss bears to the value of the whole interest of the insured in the property insured.

  • Operative Rule on Marine Co-Insurance: In marine insurance, when the property is insured for less than its full value (under-insurance) and suffers a partial loss, the insured is automatically considered a co-insurer for the uninsured difference, even without an express co-insurance stipulation in the policy:
    Insurer's Liability = (Amount of Insurance ÷ Total Value of Property) × Amount of Partial Loss
    (Note: In Fire Insurance, co-insurance is never presumed and applies only if expressly stipulated via a co-insurance clause; in Marine Insurance, co-insurance applies by operation of law under Section 159 in all partial losses).

V. FIRE INSURANCE AND ALLIED RISKS

A. Concept, Scope, and Direct Indemnity Principle

Under Section 169, Fire Insurance includes insurance against loss by fire, and—when covered by extension or riders to fire insurance policies or by separate policies—loss by lightning, windstorm, tornado, or earthquake and other allied risks.

  • Contract of Indemnity for Direct Loss Only: Standard fire insurance is strictly a contract of indemnity covering actual and direct physical loss or damage to the insured property caused by fire. Unless covered by specific allied riders, a standard fire policy does not cover indirect or consequential economic losses (such as the insured's inability to report for work or loss of business profits).
  • Allied Consequential Coverages (By Express Rider or Separate Policy):
    1. Business Interruption Insurance: Indemnifies the insured for loss of earnings (net profits that would have been realized had the business continued) and fixed operating expenses that continue despite the temporary shutdown of the business due to fire.
    2. Rent Insurance: Protects the property owner/lessor or lessee against the loss of rental income or rental value when the premises are rendered untenantable by fire.
    3. Extra Expense Insurance: Covers extraordinary expenses incurred by the insured (such as urgently leasing temporary commercial space and equipment) to maintain continuous operations and avoid an interruption of service following a fire.

B. Hostile Fire vs. Friendly Fire

To recover under a fire insurance policy, the proximate cause of the loss must be a Hostile Fire, not a Friendly Fire:

Basis of Comparison Friendly Fire Hostile Fire
Definition & Location A fire that burns in a place where it was intended to burn and ought to be, remaining completely confined within its designated receptacle (e.g., a flame inside a fireplace, chimney, furnace, gas stove, or a lit candle wick). A fire that becomes uncontrollable or escapes from the place where it was intended to burn and ought to be (e.g., a candle flame that ignites a curtain and spreads to the house).
Liability of Insurer Insurer is NOT liable. Damage caused merely by smoke, soot, or excessive heat from a fire that never escaped its proper receptacle is not recoverable. Insurer IS liable. Once the fire escapes its intended confinement, the insurer is liable for all direct fire, smoke, water, and firefighting damages.

Professor's Illustration

  1. Confined LPG Stove vs. Escaping Flame: If a flame remains confined inside a fireplace or kitchen gas stove and merely emits heavy smoke that discolors the walls, or if a mechanical rupture causes an explosion without the fire escaping to burn the house, it remains a Friendly Fire (or an unindemnified explosion loss unless covered by an explosion rider), and the fire insurer is not liable. However, if a lit candle is placed too close to a window curtain, and the flame jumps from the wick onto the curtain and burns the room, the friendly fire has transformed into a Hostile Fire, making the insurer fully liable.
  2. Wildfires: While a forest wildfire has no specific man-made receptacle where it was "intended to burn," if an uncontrolled wildfire spreads onto the insured's property and burns the insured structure, it operates as a Hostile Fire (or is alternatively covered under natural calamity/fortuitous event extensions).

C. Alteration of the Insured Property

While an insured generally retains the right to use and modify his property, Sections 170 to 172 govern when an Alteration—the introduction of a change in the use or condition of the thing insured—entitles the insurer to rescind a fire insurance contract:

  • Five Requisites for Alteration to Rescind a Fire Policy (Sec. 170):
    1. The use or condition of the thing insured is altered;
    2. The use or condition is limited by the policy (either expressly or impliedly);
    3. The alteration is made without the consent of the insurer;
    4. The alteration is made by means within the control of the insured; and
    5. The alteration increases the risk (or, under Sec. 172, even if it does not increase the risk, the act expressly violates the provisions of the policy).
  • Alterations That Do NOT Avoid the Policy (Secs. 171–172):
    1. An alteration in the use or condition of the thing insured which does not increase the risk, unless it directly violates an express policy provision (Sec. 171);
    2. When the questioned articles kept on the premises are necessarily required by the nature of the insured business covered by the policy, or where the policy would be rendered useless if such acts were prohibited; or
    3. When the increase of risk is caused purely by accident or fortuitous circumstances beyond the insured's control.
  • Alteration by the Insured's Employees or Tenants: Because a lessor/employer has legal control over his premises and lessees, an alteration that increases the risk committed by the insured's staff or tenant is deemed within the control of the insured and avoids the policy.

Professor's Illustration
A warehouse owner procures a fire insurance policy declaring that the warehouse stores only non-hazardous goods such as snacks (chichirya) and frozen foods. During the "ber" months leading up to New Year's Eve, the owner (or his tenant) uses the warehouse to store fireworks (paputok) without the insurer's consent, or blocks the mandatory fire exits. Because storing fireworks alters the use and condition of the property within the insured's control and drastically increases the risk of fire, the insurer is entitled to rescind the policy under Section 170.

D. Special Clauses in Fire Insurance

  • Option to Rebuild Clause (Sec. 174): In open policies, the parties may stipulate that instead of paying the pecuniary amount of the loss in cash, the insurer reserves the option to rebuild, reinstate, or replace the damaged or destroyed property at the insurer's expense within a reasonable time.
  • Fallen Building Clause: A standard stipulation in fire insurance policies providing that if the insured building, or any material part thereof, falls except as the result of fire (e.g., collapses due to structural defect, landslide, or earthquake), all insurance by the policy on such building or its contents shall immediately cease. Consequently, if a fire breaks out after the building has already fallen from a non-fire cause, the insurer is not liable because the fire was not the proximate cause of the collapse and the policy had already terminated upon the fall.

VI. CASUALTY AND THIRD-PARTY LIABILITY INSURANCE

A. Concept, Scope, and General Divisions

Under Section 176, Casualty Insurance is insurance covering loss or liability arising from accident or mishap, excluding certain types of loss which by law or custom are considered as falling exclusively within the scope of other types of insurance such as fire or marine.

  • Common Examples of Casualty Insurance: Personal accident and health insurance; public liability insurance; employer's liability and workmen's compensation insurance; burglary, robbery, and theft insurance; Compulsory Third Party Liability (CTPL) motor vehicle insurance; and plate glass insurance (insuring expensive structural glass panels of commercial buildings against accidental breakage or strong winds).
  • Two General Divisions of Casualty Insurance:
    1. Accident or Health Insurance: Covers injuries, disability, loss of limb, medical expenses, or death suffered by the insured himself due to accidental means.
    2. Third-Party Liability (TPL) Insurance: A contract of indemnity for the benefit of the insured and those in privity with him (or those to whom the law on grounds of public policy extends indemnity) against liability to third persons for bodily injury or property damage arising from an accident for which the insured may be held legally responsible.

B. Insurable Interest and Nature of Liability in Liability Insurance

  • Insurable Interest in Liability Insurance: Unlike life insurance (interest in one's own life or specified relationships) or property insurance (interest in the preservation of one's own property), the insurable interest of the insured in Third-Party Liability insurance lies in the safety of other persons and their property who may maintain an action against him in case of their accidental injury or destruction.
  • Contractual vs. Tortious Liability (No Solidary Liability): In a vehicular or premise accident, the liability of the offending insured to the injured third party is based on quasi-delict (torts) or crime, which is unlimited (covering actual, moral, and exemplary damages). In contrast, the liability of the insurer is based strictly on the insurance contract and is limited to the face value/coverage limit of the policy. Therefore, the insurer is never solidarily liable with the insured for the entire tort judgment beyond the policy limits.

C. Direct Action by Injured Third Party Against the Insurer

When an innocent third party is injured or suffers property damage due to the fault of an insured driver or property owner, whether the injured third party can sue the insurer directly depends on the nature of the liability policy (Guingona v. Del Monte, G.R. No. L-21806; Shafer v. Judge, RTC of Olongapo, G.R. No. 78848):

Type of Liability Policy When Insurer's Obligation Attaches Right of Injured Third Party to Sue Insurer Directly
1. Indemnity Against Third-Party Liability Liability attaches the moment the insured becomes legally liable to the third person upon the occurrence of the accident/injury, even before the insured pays anything. YES. The injured third party may directly sue the insurer (or implead the insurer together with the insured) to recover up to the policy limit.
2. Indemnity Against Actual Loss or Payment Liability attaches only after the insured has actually paid the third person's claim following a final court judgment. NO. The injured third party has no direct cause of action against the insurer; he must first sue the insured, obtain a final judgment, and execute against the insured, who then seeks reimbursement from the insurer.
  • The "No Action" Clause: A stipulation in a liability insurance policy providing that no action shall lie against the insurer unless suit and final judgment be first obtained against the insured, and only thereafter can the injured person recover on the policy. (Note: Jurisprudence holds that a "No Action" clause cannot prevail over Rules of Court provisions on third-party complaints or over policies that are expressly contracts of indemnity against liability).

VII. LIFE INSURANCE: CLASSIFICATIONS, ASSIGNMENT, AND CAUSES OF DEATH

A. Concept, Parties, and Distinction from Accident Insurance

Under Sections 181 and 182, Life Insurance is insurance on human lives and insurance appertaining thereto or connected therewith, including every contract or pledge for the payment of endowments or annuities. It may be made payable on the death of the person, or on his surviving a specified period, or contingently on the continuance or cessation of life.

  • Parties to a Life Policy: A person may procure insurance on his own life and designate any beneficiary (subject to Article 2012 in relation to Article 739 of the Civil Code), or a person may procure insurance on the life of another person in whom he has an insurable interest under Section 10 (himself, spouse and children, person on whom he depends for education/support or in whom he has a pecuniary interest, debtor, or person under a legal obligation to him) and designate himself or another as beneficiary.
  • Life Insurance vs. Accident Insurance:
    1. Traditional Life Insurance: Provides a monetary fund for the benefit of the estate, heirs, or designated beneficiaries of the insured strictly after the insured's death (or upon surviving an endowment period).
    2. Accident Insurance (or Accidental Death & Dismemberment Rider): Protects the insured during his lifetime against loss of time, disability, loss of limb or sight, and medical expenses resulting from accidental bodily injury, and pays an additional indemnity if death results from an accident.

B. Classifications of Life Insurance Policies

Type of Life Policy Key Statutory & Operational Features
1. Ordinary / Whole Life Policy Traditional life insurance without an investment component; the insured pays premiums throughout his life, and the face value is payable only upon the death of the insured.
2. Limited Payment Life Policy Premiums are payable only for a specified limited period (e.g., 5, 10, or 20 years). In strict limited-term variants described in the lecture, coverage or payment is tied to the specified period; in standard whole-life limited-pay policies, once the payment period expires, the policy is fully paid up for life.
3. Endowment Policy The insured pays premiums for a specified period (e.g., 10 or 20 years). If the insured dies within the period, the beneficiaries receive the face value; if the insured outlives/survives the period, the face value is paid directly to the insured himself while alive.
4. Term Insurance Policy Provides temporary life coverage for a fixed period or term (often via a single lump-sum premium or fixed-term payments, frequently marketed to retirees or high-net-worth individuals). If the insured dies within the term, the beneficiaries recover; if he outlives the term, no one recovers unless the policy includes a return-of-premium or interest feature.
5. Variable Life Policy (VUL) Combines life insurance protection with an investment component (Secs. 238–240), where benefits or cash values vary according to the investment performance of separate equity/bond accounts managed by the insurer.
6. Industrial Life Policy Under Section 235, that form of life insurance under which the premiums are payable weekly, or where the premiums are payable monthly or oftener, but not less than weekly, if the face amount of insurance is Php 2,000 or less (or the statutory cap) and the words "Industrial Policy" are printed upon the policy as part of the descriptive matter.

C. Transfer and Assignment of Life Insurance Policies

Unlike property insurance—where a transfer of the property without a corresponding transfer of the policy consented to by the insurer suspends the insurance—Sections 184 and 185 establish a liberal rule for transferring life policies:

  • No Insurable Interest Required in the Transferee (Sec. 184): A policy of insurance upon life or health may pass by transfer, will, or succession to any person, whether he has an insurable interest or not, and such person may recover upon it whatever the insured might have recovered.
  • Notice to Insurer Not Required for Validity (Sec. 185): Notice to an insurer of a transfer or bequest thereof is not necessary to preserve the validity of a policy of insurance upon life or health, unless thereby expressly required by the policy. (Professor's Practical Note: While valid in law without notice or insurable interest in the assignee, in actual practice insurers strictly scrutinize informal assignments to prevent wagering contracts, so transfers should always be officially endorsed).

D. Liability of the Insurer in Specific Causes of Death

  1. Death at the Hands of the Law (Legal Execution):
    • If the insured dies by legal execution (e.g., lethal injection following a final criminal conviction), the beneficiaries can recover on the life policy, as death at the hands of the law is not an excepted risk under the Insurance Code and is one of the risks assumed by the insurer.
    • However, if the beneficiary is the one executed or commits a felony against the insured, the beneficiary (or his heirs claiming through his unlawful act) cannot profit from his own crime.
  2. Suicide by the Insured (Sec. 183):
    The insurer in a life insurance contract is liable in case of suicide only in the following three instances:
    • After Two (2) Years: When the suicide is committed after the policy has been in force for a period of two (2) years from the date of its issue or of its last reinstatement;
    • Shortened Period by Stipulation: When the suicide is committed after a shorter period (e.g., 1 year) stipulated in the policy, because Section 183 allows the policy to provide a shorter waiting period favorable to the insured; or
    • State of Insanity (Regardless of Time): If the suicide is committed in a state of insanity, the insurer is always liable regardless of the date of commission (even if committed within 24 hours of policy issuance, as the 2-year waiting period does not apply to insane suicide).
  3. Willful Killing of the Insured by the Beneficiary (Sec. 12):
    • General Rule (Forfeiture): The interest of a beneficiary in a life insurance policy shall be forfeited when the beneficiary is the principal, accomplice, or accessory in willfully bringing about the death of the insured.
    • Three Exceptions (When the Beneficiary Can Still Recover Despite Causing Death): Because the law requires that the killing be "willful" and felonious, the beneficiary does not forfeit his share and can still recover if the death of the insured was caused by:
      1. Accidental killing (lack of intent/willfulness);
      2. Self-defense (justifying circumstance where the insured unlawfully provoked/attacked the beneficiary); or
      3. Insanity of the beneficiary at the time of the killing (exempting circumstance negating willful intent).
    • Order of Distribution of Forfeited Proceeds (Sec. 12, as amended by R.A. 10607): When a beneficiary is disqualified under Section 12, the forfeited share passes strictly in the following statutory order:
      1. To the other beneficiaries, unless they are also disqualified;
      2. In the absence of other qualified beneficiaries, in accordance with the policy contract; or
      3. If the policy contract is silent, to the estate of the insured.

VIII. SURETYSHIP AND MICROINSURANCE

A. Contract of Suretyship

Under Section 177, a Contract of Suretyship is an agreement whereby a party called the surety guarantees the performance by another party called the principal or obligor of an obligation or undertaking in favor of a third party called the obligee.

  • When Suretyship Constitutes Insurance Business (Sec. 2[a]): A contract of suretyship is deemed an insurance contract only if executed by the surety as a vocation, and not merely incidental to any other legitimate business or activity of the surety.
  • Nature of the Surety's Liability (Sec. 178): The liability of the surety or sureties shall be joint and several (solidary) with the obligor, shall be limited to the amount of the bond, and is determined strictly by the terms of the contract of suretyship in relation to the principal contract between the obligor and the obligee.
  • Classifications of Surety Bonds:
    1. Contract Bonds: Guarantee the faithful performance of construction or procurement contracts (Performance Bond) or guarantee that the contractor will pay laborers and materialmen (Payment Bond).
    2. Fidelity Bonds (Industrial or Public Official Bonds): Guarantee employers or the government against financial loss caused by the dishonesty, defalcation, or failure to properly account for funds by accountable employees or public officers handling public collections.
    3. Judicial Bonds: Bonds required in judicial proceedings, such as Bail Bonds in criminal cases (as an alternative to cash bail, property bond, or recognizance) or attachment, injunction, and replevin bonds in civil actions.
    4. Continuing Suretyship: An agreement whereby the principal places itself in a position to enter into a projected series of future credit transactions with its creditor without executing a separate surety contract or bond for each individual financing or credit accommodation extended to the principal debtor.

B. Microinsurance

Under Section 187, Microinsurance is a financial product or service that meets the risk protection needs of the poor and low-income sector where:

  1. The amount of contributions, premiums, fees, or charges, computed on a daily basis, does not exceed seven and a half percent (7.5%) of the current daily minimum wage rate for nonagricultural workers in Metro Manila; and
  2. The maximum sum of guaranteed benefits is not more than one thousand (1,000) times of the current daily minimum wage rate for nonagricultural workers in Metro Manila.

IX. COMPULSORY MOTOR VEHICLE LIABILITY INSURANCE (CMVLI)

A. Concept, Purpose, and Scope of Coverage

Under Sections 386 to 402, Compulsory Motor Vehicle Liability Insurance (CMVLI) is a mandatory species of liability insurance required by the Land Transportation Office (LTO) prior to the registration or renewal of registration of any motor vehicle. It provides protection coverage that answers for legal liability for losses and damages for bodily injuries or death (and property damage by extension) that may be sustained by another arising from the use and operation of a motor vehicle.

  • Statutory Purpose: To provide immediate financial assistance and compensation for the death or bodily injuries suffered by innocent third parties or passengers in motor vehicle accidents, without waiting for protracted litigation.
  • Who Must Procure and Pay for CMVLI:
    1. Private Motor Vehicle Owners: Must procure comprehensive coverage against third-party liability for death or bodily injuries (and if used to transport passengers for compensation, must also include passenger liability).
    2. Land Transportation Operators (Public Utility Vehicles): Must procure coverage against both passenger and third-party liability. The law strictly mandates that the operator—not the driver—must pay the CMVLI premiums.
  • Amended Schedule of Indemnity Limits (IC Circular Letter / LTO Issuance): Under the updated schedule of indemnities, the maximum limit of third-party liability for all CMVLI coverage is Php 200,000.00 per motor vehicle (with death indemnity including burial and funeral expenses set at Php 200,000.00, specific scheduled amounts for fractures, hospital room, surgical fees, and permanent disablement, plus up to Php 10,000.00 for unscheduled pertinent and reasonable incidental expenses, not exceeding the aggregate Php 200,000.00 cap).

B. The "No-Fault" Indemnity Clause

Under Section 391 (as updated by Insurance Commission circulars), any claim for death or bodily injury to any passenger or third party shall be paid without the necessity of proving fault or negligence of any kind, subject to the following rules:

  1. Maximum "No-Fault" Amount: The total no-fault indemnity in respect of any one person shall be Php 30,000.00 for all motor vehicles.
  2. Strictly Limited to Death or Bodily Injury: The No-Fault Indemnity Clause applies only to bodily injury or death; it never applies to property damage.
  3. Required Proofs: A police report of the accident and either a death certificate (with proof of relationship) or a medical report and medical/hospital receipts.
  4. Rule on Which Insurer to Claim From (Sec. 391[c]):
    • If the Victim is a Passenger / Occupant: The claim shall lie against the insurer of the vehicle in which the claimant was riding, mounting, or dismounting from (even if that vehicle was completely faultless and was rear-ended by another vehicle).
    • If the Victim is a Third Party / Pedestrian (Not an Occupant): The claim shall lie against the insurer of the directly offending vehicle.
    • Right of Reimbursement: In all cases, the insurer that pays the no-fault claim has the statutory right to recover against the owner of the vehicle actually responsible for the accident. Furthermore, receiving the Php 30,000.00 no-fault indemnity does not bar the victim from suing the offending party in a criminal or civil action to recover the excess damages (e.g., claiming the remaining Php 270,000.00 out of a Php 300,000.00 court judgment).

C. Special Clauses in Motor Vehicle Insurance

  1. Cooperation Clause: Requires the insured to give the insurer all information, cooperation, and assistance necessary to defend claims (including attending court trials and hearings); willful breach thereof exonerates the insurer.
  2. Authorized Driver Clause: Indemnifies the insured owner against loss or damage to the vehicle only when driven by:
    • The insured himself; or
    • Any person driving on the insured's order or with his permission (Authorized Driver), provided such driver is duly licensed to drive the motor vehicle and is not disqualified by law or violated any restriction on his license.
  3. Theft Clause (and Its Interaction with the Authorized Driver Clause): Includes theft or unlawful taking of the vehicle as an insured peril. Under Supreme Court jurisprudence (Villacorta v. Insurance Commission, G.R. No. L-54171; Palermo v. Pyramid Insurance, G.R. No. L-36480), the interaction between the Authorized Driver Clause and the Theft Clause follows strict rules:

Professor's Illustration
Car Owner A lends his insured car to his friend B, who figures in an accident.

  • Scenario 1 (Valid License): If B has a valid, unexpired driver's license and drives with A's permission, B is an authorized driver and A can recover from the insurer.
  • Scenario 2 (No License, Expired License, or Violated Restriction): If B drives with A's permission, but B has no driver's license, has an expired license, or drives at night in violation of a "Cannot Drive at Night" restriction on his license, B is NOT an authorized driver, and the insurer will validly deny A's claim.
  • Scenario 3 (Unauthorized Taking Invoking the Theft Clause): If B takes and drives A's car without A's knowledge or permission (or a repair shop employee takes it for a joyride) and crashes it, the insurer cannot deny A's claim under the Authorized Driver Clause; instead, A can recover under the Theft Clause, even if the unauthorized taker had no license or an expired license!

D. Change of Ownership in CMVLI

Under Section 395, a transfer of ownership of a motor vehicle does not suspend the CMVLI policy, provided three requisites concur:

  1. The insurance company agrees to continue such policy;
  2. The change of ownership (or change of engine) is indicated in an endorsement by the insurer; and
  3. A signed duplicate of the endorsement is filed with the LTO.

(If a vehicle is sold via an "open deed of sale" without transferring the policy and notifying the insurer and LTO, the policy is suspended because the seller no longer has insurable interest at the time of loss, and the buyer is not yet the insured under the policy).

X. CLAIMS SETTLEMENT, PRESCRIPTION, AND THE INSURANCE COMMISSION

A. Claims vs. Claim Settlement and Statutory Payment Timeframes

  • Claim vs. Claim Settlement: A Claim is the initial formal demand filed by the insured or beneficiary with the insurer for the satisfaction of a loss covered by the policy. Claim Settlement refers to the legal process and remedies for the indemnification of the insured's loss when the insurer commits an unreasonable delay or unjust refusal/denial to pay a valid claim.
  • Unfair Claim Settlement Practices (Sec. 247): An insurer may face administrative sanctions (suspension or revocation of license) for knowingly misrepresenting policy provisions, failing to acknowledge communications promptly, failing to adopt standards for prompt investigation, refusing to pay claims without conducting a reasonable investigation, or compelling insureds to litigate by offering substantially less than the amounts ultimately recovered.
  • Statutory Periods for Payment of Claims (Secs. 248–249):
Type of Insurance Policy Mandatory Statutory Period for Payment Penalty for Unreasonable Delay / Refusal (Secs. 248–250)
1. Life Insurance Maturing Upon Expiration of Term (Endowment) Immediately upon maturity of the policy (Sec. 248). Unless the delay is based on the ground that the claim is fraudulent, the insurer is liable to pay:
1. The amount of the claim;
2. Interest at twice the ceiling prescribed by the Monetary Board from the date following the time prescribed until fully satisfied;
3. Attorney's fees and other litigation expenses (Sec. 250).
2. Life Insurance Maturing at Death of Insured Within sixty (60) days after presentation of the claim and filing of proof of death of the insured (Sec. 248). Same penalties apply (double interest, attorney's fees, and litigation expenses).
3. Non-Life Insurance (Property, Marine, Fire, Casualty) Within thirty (30) days after receipt by the insurer of proof of loss, and ascertainment of the loss or damage by agreement or arbitration; if ascertainment is not had within sixty (60) days after receipt of proof of loss, then within ninety (90) days after such receipt (Sec. 249). Same penalties apply (double interest, attorney's fees, and litigation expenses).
  • Criminal Liability for Fraudulent Claims (Sec. 251): Any person who presents or causes to be presented any fraudulent claim for the payment of a loss under an insurance contract, or fraudulently prepares, makes, or subscribes any writing with intent to use the same in support of any such claim, shall be punished by a fine not exceeding twice the amount claimed, or imprisonment of two (2) years, or both, at the discretion of the court.

B. Prescriptive Periods in Insurance Actions

  1. General Insurance Policies (Sec. 63):
    • With Valid Stipulation: The parties may stipulate a prescriptive period in the policy, provided it is not less than one (1) year from the time the cause of action accrues. In insurance law, the cause of action accrues not upon the occurrence of the loss, but upon the final rejection/denial of the claim by the insurer. Any stipulation limiting the time to commence an action to less than one (1) year from accrual is void.
    • Without Stipulation (or if Stipulation is Void): If the policy contains no prescriptive stipulation, or if the stipulation is void for being less than one year, the action prescribes in ten (10) years from rejection of the claim, as an action upon a written contract under Article 1144 of the Civil Code.
  2. Special Prescriptive Periods in CMVLI (Sec. 397):
    • Notice of Claim: Must be filed in writing with the insurer within six (6) months from the date of the accident; otherwise, the claim is deemed waived.
    • Court / IC Action: If the claim is denied or contested, the action must be brought within one (1) year from the date of the denial of the claim with the Insurance Commissioner or the proper court.

C. Powers and Jurisdiction of the Insurance Commission

Under Sections 437 to 439, the Insurance Commission (established in 1949 and headed by the Insurance Commissioner) exercises two distinct sets of powers to regulate the insurance industry and protect the insuring public:

  1. Administrative and Regulatory Powers (Sec. 437):
    • Enforcing the provisions of the Insurance Code;
    • Issuing, suspending, or revoking Certificates of Authority for entities engaging in the insurance business;
    • Examining the books, records, and financial condition of insurance companies at any time; and
    • Exercising quasi-legislative / rule-making authority to issue circulars, rules, and regulations (which may carry administrative penalties).
  2. Adjudicatory (Quasi-Judicial) Jurisdiction (Sec. 439 & Sec. 398):
    • Exclusive Jurisdiction over CMVLI Claims: Under Section 398, the Insurance Commissioner has exclusive original jurisdiction to adjudicate claims and complaints under Compulsory Motor Vehicle Liability Insurance (CMVLI) within the statutory coverage limits.
    • Concurrent Original Jurisdiction up to Php 5,000,000.00: Under Section 439, the Insurance Commissioner has concurrent original jurisdiction with the Regional Trial Courts (RTC) to adjudicate any claim or complaint involving any loss, damage, or liability where the amount of the loss, damage, or liability being claimed or sued upon does not exceed Five Million Pesos (Php 5,000,000.00) for any single claim, exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney's fees.
      • Doctrine on Non-Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies: Because jurisdiction for claims of Php 5,000,000.00 and below is concurrent, a claimant (for instance, in Visayas or Mindanao far from the Insurance Commission's main office) has the absolute option to file a Php 4,000,000.00 insurance claim directly with the RTC, and the RTC cannot dismiss the complaint on the ground of failure to exhaust administrative remedies.
    • Exclusive Original Jurisdiction of the RTC Above Php 5,000,000.00: If the amount of the single insurance claim exceeds Five Million Pesos (Php 5,000,000.00), exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney's fees, the Regional Trial Court (RTC) has exclusive original jurisdiction, and the Insurance Commissioner has no authority to adjudicate it.
