I. GENERAL PRINCIPLES & ESSENTIAL REQUISITES OF DISINHERITANCE

Nature and Scope of Disinheritance

Under Article 915 of the Civil Code, disinheritance is the testamentary act by which a testator, for a just and legal cause expressly stated in a valid will, deprives a compulsory heir of his or her legitime. Because compulsory heirs succeed to their legitime by operation of law regardless of the testator's wishes, the testator cannot arbitrarily strip them of this reserved portion; deprivation is permissible only when the compulsory heir commits a grave statutory offense against the testator.

Conversely, voluntary or testamentary heirs (such as friends, strangers, or collateral relatives) cannot be the subject of disinheritance. Because voluntary heirs inherit solely out of the testator's liberality from the free portion of the estate, a testator who wishes to exclude them simply omits their names from the will or revokes an existing testamentary institution.

Essential Requisites of a Valid Disinheritance

Pursuant to Articles 915, 916, and 917 of the Civil Code, a valid and effective disinheritance requires the concurrence of seven strict requisites:

  1. It must be made in a valid will: Disinheritance is strictly a testamentary disposition. It cannot be effected through an inter vivos public instrument, an affidavit, or an oral declaration. Furthermore, the will itself must comply with all extrinsic formalities required by law (under Articles 804 to 814) and must be duly admitted to probate.
  2. It must be made expressly, identifying the disinherited heir: The testator must unequivocally manifest the intent to disinherit a specifically identified compulsory heir. Tacit omission does not constitute disinheritance; rather, the unintentional or unexplained omission of a compulsory heir in the direct line results in preterition under Article 854.
  3. It must be based on a cause expressly specified by law: The grounds enumerated in Articles 919, 920, and 921 of the Civil Code are exclusive (numerus clausus). A testator cannot invent personal grounds based on whim, caprice, or petty family grievances.
  4. The legal cause must be expressly stated in the will itself: It is insufficient for a legal ground to exist in fact; the testator must explicitly articulate the specific statutory cause within the text of the will so the probate or settlement court can evaluate its legal sufficiency.
  5. The cause must be certain and true (existing at the time of execution): The offense or ground relied upon must already exist at the time the testator executes the will. It cannot be predicated on a future, speculative, or conditional offense that has not yet occurred.
  6. It must be unconditional: Because disinheritance is a punitive sanction for an offense already committed against the testator, the deprivation of the legitime cannot be made to depend upon the fulfillment of a suspensive or resolutory condition.
  7. It must be total and not partial: The legitime cannot be partially forfeited. Because statutory grounds for disinheritance constitute grave offenses of ingratitude against the testator, the law does not permit the testator to quantify the degree of offense and deduct a mere fraction (such as one-third or one-half) of the heir's legitime. The testator must either forgive the offense entirely or disinherit the compulsory heir completely.

Conditional Disinheritance vs. Conditional Revocation of Disinheritance

While the act of disinheritance itself cannot be subjected to a condition, Philippine succession law distinguishes between a conditional disinheritance (which is void) and a conditional revocation or lifting of a disinheritance (which is valid). When a testator disinherits a compulsory heir based on an existing, actual statutory ground, the disinheritance is immediately valid and unconditional; if the testator subsequently attaches a condition allowing the heir to regain his or her successional rights upon reforming or making amends, the condition attaches not to the disinheritance per se, but to the revocation of the disinheritance (or the testator's conditional pardon).

Application Example: Unapproved Marriage as a Future Ground

Suppose a father states in his will: "I hereby disinherit my daughter if she marries a man below our family's socioeconomic bracket." This disinheritance is void on two independent grounds. First, marrying a person against the parents' socioeconomic preferences is not among the exclusive grounds enumerated in Article 919 of the Civil Code. Second, the ground is a future, contingent event rather than an existing cause at the time of the execution of the will, violating the rule that disinheritance must be based on an actual, completed offense.

Professor's Illustration: Conditional Lifting of Disinheritance

A testator provides in his will: "I hereby disinherit my daughter because she is currently living a dishonorable and disgraceful life. If, however, she mends her ways, leaves that lifestyle, returns to my house, and serves me well, this disinheritance shall be revoked and lifted." This testamentary provision is valid. The disinheritance itself is anchored on an existing, statutory ground under Article 919(7). The condition attaches not to the imposition of the disinheritance, but to its revocation, effectively operating as a valid conditional pardon by the testator.

Burden of Proof in Contested Disinheritance

Under Article 917 of the Civil Code, if the disinherited compulsory heir denies or contradicts the truth of the cause stated in the will, the burden of proof (onus probandi) rests upon the other heirs of the testator who seek to sustain the disinheritance. The disinherited heir is not required to prove a negative or establish his or her innocence. Because the other heirs stand to benefit financially from the forfeiture of the disinherited heir's legitime, they must affirmatively establish by preponderance of evidence that the statutory ground alleged by the testator actually occurred.

1. Attempt Against the Life of the Testator, Spouse, Descendants, or Ascendants

Under Article 919(1), a child or descendant may be disinherited when he or she has been found guilty of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her spouse, descendants, or ascendants.

  • Requisites:
    1. The compulsory heir committed an intentional felony against the life of the testator, the testator's spouse, the testator's descendant (e.g., a sibling or child of the heir), or the testator's ascendant (e.g., the testator's parent).
    2. The felony reached at least the attempted stage under Article 6 of the Revised Penal Code (consummated or frustrated stages are necessarily included).
    3. The heir was prosecuted and found guilty by final judgment in a criminal proceeding.
  • Effect of Executive Clemency: If the convicted child or descendant is subsequently granted an absolute presidential pardon, the testator may still validly disinherit him or her. An executive pardon blots out the criminal liability and penal consequences against the State, but it cannot erase the personal ingratitude and moral offense already committed against the testator.
  • Effect of Non-Prosecution: If the child attempts to kill the testator, but the family refuses to file a criminal case to avoid public scandal, the child cannot be disinherited under Article 919(1) because the statutory element of being "found guilty" is absent. However, the testator may validly disinherit the child under Article 919(6) for maltreatment of the testator by deed, which does not require a prior criminal conviction.

2. Groundless Accusation of a Crime Punishable by Six Years or More

Under Article 919(2), disinheritance is warranted when a child or descendant has accused the testator of a crime for which the law prescribes imprisonment for six years or more (prision correccional in its maximum period to reclusion perpetua), if the accusation has been found groundless.

  • Scope of "Accusation": The term encompasses filing a criminal complaint, acting as a private complainant, or voluntarily testifying as a prosecution witness against the testator.
  • Distinction Between Acquittal on Reasonable Doubt and a "Groundless" Accusation: The mere acquittal of the testator does not automatically render the accusation "groundless." If the criminal court acquits the testator merely on the ground that the prosecution failed to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt (insufficiency of evidence) or due to a procedural technicality, Article 919(2) is not satisfied. To justify disinheritance, the judgment of acquittal must positively declare or unmistakably demonstrate that the accusation was false, malicious, or fabricated—meaning the crime was not committed at all, or the child knew the testator was innocent.

3. Conviction of Adultery or Concubinage with the Testator's Spouse

Under Article 919(3), a child or descendant may be disinherited when he or she has been convicted of adultery or concubinage with the spouse of the testator.

  • Requirement of Final Conviction: Just as in the first ground, a criminal prosecution and final judgment of conviction for adultery (Article 333, Revised Penal Code) or concubinage (Article 334, Revised Penal Code) are indispensable.
  • Application to Step-Parents: This ground does not necessarily involve biological incest. It frequently applies where the testator's first marriage was dissolved by death or declaration of nullity, the testator contracted a subsequent marriage, and the testator's child from the prior marriage engaged in an illicit sexual relationship with the stepmother or stepfather resulting in a criminal conviction.

4. Fraud, Violence, Intimidation, or Undue Influence in Making or Changing a Will

Under Article 919(4), a child or descendant may be disinherited when, by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence, he or she causes the testator to make a will or to change one already made. This ground penalizes any coercive or deceitful interference with the testator's testamentary freedom, even if the coerced will was subsequently revoked and replaced by a new, freely executed will containing the disinheritance.

5. Refusal Without Justifiable Cause to Support the Testator

Under Article 919(5), a child or descendant may be disinherited for refusal without justifiable cause to support the parent or ascendant who disinherits such child or descendant.

  • Requisites:
    1. The testator-ascendant was in actual need of support under the Family Code.
    2. A formal or extrajudicial demand for support was made upon the child or descendant.
    3. The child or descendant had the financial means and capacity to provide support (otherwise, the refusal has a justifiable cause).
    4. The child or descendant unjustifiably refused to provide the requested support.

6. Maltreatment of the Testator by Word or Deed

Under Article 919(6), maltreatment of the testator by word or by deed by the child or descendant is a sufficient cause for disinheritance.

  • Absence of Conviction Requirement: Unlike Article 919(1), maltreatment requires no criminal conviction.
  • Scope of Verbal and Physical Maltreatment: Maltreatment "by deed" covers any physical violence, assault, or unprosecuted attempt against the life of the parent. Maltreatment "by word" encompasses grave oral defamation, vicious cursing, public humiliation, or broadcasting vile insults and derogatory attacks against the parent on social media platforms.

7. Leading a Dishonorable or Disgraceful Life

Under Article 919(7), a child or descendant may be disinherited when he or she leads a dishonorable or disgraceful life. The phrase "leads a life" implies continuity, habituality, and permanence of conduct that brings shame and moral disrepute to the family name, rather than an isolated indiscretion. Furthermore, this ground is not limited to sexual immorality; it includes habitual criminality, chronic drug trafficking, or notorious public scandal.

8. Conviction of a Crime Carrying the Penalty of Civil Interdiction

Under Article 919(8), disinheritance is authorized upon the child's or descendant's conviction of a crime which carries with it the penalty of civil interdiction.

  • Crimes Carrying Civil Interdiction: Under Articles 34 and 41 of the Revised Penal Code, civil interdiction is an accessory penalty that attaches by operation of law to the principal penalties of death (when not executed), reclusion perpetua, and reclusion temporal.
  • Offense Need Not Be Committed Against the Testator: Unlike Article 919(1), where the attempt on life must be directed against the testator or specific close family members, the crime under Article 919(8) may be committed against a total stranger. Because crimes punishable by reclusion temporal or reclusion perpetua (such as murder, robbery with homicide, or plunder) are exceptionally heinous, final conviction thereof severs the moral bond between parent and child and taints the family honor, empowering the testator to disinherit the offender.

III. SPECIFIC GROUNDS FOR DISINHERITANCE OF PARENTS AND ASCENDANTS (ARTICLE 920)

When the testator has no surviving children or descendants, the parents and ascendants become compulsory heirs. Article 920 of the Civil Code enumerates eight exclusive grounds by which a descendant-testator may disinherit his or her parents or ascendants.

1. Abandonment of Children vs. Inducement to Immorality or Attempt Against Virtue

Under Article 920(1), parents or ascendants may be disinherited when they have abandoned their children or induced their daughters to live a corrupt or immoral life, or attempted against their virtue. This provision contains three distinct acts with different gender applications:

🏚️
Abandonment of Children
Gender-Neutral: Applies to both male and female descendants (total deprivation of care/support/guidance)
⚠️
Inducement to Immorality
Female-Specific: Applies strictly to daughters and granddaughters
🛡️
Attempt Against Virtue
Female-Specific: Sexual offenses against a daughter/granddaughter (no prior conviction required)
  • Abandonment of Children (Gender-Neutral): The first clause—"when the parents have abandoned their children"—applies equally to both male and female descendants (sons, daughters, grandsons, or granddaughters). Abandonment signifies the total deprivation of basic parental care, financial support, and moral guidance during the child's minority, rather than mere temporary physical separation.
  • Inducement to Live a Corrupt or Immoral Life (Female-Specific): The second clause—"induced their daughters to live a corrupt or immoral life" (e.g., forcing a daughter into prostitution)—applies strictly to daughters and granddaughters (female descendants).
  • Attempt Against Their Virtue (Female-Specific): The third clause—"attempted against their virtue"—likewise applies to female descendants and encompasses sexual offenses committed by an ascendant against a daughter or granddaughter, such as attempted rape, acts of lasciviousness, or sexual abuse, even without a prior criminal conviction.

2. Overlapping Grounds Between Descendants (Art. 919) and Ascendants (Art. 920)

Several grounds under Article 920 mirror the provisions of Article 919, operating reciprocally when the descendant is the testator and the ascendant is the compulsory heir:

  • Article 920(2): Conviction of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her spouse, descendants, or ascendants (mirrors Art. 919[1]).
  • Article 920(3): Groundless accusation of a crime punishable by imprisonment for six years or more (mirrors Art. 919[2]).
  • Article 920(4): Conviction of adultery or concubinage with the spouse of the testator (mirrors Art. 919[3]).
  • Article 920(5): Causing the testator by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence to make a will or change one already made (mirrors Art. 919[4]).
  • Article 920(7): Refusal without justifiable cause to support the children or descendants (mirrors Art. 919[5]).

3. Loss of Parental Authority (Article 920[6])

Under Article 920(6), a parent or ascendant may be disinherited for the loss of parental authority for causes specified in the Code (now governed by the Family Code, such as judicial deprivation of parental authority due to excessive harshness, corrupting orders, or conviction of a crime carrying civil interdiction). To constitute a ground for disinheritance, the loss of parental authority must be attributable to the fault or culpability of the parent, not due to benign statutory termination such as the child reaching the age of majority (emancipation).

4. Attempt by One Parent Against the Life of the Other (Art. 920[8] vs. Art. 920[2])

Under Article 920(8), a child or descendant may disinherit a parent for "an attempt by one of the parents against the life of the other, unless there has been a reconciliation between them." A critical doctrinal distinction exists between Article 920(8) and Article 920(2) regarding the requirement of conviction and the legal effect of reconciliation between the parents:

  • Under Article 920(8) (No Conviction Required; Extinguished by Parental Reconciliation): If the father attempts to kill the mother, the child can disinherit the father under Article 920(8) even if no criminal case was filed and there was no conviction. However, because the primary offended party is the mother, if the mother subsequently forgives and reconciles with the father, the child loses the right to disinherit the father under Article 920(8).
  • Under Article 920(2) (With Final Conviction; Unaffected by Parental Reconciliation): If the father attempts to kill the mother (who is an ascendant of the child-testator), is prosecuted, and is convicted by final judgment, the child's right to disinherit the father falls squarely under Article 920(2). Once a final conviction is handed down, a subsequent reconciliation between the mother and the father does NOT bar the child from disinheriting the father under Article 920(2). Under Article 922, only a reconciliation between the offender (the father) and the testator himself (the child) can extinguish the child's right to disinherit under Article 920(2).

Professor's Illustration: Parental Reconciliation After Conviction

A father attempts to murder the testator's mother. A criminal case for frustrated parricide is filed, and the father is convicted by final judgment. While serving his sentence, the mother forgives the father and fully reconciles with him. Can the child-testator still validly disinherit the father? Yes. While parental reconciliation extinguishes the ground under Article 920(8) (which applies when there is no conviction), the father's final conviction triggers Article 920(2) (conviction of an attempt against the life of the testator's ascendant). Only a personal reconciliation between the father and the child-testator under Article 922 can defeat a disinheritance anchored on Article 920(2).

IV. DISINHERITANCE OF A SPOUSE, RECONCILIATION, AND INEFFECTUAL DISINHERITANCE (ARTICLES 918, 921–923)

Specific Grounds for Disinheritance of a Spouse (Article 921)

Under Article 921 of the Civil Code, a testator may validly disinherit his or her surviving spouse upon any of six exclusive statutory causes:

  1. Conviction of an attempt against the life of the testator, his or her descendants, or ascendants (Art. 921[1]).
  2. Groundless accusation against the testator of a crime for which the law prescribes imprisonment for six years or more (Art. 921[2]).
  3. Causing the testator by fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence to make a will or to change one already made (Art. 921[3]).
  4. When the spouse has given cause for legal separation (Art. 921[4]).
  5. When the spouse has given grounds for the loss of parental authority (Art. 921[5]).
  6. Unjustifiable refusal to support the children or the other spouse (Art. 921[6]).

Incorporation of Family Code Grounds for Legal Separation (Article 921[4])

Under Article 921(4), a final judicial decree of legal separation is not required to disinherit a spouse; it is sufficient that the offending spouse "has given cause" for legal separation. Note that if a final decree of legal separation has already been granted by the Family Court prior to the testator's death, the offending spouse is disqualified from inheriting by operation of law under Article 63(4) of the Family Code without any need for testamentary disinheritance. Thus, Article 921(4) is specifically utilized when the testator chooses not to file (or has not yet completed) a petition for legal separation, but executes a will disinheriting the guilty spouse based on any of the ten grounds under Article 55 of the Family Code:

  • Repeated physical violence or grossly abusive conduct directed against the testator, a common child, or a child of the testator (e.g., a spouse who habitually batters or physically abuses the testator).
  • Physical violence or moral pressure to compel the testator to change religious or political affiliation.
  • Attempt of the respondent spouse to corrupt or induce the testator, a common child, or a child of the testator, to engage in prostitution, or connivance in such corruption or inducement.
  • Final judgment sentencing the respondent spouse to imprisonment of more than six years, even if pardoned.
  • Drug addiction or habitual alcoholism of the respondent spouse.
  • Lesbianism or homosexuality of the respondent spouse.
  • Contracting by the respondent spouse of a subsequent bigamous marriage, whether in the Philippines or abroad.
  • Sexual infidelity or perversion (which is broader than criminal adultery or concubinage and requires no criminal conviction).
  • Attempt by the respondent spouse against the life of the testator (note that because this is a cause for legal separation under Art. 55[9] of the Family Code, a spouse who attempts to kill the testator personally can be disinherited under Art. 921[4] even without a prior criminal conviction, bypassing the conviction requirement of Art. 921[1]).
  • Abandonment of the testator without justifiable cause for more than one year.

The Doctrine of Reconciliation (Article 922)

Under Article 922 of the Civil Code, a subsequent reconciliation between the offender and the offended person (the testator) produces two automatic legal effects:

  1. If made before the execution of a will, it deprives the testator of the right to disinherit the offending heir for that specific offense.
  2. If made after the execution of a will containing a valid disinheritance, it renders the disinheritance ineffectual by operation of law, without requiring the testator to execute a new will or codicil revoking the disinheritance.

Bilateral Nature of Reconciliation vs. Unilateral Pardon

For Article 922 to operate, there must be a genuine bilateral reconciliation, not a mere unilateral pardon or general expression of forgiveness. Reconciliation requires:

  1. An offer of forgiveness by the offended testator;
  2. An acceptance of that forgiveness by the offending compulsory heir; and
  3. An actual restoration of the former familial relationship and harmony between the parties.

Professor's Illustration: Deathbed Forgiveness vs. True Reconciliation

A testator who previously executed a valid will disinheriting an erring son lies on his deathbed. Fearing spiritual judgment as he is about to expire, the testator declares before witnesses: "I forgive everyone who has wronged me, including the children I disinherited in my will." The testator dies hours later without the disinherited son accepting the pardon or resuming their filial relationship. The disinheritance remains valid and effective. A unilateral, general deathbed pardon does not satisfy Article 922, which requires mutual reconciliation and the actual restoration of former relations between the offender and the offended testator.

Representation of the Disinherited Heir (Article 923)

Under Article 923 of the Civil Code, although a validly disinherited compulsory heir forfeits his or her legitime, the children and descendants of the person disinherited shall take his or her place and shall preserve the rights of compulsory heirs with respect to the legitime.

  • Underlying Rationale: Grounded in the principle that the guilt of the parent must not be visited upon the innocent child (peccata patrum non nocent filiis), the law elevates the disinherited heir's descendants by right of representation.
  • Extent of Representation: The representatives are entitled only to the legitime that the disinherited parent would have received. If the testator also left the free portion unassigned, the disinherited heir's children may additionally represent their parent in the intestate distribution of the undisposed free portion. However, the disinherited parent is strictly barred from enjoying the usufruct or administration of the property that constitutes the legitime inherited by his or her children.

Legal Effects of Imperfect or Ineffectual Disinheritance (Article 918)

Under Article 918 of the Civil Code, a disinheritance is ineffectual (imperfect or defective) when it is made: (1) without expression of the cause; (2) for a cause the truth of which, if contradicted, is not proved; or (3) for a cause which is not one of those set forth in the Civil Code.

  • Effect on the Institution of Heirs: Ineffectual disinheritance annuls the institution of heirs insofar as it may prejudice the person disinherited, meaning the invalidly disinherited compulsory heir must first be given his or her full legitime. However, unlike preterition under Article 854 (which annuls the institution of heirs totally unless there are legacies and devises), imperfect disinheritance under Article 918 annuls the institution of heirs only partially—to the extent necessary to satisfy the prejudiced heir's legitime. The institution of heirs over the free portion remains valid.
  • When the Invalidly Disinherited Heir Receives More Than the Legitime: While an invalidly disinherited heir is always guaranteed at least his or her legitime, he or she will receive more than the legitime (i.e., his or her full intestate share) if the testator failed to validly dispose of the free portion—such as when no voluntary heirs were instituted, or when the institution of heirs was totally annulled due to the concurrent preterition of another compulsory heir in the direct line.

Professor's Illustration: Scenario 1 — Invalid Disinheritance Concurrent with Preterition

Testator X dies leaving four legitimate children (A, B, C, and D) and a net estate of ₱120 Million. In his will, X states: "I disinherit my son D. Simultaneously, the complete omission of C—a compulsory heir in the direct line—constitutes preterition under Article 854, which totally annuls the institution of A and B. Because both the disinheritance and the institution of heirs are void, the entire ₱120 Million estate passes by intestate succession, dividing equally among A, B, C, and D at ₱30 Million each (exceeding D's strict legitime of ₱15 Million).

Professor's Illustration: Scenario 2 — Invalid Disinheritance Without Preterition

Assume the same facts (estate of ₱120 Million; legitimate children A, B, C, and D), except X's will states: "I disinherit my son D. I give my entire estate to my children A, B, and C." Here, there is no preterition because all four children were mentioned in the will. The disinheritance of D is invalid under Article 918 for lack of a stated cause, which annuls the institution of A, B, and C only insofar as it prejudices D's legitime. The collective legitime of the four legitimate children is one-half of the estate (₱60 Million), giving A, B, C, and D ₱15 Million each. Because the institution of A, B, and C over the ₱60 Million free portion remains valid, they divide the free portion equally (₱20 Million each). The remaining ₱105 Million (comprising A, B, and C's legitimes of ₱45 Million plus the ₱60 Million free portion instituted to A, B, and C) is divided equally among A, B, and C at ₱35 Million each.

V. FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES AND ORDERS OF INTESTATE SUCCESSION (ARTICLES 960–962, 978–1014)

1. The Rule of Preference of Lines

In intestate succession, relatives of the decedent are grouped into three successional lines, which enjoy a strict hierarchy of preference:

  1. The Direct Descending Line (children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren);
  2. The Direct Ascending Line (parents, grandparents, great-grandparents); and
  3. The Collateral Line (brothers, sisters, nephews, nieces, uncles, aunts, cousins).
1. Direct Descending Line
Children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren
↓ Strictly Preferred Over
2. Direct Ascending Line
Parents, grandparents, great-grandparents
↓ Strictly Preferred Over
3. Collateral Line
Brothers, sisters, nephews, nieces, uncles, aunts, cousins
  • Operative Rule: The direct descending line is strictly preferred over the direct ascending line, and the direct ascending line is strictly preferred over the collateral line. Consequently, the presence of even one legitimate child or descendant completely excludes the decedent's legitimate parents and ascendants. Similarly, if the decedent leaves no descendants, the presence of surviving parents or ascendants completely excludes the decedent's brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (who belong to the collateral line).

2. The Rule of Proximity of Degree (Article 962)

Under Article 962 of the Civil Code, in every inheritance, the relative nearest in degree excludes the more distant ones, saving the right of representation when it properly takes place.

  • Subordination to the Order of Intestate Succession: While proximity of degree governs priority within the same line or class, the Rule of Proximity always yields to the statutory Order of Intestate Succession. A relative who is in an equal or even nearer degree of consanguinity will be excluded by another relative who belongs to a higher preferred class in the statutory order of intestacy.

3. The Regular vs. Irregular Order of Intestate Succession

Philippine law establishes two distinct hierarchies of intestate succession depending on the filial status of the decedent (the person who died):

  • Regular Order of Intestate Succession: Applies when the decedent is a legitimate person.
  • Irregular Order of Intestate Succession: Applies when the decedent is an illegitimate person.
Priority Rank Regular Order (Legitimate Decedent) Irregular Order (Illegitimate Decedent) Key Doctrinal Distinctions
1st Class Legitimate Children and Legitimate Descendants (Art. 979) Legitimate Children and Legitimate Descendants (Art. 979) Identical in both orders; always first in preference.
2nd Class Legitimate Parents and Legitimate Ascendants (Art. 985) Illegitimate Children and their Descendants, legitimate or illegitimate (Art. 988) Ranks 2 and 3 interchange. In the Regular Order, legitimate ascendants come before illegitimate children (though they concur). In the Irregular Order, illegitimate children exclude illegitimate parents.
3rd Class Illegitimate Children and their Descendants, legitimate or illegitimate (Art. 988) Illegitimate Parents ONLY (Art. 993) For an illegitimate decedent, succession in the ascending line stops strictly at illegitimate parents; it does not ascend to grandparents or other ascendants due to the barrier under Art. 992.
4th Class Surviving Spouse (Art. 995) Surviving Spouse (Art. 994) Identical rank in both orders (without prejudice to concurrence with Ranks 1, 2, 3, and 5).
5th Class Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Art. 1003) Illegitimate Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Art. 994) Identical rank, though for an illegitimate decedent, Article 992 bars inheritance by/from legitimate brothers and sisters.
6th Class Other Collateral Relatives within the 5th civil degree (Art. 1009) The State (Art. 1011) Other collateral relatives (uncles, aunts, cousins up to the 5th degree) inherit only in the Regular Order. In the Irregular Order, they are excluded by Art. 992, so the State is Rank 6.
7th Class The State (Art. 1011) (None — Ends at Rank 6) In the Regular Order, the State inherits only upon failure of 5th-degree collaterals.

4. Rules on Concurrence and Exclusion in Intestate Succession

Being listed in a particular numerical rank in the Order of Intestate Succession does not mean that an upper rank automatically excludes all lower ranks. Several classes concur (inherit simultaneously) with one another:

  • Concurrence of Legitimate Children, Illegitimate Children, and Surviving Spouse: In the Regular Order, Rank 1 (Legitimate Children), Rank 3 (Illegitimate Children), and Rank 4 (Surviving Spouse) concur with one another. The presence of legitimate children excludes only Rank 2 (Legitimate Parents and Ascendants) and Ranks 5 to 7, but does not exclude illegitimate children or the surviving spouse.
  • Exclusion of Illegitimate Parents by Any Child (Irregular Order): In the Irregular Order, the presence of either legitimate children (Rank 1) or illegitimate children (Rank 2) completely excludes the illegitimate parents (Rank 3) pursuant to Article 993.
  • Concurrence of Surviving Spouse with Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Article 1001): If the decedent dies without descendants or ascendants, the Surviving Spouse (Rank 4) does NOT exclude Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces (Rank 5). Instead, they concur: one-half (1/2) of the estate goes to the surviving spouse, and the other one-half (1/2) goes to the brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces as a class.
  • Exclusion of Other Collaterals by Brothers, Sisters, Nephews, and Nieces: Within the collateral line, brothers, sisters, nephews, and nieces (Rank 5) form a privileged class that completely excludes all other collateral relatives (Rank 6), such as uncles, aunts, and first cousins, even if they stand in the exact same civil degree of relationship to the decedent.

Professor's Illustration: Subordination of Proximity to the Order of Intestate Succession

Decedent X (a legitimate person) dies intestate without descendants, ascendants, or a spouse. He is survived by his Uncle E (the brother of X's predeceased father) and his two Nephews, C and D (the children of X's predeceased brothers, A and B). Computing civil degrees under Articles 963–966: Uncle E is three degrees removed from X (X up to Father [1], up to Grandfather [2], down to Uncle E [3]). Nephews C and D are also three degrees removed from X (X up to Father [1], down to Brothers A/B [2], down to Nephews C/D [3]). Although Uncle E and Nephews C and D stand in equal proximity (3rd civil degree), Nephews C and D exclude Uncle E and inherit the entire estate. The Rule of Proximity yields to the Order of Intestate Succession, where nephews and nieces belong to Rank 5, whereas uncles and aunts belong to Rank 6 (Other Collateral Relatives).

VI. THE RIGHT OF REPRESENTATION IN TESTATE AND INTESTATE SUCCESSION (ARTICLES 969–977)

Definition and Nature of Representation (Article 970)

Under Article 970 of the Civil Code, representation is a right created by fiction of law, by virtue of which the representative is raised to the place and the degree of the person represented, and acquires the rights which the latter would have if he were living or if he could have inherited. Through this legal fiction, a more distant relative (such as a grandchild) is elevated into the shoes of a nearer relative (such as a predeceased child) so as not to be excluded by other surviving nearer relatives under the Rule of Proximity.

Operative Causes and Scope: Testate vs. Intestate Succession

The availability and scope of the right of representation differ fundamentally depending on whether the succession is testate or intestate:

Parameter Testate Succession (With a Will) Intestate Succession (Without a Will)
Operative Causes (When It Exists) 1. Predecease (Art. 856)
2. Incapacity / Unworthiness (Art. 1035)
3. Valid Disinheritance (Art. 923)		 1. Predecease (Art. 981)
2. Incapacity / Unworthiness (Art. 1035)
(Note: Disinheritance does not exist in intestacy because it requires a valid will).
Scope of Property Inherited Covers ONLY the Legitime. There is no right to represent a voluntary heir or take the free portion instituted to the represented person, because testamentary liberality is strictly personal to the instituted heir. Covers ALL (the entire intestate share) that the person represented would have inherited if he or she were living or could have inherited.

Lineal and Status Restrictions on Representation

  1. Direct Descending Line Only (Never in the Ascending Line): Under Article 972 of the Civil Code, representation takes place continuously in the direct descending line, but NEVER in the ascending line. Thus, a grandchild or great-grandchild can represent a father, but a paternal grandfather can never represent a predeceased father to inherit from a grandson.
  2. Collateral Line Restriction: Under Article 972, in the collateral line, representation takes place only in favor of the children of brothers or sisters (nephews and nieces), whether they be of the full or half blood. Great-nephews and great-nieces (grandnephews/grandnieces) have no right of representation. Furthermore, under Article 975, nephews and nieces inherit by right of representation (per stirpes) only when they survive with at least one uncle or aunt (a surviving brother or sister of the decedent); if only nephews and nieces survive without any living uncle or aunt, they inherit in their own right (per capita) in equal shares.
  3. Exclusion of Adopted Children: Under established Philippine civil law doctrine, an adopted child cannot represent the adopter, nor can an adopted child be represented by his or her descendants in the succession of the adopter's relatives. Because the legal fiction of adoption creates a relationship purely personal between the adopter and the adopted, it does not extend to create blood ties with the adopter's parents or other relatives.

The Two-Way Rule on Repudiation (Articles 976 and 977)

A crucial distinction governs the interplay between repudiation (the voluntary renunciation of an inheritance) and the right of representation:

✕
Article 977: Repudiating Heir CANNOT Be Represented
If Heir B renounces Decedent X's estate, B's child C cannot represent B in X's estate
✓
Article 976: Heir MAY Represent Person Whose Estate He Renounced
If C renounced predeceased Father B's estate, C can still represent B in Grandfather X's estate
  • Rule 1: An Heir Who Repudiates CANNOT Be Represented (Article 977): Under Article 977, heirs who repudiate their share may not be represented. Unlike predecease, incapacity, or disinheritance (which are involuntary deprivations), an heir who is alive and capacitated but voluntarily waives his inheritance effectively surrenders that share for both himself and his line; his children cannot step into his shoes by representation.
  • Rule 2: An Heir MAY Represent a Person Whose Inheritance He Previously Repudiated (Article 976): Under Article 976, a person may represent him whose inheritance he has renounced. If a father dies first and his son repudiates the inheritance from the father's estate (e.g., because the father's estate is heavily indebted), and subsequently the paternal grandfather dies, the son can still represent his predeceased father in the inheritance of the grandfather's estate. In representation, the representative inherits directly from the decedent (the grandfather), not through the estate of the person represented (the father).

Professor's Illustration: Contrasting Article 977 and Article 976 on Repudiation

Case A (Art. 977): Grandfather X dies intestate, survived by his two sons, A and B, and B's son (Grandson C). B repudiates his inheritance from X. C cannot represent B because a repudiating heir cannot be represented (Art. 977), nor can C inherit in his own right because Uncle A (a nearer 1st-degree relative) excludes C under the Rule of Proximity; thus, A takes the entire estate. Case B (Art. 976): Son B predeceases Grandfather X. Upon B's death, Grandson C repudiates his inheritance from B's estate. Later, Grandfather X dies, survived by Son A and Grandson C. C can validly represent B in X's estate and take half of X's estate alongside A, because an heir may represent a predeceased parent whose own estate he previously renounced (Art. 976).

Grandchildren Inheriting Per Stirpes vs. Per Capita (Article 969)

As a general rule under Article 982, grandchildren and other descendants always inherit by right of representation (per stirpes), dividing among themselves the specific share pertaining to their respective parent. However, Article 969 of the Civil Code provides a singular exception: if ALL the children of the decedent repudiate the inheritance, the grandchildren do not inherit by representation (since representation is barred in repudiation under Art. 977); instead, being the next degree of relatives, they inherit in their own right (per capita), dividing the estate into equal individual shares.

Professor's Illustration: The ₱300 Million Estate — Representation vs. Universal Repudiation

Decedent X dies intestate leaving a ₱300 Million estate, three children (A, B, and G), and five grandchildren: D (sole child of A), E and F (children of B), and H and I (children of G).

  • Scenario 1 (Predecease, Incapacity, and Disinheritance): Suppose A was validly disinherited (in a prior will disposing only of disinheritance), B is incapacitated, and G predeceased X. All three causes permit representation (per stirpes). The ₱300M estate is divided into 3 lines of ₱100M each: D takes ₱100M (representing A); E and F take ₱50M each (sharing B's ₱100M); and H and I take ₱50M each (sharing G's ₱100M).
  • Scenario 2 (Universal Repudiation by All Children): Suppose A, B, and G are all alive and capacitated, and all three repudiate their inheritance from X in hopes of favoring their children. Under Article 969, representation is barred, and the five grandchildren (D, E, F, H, and I) inherit in their own right (per capita). The ₱300M estate is divided equally by 5, giving D, E, F, H, and I ₱60 Million each.

VII. THE BARRIER BETWEEN LEGITIMATES AND ILLEGITIMATES (ARTICLE 992) AND INTERPLAY WITH ARTICLE 777

The Iron Curtain Rule (Article 992)

Under Article 992 of the Civil Code, an illegitimate child has no right to inherit ab intestato from the legitimate children and relatives of his father or mother; nor shall such children or relatives inherit in the same manner from the illegitimate child.

  • Historical Rationale vs. Modern Context: Known in Philippine jurisprudence as the "Iron Curtain Rule" or the successional barrier, Article 992 was originally enacted by the Code Commission on the presumption of mutual hostility and animosity between the legitimate family and the illegitimate child (historically viewed as the product of sin or marital infidelity). While modern social realities increasingly reflect blended or "hybrid" families where legitimate and nonmarital children cohabit harmoniously, Article 992 remains binding statutory law until repealed by Congress.
  • Terminology Caveat for the Bar Examinations: In recent Supreme Court jurisprudence (notably penned by Senior Associate Justice Marvic Leonen, [Assumed reference to Aquino v. Aquino, G.R. No. 208912, Dec. 7, 2021]), the Court adopted the sociological term "nonmarital children" to avoid the pejorative stigma attached to the word "illegitimate." However, for purposes of the Bar Examinations, examinees must continue to use the statutory term "illegitimate children" as expressly written in the Civil Code and Family Code to ensure precision in codal citations.
  • Restricted to Intestate Succession: By its express terms ("ab intestato"), the barrier under Article 992 applies strictly to intestate succession (and by extension, to representation in compulsory succession). It does not prohibit a testator from voluntarily instituting an illegitimate half-brother, stepchild, or illegitimate grandchild as a voluntary heir, legatee, or devisee out of the free portion of the estate in a valid will.

Qualification of Representatives Based on Filial Status (Articles 902, 989, 990, and 992)

When applying the Right of Representation in the direct descending line, the interaction between Article 992 (which bars an illegitimate child from representing a legitimate parent in the succession of a legitimate grandparent) and Articles 902 and 990 (which transmit the rights of an illegitimate child to both his legitimate and illegitimate descendants) yields a cardinal rule:

  1. If the person to be represented is a LEGITIMATE child: He or she can be represented ONLY by a legitimate child or descendant. An illegitimate child of a legitimate child is barred by Article 992 from representing his parent in the estate of the legitimate grandparent.
  2. If the person to be represented is an ILLEGITIMATE child: He or she can be represented by BOTH legitimate and illegitimate children or descendants (Articles 902 and 990). When both legitimate and illegitimate descendants represent an illegitimate parent, they divide the represented parent's share between themselves observing the 2:1 ratio (each legitimate representative gets double the share of each illegitimate representative).

Professor's Illustration: Legitimate vs. Illegitimate Representation and the 2:1 Ratio

Decedent X dies intestate. X had two predeceased children: A (a legitimate child) and B (an illegitimate child). Predeceased legitimate child A left two children: C (legitimate) and D (illegitimate). Predeceased illegitimate child B also left two children: E (legitimate) and F (illegitimate).

Jurisprudential Analysis: Dela Merced v. Dela Merced (G.R. No. 126707, February 25, 1999)

A frequent Bar Examination trap involves confusing succession by right of representation (where Article 992 applies) with two successive, independent transmissions of inheritance governed by Article 777 of the Civil Code, which provides that "the rights to the succession are transmitted from the moment of the death of the decedent."

Case Brief: Dela Merced v. Dela Merced (G.R. No. 126707, February 25, 1999)

  • Facts: A, B, C, and D were legitimate siblings. A (Evarista) died intestate without issue, leaving an estate inherited by her surviving legitimate siblings B (Francisco), C, and D (say, ₱300 Million total, or ₱100 Million each). Subsequently, B died intestate, and his recognized illegitimate son, S (Joselito), claimed B's entire estate, including the ₱100 Million share B had inherited from his legitimate sister A; C and D opposed S's claim, invoking the Iron Curtain Rule under Article 992 to argue that S, being an illegitimate child, could not inherit property originating from B's legitimate sibling A.
  • Issue: Does Article 992 of the Civil Code bar an illegitimate child (S) from inheriting property that his deceased parent (B) had previously inherited from a legitimate sibling (A) who died before the parent?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No. The Supreme Court held that Article 992 is completely inapplicable because B did not predecease A; rather, B survived A, and under Article 777 of the Civil Code, B's one-third share (₱100 Million) in A's estate vested in B immediately upon A's death, becoming an absolute, indistinguishable part of B's own personal estate. When B subsequently died, his illegitimate son S inherited that property in his own right directly from his father B (as a compulsory heir of B), and not by right of representation in A's estate; hence, there was no collateral intestate succession between S and A for Article 992 to block.
