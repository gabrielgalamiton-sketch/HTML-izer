I. NATURE, PURPOSE, AND JURISPRUDENTIAL FOUNDATIONS OF INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY RIGHTS

Concept and Commercial Ubiquity of Intellectual Property

Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the mind—such as inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, symbols, names, and images—used in commerce and daily life. Far from being a purely theoretical or elitist legal regime, Intellectual Property Law (governed primarily by Republic Act No. 8293, otherwise known as the Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines [IP Code]) regulates everyday commercial articles and digital interactions. Consumer goods such as branded bags, skincare formulations, textbooks, mobile applications, artificial intelligence training datasets, and secret culinary recipes are all governed by distinct spheres of intellectual property protection.

Broad Bifurcation of Intellectual Property

Under international conventions and Philippine commercial law, intellectual property is broadly classified into two main branches:

  1. Industrial Property: Encompasses creations and commercial identifiers utilized in manufacturing, trade, and industry. This category includes patents (inventions), utility models, industrial designs, trademarks, service marks, trade names, geographic indications of source, and the protection of undisclosed information (trade secrets). Industrial property typically relates to commercial goods, technical processes, or tangible articles of manufacture.
  2. Copyright and Related (Neighboring) Rights: Encompasses original and derivative intellectual creations in the literary, scientific, and artistic domains. Related rights protect the legal interests of performing artists in their performances, producers of phonograms (sound recordings) in their recordings, and broadcasting organizations in their radio and television programs.

Legal Characteristics of Intellectual Property Rights

Intellectual property rights possess four fundamental legal attributes that define their enforcement and limitation under Philippine law:

  • Statutory in Nature: Intellectual property rights are purely creatures of statute. The exclusive rights to exploit, license, or prohibit unauthorized third-party use of an intellectual creation exist only because the law (R.A. No. 8293 and related special laws) expressly confers and defines them. Absent a statutory grant or compliance with statutory requisites, no exclusive monopoly may be asserted against the public.
  • Incorporeal and Intangible: An intellectual property right is an intangible right (res incorporales) that exists separately and distinctly from the physical or material object in which it is embodied. Pursuant to the principle reflected in Section 181 of the IP Code, the transfer or sale of the physical object (e.g., purchasing a physical book, painting, or laptop) does not, by itself, transfer the underlying copyright, patent, or trademark attached to that object.
  • Private Right: Intellectual property is a private property right vested in an individual creator, inventor, or juridical entity. Within the bounds of the law, the right-holder may exercise exclusivity, restrict unauthorized commercial exploitation, assign the right, or collect royalties through licensing agreements.
  • Not Absolute (Subject to Statutory Limitations and Fixed Duration): Intellectual property rights are neither perpetual nor absolute. The State grants a temporary monopoly to incentivize innovation and reward creative labor, balanced against the overarching public interest. Once the statutory term of protection expires, the invention or creative work falls into the public domain, becoming available for free use and replication by the general public. Furthermore, even during the term of protection, the law imposes limitations such as fair use, compulsory licensing, parallel importation, and government exploitation for national security or public health.

Professor's Illustration

Application Example: Consider a consumer purchasing a high-end laptop. While the buyer acquires ownership over the corporeal machine under the Civil Code, multiple incorporeal IP rights remain vested in the manufacturer. The brand logo on the lid is protected as a trademark; the outer chassis contour is protected as an industrial design; the internal semiconductor architecture is protected as a layout-design of an integrated circuit; the novel cooling mechanism is covered by a patent; and the pre-installed operating system is protected by copyright.

Copyright and Related Rights

Under Sections 172.1 and 172.2 of the IP Code, copyright subsists in original and derivative intellectual creations in the literary and artistic domain. The defining hallmark of copyright is that works are protected from the moment of their creation, irrespective of their mode or form of expression, as well as of their content, quality, and purpose. Registration and deposit of the work with the National Library or the Intellectual Property Office of the Philippines (IPOPHL) are not conditions precedent for the acquisition of copyright; rather, registration merely establishes a prima facie presumption of ownership and facilitates evidentiary proof in infringement suits. Covered works range from books, musical compositions, paintings, sculptures, and cinematographic films to computer programs, databases, advertisements, maps, and technical drawings.

Trademarks and Service Marks

Under Section 121.1 of the IP Code, a "mark" means any visible sign capable of distinguishing the goods (trademark) or services (service mark) of an enterprise and shall include a stamped or marked container of goods. While commercial parlance often uses "trademark" as an umbrella term, the statute draws a functional distinction based on the commercial offering of the enterprise:

  • Trademark: A visible sign used to identify and distinguish tangible goods or merchandise manufactured or sold by one enterprise from those of others (e.g., Apple for mobile phones, Jollibee for food products).
  • Service Mark: A visible sign used to identify and distinguish services rendered by an enterprise that does not sell physical goods, but rather offers intangible services to the public (e.g., Netflix for digital streaming services, Grab for ride-hailing and transport logistics, BDO for banking services, or the brand name and logo of a law firm, accounting firm, or management consultancy).
  • Stamped or Marked Container of Goods: The distinctive three-dimensional configuration or embossed bottle/packaging of a product capable of distinguishing the goods contained therein.

Industrial Designs and Patents (Overview)

  • Industrial Design (Sec. 112.1, IP Code): Any composition of lines or colors or any three-dimensional form, whether or not associated with lines or colors, provided that such composition or form gives a special appearance to and can serve as a pattern for an industrial product or handicraft. It protects the ornamental, aesthetic, or structural framework of a commercial article (e.g., the ergonomic contour of a chair or the external cap structure of a specialized tumbler).
  • Patent (Sec. 21, IP Code): An exclusive statutory grant issued over an invention that provides any technical solution of a problem in any field of human activity which is new (novel), involves an inventive step, and is industrially applicable.

III. GEOGRAPHIC INDICATIONS, LAYOUT-DESIGNS OF INTEGRATED CIRCUITS, AND TRADE SECRETS

Geographic Indications (GIs)

Under Section 4.1(c) of the IP Code [Note: Transcript references Sec. 112; the substantive definition is anchored in Sec. 4.1(c) of R.A. 8293 in relation to Art. 22.1 of the TRIPS Agreement and the IPOPHL Bureau of Trademarks Regulations on Geographical Indications], Geographic Indications are indications which identify a good as originating in the territory of a country, or a region or locality in that territory, where a given quality, reputation, or other characteristic of the good is essentially attributable to its geographical origin.

Unlike a regular trademark which identifies a specific commercial enterprise, a geographic indication links the reputation and superior quality of a product directly to the terroir, climate, raw materials, or traditional craftsmanship of a specific geographic locality.

Global and Local Illustrations of Geographic Indications
  • International Examples: Bordeaux (France) for wine; Grasse (France) for high-grade perfumery fragrances; Tuscany (Italy) for extra-virgin olive oil; and Uji, Kyoto (Japan) for ceremonial-grade matcha green tea cultivars.
  • Philippine Local Examples: Vigan (Ilocos Sur) for Vigan Longganisa; Baguio (Benguet) for strawberry jam and peanut brittle; Ilocos for Inabel handwoven textiles; and South Cotabato / Lake Sebu (Mindanao) for T'nalak abaca cloth woven by the T'boli people.
Four Principal Categories of Goods Protected by Geographic Indications
  1. Agricultural Products: Fresh produce, tea leaves (e.g., Uji matcha), coffee beans, and regional crops whose flavor profile depends on local soil and climate.
  2. Foodstuffs, Wines, and Spirits: Prepared regional delicacies, cured meats (e.g., Vigan longganisa), olive oils (e.g., Tuscany), and fermented beverages (e.g., Bordeaux wine, Champagne).
  3. Textiles and Clothing: Traditional woven fabrics and garments utilizing indigenous techniques and local fibers (e.g., Ilocos Inabel, T'boli T'nalak).
  4. Handicrafts and Industrial Products: Artisanal pottery, woodwork, jewelry, or manufactured goods associated with the specialized skill of a locality (e.g., Grasse perfumery).

Layout-Designs (Topographies) of Integrated Circuits

Under Section 112 of the IP Code, as amended by R.A. No. 9150, the law protects both the physical semiconductor chip concept and its three-dimensional blueprint:

  • Integrated Circuit (Sec. 112.2, IP Code): A product, in its final form, or an intermediate form, in which the elements, at least one of which is an active element, and some or all of the interconnections are integrally formed in and/or on a piece of material, and which is intended to perform an electronic function.
  • Layout-Design / Topography (Sec. 112.3, IP Code): The three-dimensional disposition, however expressed, of the elements, at least one of which is an active element, and of some or all of the interconnections of an integrated circuit, or such a three-dimensional disposition prepared for an integrated circuit intended for manufacture. Only layout-designs that are original (i.e., the result of their creators' own intellectual effort and not commonplace among creators of layout-designs and manufacturers of integrated circuits at the time of creation) are entitled to protection for a non-renewable period of ten (10) years.

Protection of Undisclosed Information (Trade Secrets)

Under Section 4.1(g) of the IP Code, in relation to Article 39.2 of the TRIPS Agreement and Philippine jurisprudence, natural and legal persons have the right to prevent information lawfully within their control from being disclosed to, acquired by, or used by others without their consent in a manner contrary to honest commercial practices.

Three Requisites for Trade Secret Protection

To qualify as protected undisclosed information, the information must concurrently satisfy three statutory elements:

  1. Secrecy: The information is secret in the sense that it is not, as a body or in the precise configuration and assembly of its components, generally known among or readily accessible to persons within the circles that normally deal with the kind of information in question.
  2. Commercial Value: The information has actual or potential commercial value because it is secret (i.e., its confidentiality confers a competitive economic edge over rivals).
  3. Reasonable Protective Measures: The information has been subject to reasonable steps under the circumstances, by the person lawfully in control of the information, to keep it secret (e.g., non-disclosure agreements, restricted vault access, fragmented manufacturing steps).

Air Philippines Corporation v. Pennswell, Inc., G.R. No. 172835 (2007)

  • Facts: Pennswell sued Air Philippines to collect unpaid balances for industrial chemical solvents, prompting Air Philippines to demand the compulsory disclosure of the exact chemical composition of the solvents. Pennswell refused to disclose the chemical formula, invoking trade secret protection.
  • Issue: May a manufacturer be compelled in a collection suit to disclose the chemical formula and ingredients of its industrial products?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No, the chemical formula is privileged as a protected trade secret. Under R.A. No. 8293 and the TRIPS Agreement, a trade secret is any formula, pattern, device, or compilation of information used in one's business that gives the employer an opportunity to obtain an advantage over competitors who do not know or use it. Compelling disclosure would destroy its commercial value and cause irreparable economic injury to the proprietor.

Professor's Illustration

Application Example: A local family bakery develops an exclusive pastry flavoring, or a fast-food chain like KFC guards its "11 herbs and spices" blend. Instead of applying for a patent—which requires public disclosure of the exact formula and expires after 20 years—the business keeps the recipe confidential behind non-disclosure agreements and restricted kitchen access. Because the recipe is not generally known, derives commercial value from its secrecy, and is guarded by reasonable security measures, it is protected indefinitely as undisclosed information (trade secret) so long as secrecy is maintained.

Rule on Non-Interchangeability vs. Co-Existence Over a Single Product

  1. Strict Non-Interchangeability of IP Regimes: The legal regimes of trademark, patent, and copyright are mutually exclusive in their respective scopes and cannot be used interchangeably. A creator who obtains a copyright registration over a technical drawing or marketing poster does not thereby acquire a patent over the underlying mechanical device, nor does a trademark registration protect the functional utility of an article.
  2. Co-Existence of Multiple IP Rights Over a Single Commercial Product: While the legal terms are not interchangeable, several distinct intellectual property rights may simultaneously subsist over different facets of a single physical product. Because IP rights are incorporeal, each distinct aspect of an item—its brand identifier, its ornamental shape, its separable artistic surface design, and its internal technical mechanism—is governed by its corresponding IP category.

Kho v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 115758 (2002)

  • Facts: Elidad Kho registered the business name and copyright/patent over the containers and labels of "Chin Chun Su" medicated facial cream and sought a preliminary injunction to stop Summerville General Merchandising from selling cream under the same brand. Summerville proved that it was the exclusive importer authorized by the Taiwanese manufacturer who originated the product.
  • Issue: Does registration of a copyright or industrial design over a container confer the exclusive trademark right to use the trade name "Chin Chun Su" on medicated cream?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No, trademark, copyright, and patents are completely different intellectual property rights that cannot be interchanged with one another. A copyright over the label design or a patent over the container shape does not grant an exclusive right over the brand name itself, which falls strictly within the domain of trademark law.

Professor's Illustrations on Multi-Layered IP Protection

Application Example 1 (Designer Coffee Mug & Casetify x Heart Evangelista Phone Case): A specialty coffee tumbler or a Casetify phone case features a collaboration artwork painted by Heart Evangelista. Here, three distinct IP rights co-exist: (1) The brand name and distinctive camera-ring logo of Casetify or the stamped container mark is protected as a Trademark; (2) Heart Evangelista's painting printed on the removable cup sleeve or phone backplate is protected by Copyright as an artistic work because its aesthetic value can be physically or conceptually separated from the utilitarian aspect of the mug or phone case (the case still protects the phone even without the painting); and (3) The ergonomic shape or protective bumper contour of the case or mug lid can be registered as an Industrial Design, though not as an invention patent because a standard case or mug lacks novelty and an inventive step.

Application Example 2 (Apple iPhone): A single Apple iPhone embodies four distinct IP regimes simultaneously: (1) The bitten-apple logo and the wordmark "iPhone" are protected by Trademark Law; (2) The iOS operating system source code, icons, and graphical user interface are protected by Copyright Law; (3) The external rounded-rectangle glass-and-titanium housing is protected as an Industrial Design; and (4) The proprietary internal Bionic processor technology and periscope camera stabilization mechanism are protected by Invention Patents.

V. GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF PATENT LAW, UTILITY MODELS, AND INDUSTRIAL DESIGNS

Definition and Fourfold Policy Purpose of Patents

A patent is a statutory monopoly granted by the State to an inventor over an invention (or, in its broader sense under Part II of the IP Code, a registration granted over a utility model or an industrial design), conferring the exclusive right to make, use, offer for sale, sell, or import the same for commerce and industry for a limited period.

As established in Philippine jurisprudence (Pearl & Dean (Phil.), Inc. v. Shoemart, Inc., G.R. No. 148222, 2003), the patent system is anchored on a fourfold statutory policy:

  1. To Foster and Reward Invention: It incentivizes creative and scientific research by granting inventors a temporary exclusive period to recoup their investments and profit from their ingenuity.
  2. To Promote Disclosure of Inventions: In exchange for the monopoly, the inventor must fully disclose the technical specifications of the invention, thereby stimulating further innovation and permitting the public to practice the invention freely once the patent expires.
  3. To Safeguard the Public Domain: Stringent patentability standards ensure that ideas, natural laws, and existing technologies already in the public domain remain there for the free use of society.
  4. Exhaustive Administrative Examination: A patent is issued only after a rigorous substantive examination by the Bureau of Patents of the IPOPHL confirms that all statutory requisites are met.

Three Categories Under the Law on Patents: Inventions, Utility Models, and Industrial Designs

While the term "patent" ordinarily refers to Patentable Inventions, Part II of R.A. No. 8293 governs three distinct technical and structural registrations:

  • Invention Patent (Sec. 21, IP Code): Any technical solution of a problem in any field of human activity which is new (novel), involves an inventive step, and is industrially applicable. It may be, or may relate to, a product, or process, or an improvement of any of the foregoing.
  • Utility Model (Sec. 109.1, IP Code): Often termed a "petty patent" or "innovation patent," it refers to any technical solution or model of implements, tools, or product (or part thereof) of practical utility by reason of its form, configuration, construction, or composition. To qualify for registration as a utility model, the creation must be new and industrially applicable, but it does not require an inventive step. [Note: While colloquial classroom discussion sometimes describes a utility model as "semi-novel" because it merely modifies an existing tool without a "wow factor," Section 109.1 strictly requires novelty and industrial applicability, dispensing only with the requisite of an inventive step.]
  • Industrial Design (Sec. 112.1 & Sec. 113, IP Code): Any composition of lines or colors or any three-dimensional form that gives a special appearance to and can serve as a pattern for an industrial product or handicraft. It must be new or ornamental, and excludes anything dictated essentially by technical or functional considerations.

Professor's Illustrations on Inventions, Utility Models, and Industrial Designs

Application Example 1 (Utility Models — Frixion Pen, Bottom-Load Dispenser, and Karaoke Machine): A Pilot Frixion erasable pen combines a standard ballpoint pen with thermo-sensitive ink and a rubber friction tip; a bottom-load water dispenser relocates the heavy water gallon from the top to a lower cabinet to prevent back strain; and a Filipino coin-operated karaoke machine (videoke) integrates a microphone, television display, song-selection keypad, and coin slot into a wheeled cabinet. None of these exhibit a revolutionary "wow factor" (inventive step) that alters science, as their components are obvious to average mechanics, yet each offers practical utility and industrial applicability, qualifying them as Utility Models.

Application Example 2 (Inventions vs. Industrial Designs — Smartphones, Oura Ring, and Owala Tumbler): When first introduced to the market, smartphones, Google Maps GPS navigation, smartwatches, and the Oura smart health-tracking ring provided non-obvious technical solutions to human problems, qualifying as Patentable Inventions. Conversely, the distinctive outer aesthetic contour and dual-opening lid appearance of an Owala FreeSip tumbler (which allows sipping through a built-in straw or swigging through a wide mouth) can be registered as an Industrial Design for its special appearance, and as a Utility Model for its practical lid configuration.

VI. STATUTORY REQUISITES OF A PATENTABLE INVENTION: NOVELTY, INVENTIVE STEP, AND INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

Under Section 21 of the IP Code, an invention is patentable only if it is a technical solution of a problem in any field of human activity and concurrently satisfies three mandatory requisites: (1) Novelty (Newness); (2) Inventive Step; and (3) Industrial Applicability.

1. Novelty (Newness) and Prior Art

  • Statutory Rule on Novelty (Sec. 23, IP Code): An invention shall not be considered new if it forms part of a prior art. If an invention is already known or available to the public before the patent application is filed, it lacks novelty and cannot be patented.
  • Definition of Prior Art (Sec. 24, IP Code): Prior art consists of two broad components:
    1. Worldwide Public Availability (Sec. 24.1): Everything which has been made available to the public anywhere in the world, before the filing date or the priority date of the application claiming the invention (whether by written or oral disclosure, actual use, or any other means).
    2. Prior Philippine Applications (Sec. 24.2): The whole contents of a published application for a patent, utility model, or industrial design registration, filed or effective in the Philippines, with a filing or priority date that is earlier than the filing or priority date of the application, provided that the application was published in accordance with the IP Code and the inventors or applicants are not the same persons.
The "No Patent, No Protection" Rule

Under Philippine Patent Law, there is no protection without a valid patent registration. Unlike copyright which vests from the moment of creation, an inventor who fails to register a patent—or who prematurely releases the invention to the market outside the statutory grace period—cannot restrain third parties from manufacturing or selling the invention, as unpatented disclosed technology forms part of the prior art and belongs to the public domain.

Exception: Doctrine of Non-Prejudicial Disclosure (Sec. 25, IP Code)

Under Section 25.1 of the IP Code, the disclosure of information contained in the application during the twelve (12) months preceding the filing date or the priority date of the application shall not prejudice the applicant on the ground of lack of novelty if such disclosure was made by:

  1. The Inventor Himself: The inventor (or his successor-in-interest) publicly disclosed, tested, or exhibited the invention within the 12-month grace period prior to filing;
  2. A Patent Office: A patent office published the information in contravention of the law, or in a separate application filed by the inventor without his knowledge or consent by a third party; or
  3. A Third Party: A third party obtained the information directly or indirectly from the inventor (whether with consent or through breach of confidence).

Aguas v. De Leon, G.R. No. L-32160 (1982)

  • Facts: Conrado De Leon obtained a patent for a new process of making pre-cast mosaic tiles having a specific depth, lip width, and reinforcement that solved the problem of heavy tiles fracturing or detaching from walls. Domiciano Aguas copied the mould and manufactured identical tiles, arguing that mosaic tiles were old and not patentable.
  • Issue: Does De Leon's improved process and structural reinforcement for producing thinner, durable mosaic tiles constitute a patentable invention?
  • Ruling / Ratio: Yes, the patent is valid and enforceable. Under patent law, an improvement of an existing product or process is patentable if it is new, involves an inventive step, and provides a technical solution to an existing industrial problem. De Leon did not claim a monopoly over old mosaic tiles in general, but over his novel critical depth, lip width, and engraving process that eliminated tile fractures.

2. Inventive Step ("The Non-Obviousness / Wow Factor Standard")

  • Statutory Rule on Inventive Step (Sec. 26.1, IP Code): An invention involves an inventive step if, having regard to prior art, it is not obvious to a person skilled in the art at the time of the filing date or priority date of the application claiming the invention.
  • Meaning of "Person Skilled in the Art": A "person skilled in the art" does not refer to a Nobel laureate or a top-tier scientific genius; rather, jurisprudence defines this benchmark as an ordinary practitioner possessing average skill and general knowledge in the relevant technical field at the time of filing. If an average technician in that industry would find the solution obvious or predictable based on existing prior art (much like judges on a talent show seeing a routine performance as opposed to a non-obvious "golden buzzer" act like El Gamma Penumbra or Marcelito Pomoy), the creation lacks an inventive step.
  • Special Rule on Drugs and Medicines (Sec. 26.2, IP Code, as amended by R.A. No. 9502 [Universally Accessible Cheaper and Quality Medicines Act of 2008]): In the case of drugs and medicines, there is no inventive step if the invention results from the mere discovery of a new form or new property of a known substance which does not result in the enhancement of the known efficacy of that substance, or the mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance, or the mere use of a known process unless such known process results in a new product that employs at least one new reactant.

Professor's Illustration

Application Example (Glutathione Repurposing): Glutathione was originally developed and used in medicine as an antioxidant for liver treatment, during which patients and physicians observed skin lightening as a side effect. When pharmaceutical companies subsequently marketed and articulated glutathione capsules specifically as a skin-whitening agent, could they obtain an invention patent over glutathione for whitening? No. Under Section 22.1 and Section 26.2 of the IP Code (as amended by R.A. No. 9502), discovering a new property or new use of a known substance (its whitening side effect) without altering its chemical structure to enhance its known therapeutic efficacy lacks an inventive step and is non-patentable.

3. Industrial Applicability

Under Section 27 of the IP Code, an invention that can be produced and used in any industry shall be industrially applicable. This requires that the invention be capable of practical, repeatable manufacture and real-world utilization in commerce, agriculture, or industry, rather than remaining a purely abstract, theoretical, or inoperable concept.

VII. NON-PATENTABLE INVENTIONS AND STATUTORY EXCLUSIONS

Under Section 22 of the IP Code (as amended by R.A. No. 9502), the following are expressly excluded from patent protection even if they appear novel or useful:

  1. Discoveries, Scientific Theories, and Mathematical Methods (Sec. 22.1): Natural phenomena, laws of nature (e.g., Albert Einstein's Theory of Relativity, E=mc2), and mathematical formulas or algorithms cannot be monopolized by any individual, as they are fundamental tools of scientific and technological work. Furthermore, for drugs and medicines, this exclusion encompasses the mere discovery of a new form or new property of a known substance that does not enhance its known efficacy, or the mere discovery of any new property or new use for a known substance.
  2. Schemes, Rules, and Methods of Performing Mental Acts, Playing Games, or Doing Business, and Programs for Computers (Sec. 22.2): Abstract business models, accounting systems, rules of board games, mental calculation methods, and standalone computer programs are excluded from patentability. (Note: Computer programs are protected under Copyright Law pursuant to Sec. 172.1(n) of the IP Code).
  3. Methods for Treatment of the Human or Animal Body by Surgery or Therapy and Diagnostic Methods Practiced on the Human or Animal Body (Sec. 22.3): To protect public health and ensure that physicians and veterinarians are never restrained from saving lives, surgical techniques, therapeutic procedures, and diagnostic methods cannot be patented. Exception: This exclusion shall not apply to products and compositions for use in any of these methods (e.g., surgical instruments, medical devices, or novel pharmaceutical compounds with enhanced efficacy remain patentable).
  4. Plant Varieties or Animal Breeds or Essentially Biological Processes for the Production of Plants or Animals (Sec. 22.4): Living organisms found in nature, cross-bred plant varieties, animal breeds, and natural biological processes (e.g., an unaltered natural meat-tenderizing enzyme extracted from papaya) are not inventions of man. Exception: This exclusion shall not apply to micro-organisms and non-biological and microbiological processes (e.g., genetically engineered bacteria used to synthesize insulin or clean oil spills). (Note: Plant varieties are protected separately under R.A. No. 9168, the Philippine Plant Variety Protection Act of 2002).
  5. Aesthetic Creations (Sec. 22.5): Purely artistic, literary, or ornamental works lack technical functionality and fall under Copyright Law or Industrial Design, not invention patents.
  6. Anything Contrary to Public Order or Morality (Sec. 22.6): The State will not grant a monopoly over inventions designed for illegal, immoral, or socially destructive purposes (e.g., a device designed specifically to facilitate sexual assault, counterfeiting plates, or bogus coin detectors used exclusively in illegal gambling machines where gambling is prohibited by public policy).

VIII. OWNERSHIP OF PATENTS, THE FIRST-TO-FILE RULE, AND REMEDIES OF THE TRUE INVENTOR

General Rule on Patent Ownership (Sec. 28, IP Code)

The right to a patent belongs to the inventor, his heirs, or assigns. When two (2) or more persons have jointly made an invention, the right to a patent shall belong to them jointly.

Pearl & Dean (Phil.), Inc. v. Shoemart, Inc., G.R. No. 148222 (2003)

  • Facts: Pearl & Dean obtained a Certificate of Copyright Registration over the technical engineering drawings of its illuminated advertising display units ("lightboxes") and sued Shoemart (SM) for copyright and patent infringement when SM contracted other fabricators to construct similar lightboxes for its malls. Pearl & Dean had never applied for or secured a patent registration over the mechanical lightbox unit itself.
  • Issue: Did Pearl & Dean's copyright registration over the technical drawings confer an exclusive patent right over the physical lightbox units themselves?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No, Pearl & Dean cannot sue for infringement over the manufacture of the physical lightboxes. Copyright over engineering drawings covers only the printed graphical illustrations and posters, not the functional mechanical article depicted therein. To secure an exclusive monopoly over the manufacture and use of the physical lightbox structure, Pearl & Dean was required to obtain a patent; having failed to do so, the lightbox design was in the public domain and freely usable by competitors.

The First-to-File Rule (Sec. 29, IP Code)

Under Section 29 of the IP Code, if two (2) or more persons have made the invention separately and independently of each other, the right to the patent shall belong to the person who filed an application for such invention, or where two or more applications are filed for the same invention, to the applicant who has the earliest filing date or, the earliest priority date.

  • Crucial Qualification: The First-to-File Rule under Section 29 applies only when both parties invented the item separately, independently, and in good faith. If Applicant A merely stole, misappropriated, or copied the invention from True Inventor B and raced to the IPOPHL to file first, Section 29 does not apply because A did not make the invention independently.

Remedies of the True and Actual Inventor (Secs. 67–68, IP Code)

When a bad-faith usurper (A) files a patent application or obtains a patent over an invention stolen from the True and Actual Inventor (B), can B immediately file an action for Patent Infringement against A? No. An action for patent infringement under Section 76 is available only to a patentee (or his assignee/licensee) holding an issued patent. Because B does not yet hold a patent ("no patent, no protection"), B must first establish ownership in court.

Under Sections 67 and 68 of the IP Code, if a person other than the applicant is declared by final court order or decision as having the right to the patent, the True and Actual Inventor (B) may exercise the following remedies:

  1. Where the Usurper's Application is Still Pending (Sec. 67.1, IP Code): Within three (3) months after the court decision has become final, the true inventor may, at his option:
    • (a) Prosecute the application as his own application in place of the usurper;
    • (b) File a new patent application in respect of the same invention (which shall be accorded the filing date of the usurper's initial application without prejudice);
    • (c) Request that the pending application be refused; or
    • (d) Seek cancellation of the patent, if one has already been granted in the interim.
  2. Where a Patent Has Already Been Granted to the Usurper (Sec. 68, IP Code): If the true and actual inventor was deprived of the patent without his consent or through fraud, and is declared by final court order to be the true and actual inventor, the court shall order:
    • (a) His substitution as patentee, or
    • (b) At the option of the true inventor, the cancellation of the patent, and the award of actual and other damages in his favor if warranted by the circumstances.

Inventions Pursuant to a Commission vs. Employee Inventions (Sec. 30, IP Code)

  • Inventions Pursuant to a Commission (Sec. 30.1, IP Code): The person who commissions the work shall own the patent, unless otherwise provided in the contract.
    • Contrast with Commissioned Copyrightable Works (Sec. 178.4, IP Code): In Patent Law, the commissioning party owns both the physical output and the patent itself (unless agreed otherwise). Conversely, in Copyright Law, when a work is commissioned (e.g., a commissioned sculpture or portrait), the person who commissioned the work owns the physical object/work itself, but the copyright remains with the creator/author, unless there is a written stipulation to the contrary.
  • Inventions Created by an Employee (Sec. 30.2, IP Code): When an employee makes an invention in the course of an employment contract, ownership depends on the scope of the employee's regular duties:
    1. Owned by the Employee (Sec. 30.2[a]): The patent belongs to the employee if the inventive activity is not a part of his regular duties, even if the employee uses the time, facilities, and materials of the employer.
    2. Owned by the Employer (Sec. 30.2[b]): The patent belongs to the employer if the invention is the result of the performance of his regularly-assigned duties, unless there is an agreement, express or implied, to the contrary.
Scenario Ownership of the Right Statutory Exception
Commissioned Invention (Patent) Person who commissioned the work owns the patent (Sec. 30.1). Unless otherwise provided in the contract.
Commissioned Artistic Work (Copyright) Commissioning party owns the physical object; Creator/Author owns the copyright (Sec. 178.4). Unless there is a written stipulation to the contrary.
Employee Invention (Outside Regular Duties) Employee owns the patent, even if using employer's time, facilities, and materials (Sec. 30.2[a]). Unless otherwise agreed by the parties.

Professor's Illustration

Application Example (Employee Invention): A court stenographer employed by the Supreme Court uses her official office hours, court computer, and government electricity to tinker with hardware and invents a novel mechanical keyboard switch. Who owns the patent? The stenographer. Under Section 30.2(a) of the IP Code, because her regular duty is to transcribe stenographic notes and not to invent computer hardware, the patent belongs to her even though she used the time, facilities, and materials of her employer.

Right of Priority (Sec. 31, IP Code)

Under Section 31 of the IP Code, an application for a patent filed by any person who has previously applied for the same invention in another country which by treaty, convention, or law affords similar privileges to Filipino citizens, shall be considered as filed as of the date of filing the foreign application, provided the following three (3) statutory requisites are complied with:

  1. The local application expressly claims priority;
  2. The local application is filed within twelve (12) months from the date the earliest foreign application was filed; and
  3. A certified copy of the foreign application together with an English translation is filed within six (6) months from the date of filing in the Philippines.

IX. CANCELLATION OF PATENTS, RIGHTS CONFERRED, AND STATUTORY LIMITATIONS ON PATENT RIGHTS

Grounds for Cancellation of a Patent (Sec. 61, IP Code)

Under Section 61.1 of the IP Code, any interested person may, upon payment of the required fee, petition to cancel the patent or any claim thereof, or parts of the claim, on any of the following three (3) exclusive statutory grounds:

🚫
1. Not New or Patentable
Fails Sec. 21 requisites or falls under Sec. 22 exclusions
📄
2. Insufficient Disclosure
Not sufficiently clear & complete for a person skilled in the art
⚖️
3. Public Order / Morality
Patent is contrary to public order or morality
  1. Lack of Novelty or Patentability: That what is claimed as the invention is not new or Patentable (i.e., it fails to meet the requisites of Section 21 or falls under the non-patentable exclusions of Section 22);
  2. Insufficient Disclosure (Lack of Enablement): That the patent does not disclose the invention in a manner sufficiently clear and complete for it to be carried out by any person skilled in the art; or
  3. Public Order or Morality: That the patent is contrary to public order or morality.

Where the grounds for cancellation relate to some of the claims or parts of a claim, cancellation may be effected to such extent only (Sec. 61.2, IP Code).

Rights Conferred by a Patent (Sec. 71, IP Code) and Term of Patent (Sec. 54, IP Code)

  • Term of Patent (Sec. 54, IP Code): The term of a patent shall be twenty (20) years from the filing date of the application, strictly without possibility of extension.
  • Exclusive Rights Over a Product vs. a Process (Sec. 71.1, IP Code): A patent confers on its owner the following exclusive negative rights:
    1. Where the subject matter of a patent is a Product: The right to restrain, prohibit, and prevent any unauthorized person or entity from making, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing that product.
    2. Where the subject matter of a patent is a Process: The right to restrain, prevent, or prohibit any unauthorized person or entity from using the process, and from manufacturing, dealing in, using, selling, or offering for sale, or importing any product obtained directly or indirectly from such process.
  • Right to Assign and License (Sec. 71.2, IP Code): Patent owners shall also have the right to assign, or transfer by succession the patent, and to conclude licensing contracts for the same.

Statutory Limitations on Patent Rights (Sec. 72, IP Code)

Under Section 72 of the IP Code (as amended by R.A. No. 9502), the owner of a patent has no right to prevent third parties from performing acts in the following circumstances:

  1. Doctrine of Exhaustion / First Sale Doctrine (Sec. 72.1): Using a patented product which has been put on the market in the Philippines by the owner of the product, or with his express consent, insofar as such use is performed after that product has been so put on the said market. Special Rule for Drugs and Medicines: Under R.A. No. 9502, the limitation on patent rights applies to drugs and medicines after they have been introduced anywhere in the world by the patent owner or by any party authorized to use the invention (International Exhaustion / Parallel Importation).
  2. Private and Non-Commercial Use (Sec. 72.2): Where the act is done privately and on a non-commercial scale or for a non-commercial purpose, provided that it does not significantly prejudice the economic interests of the owner of the patent.
  3. Experimental Use Exception (Sec. 72.3): Where the act consists of making or using exclusively for experimental use of the invention for scientific or educational purposes, and such other activities directly related to such scientific or educational experimental use.
  4. Bolar Provision / Regulatory Approval Exception for Drugs (Sec. 72.4, added by R.A. No. 9502): In the case of drugs and medicines, where the act includes testing, using, making, or selling the invention solely for purposes reasonably related to the development and submission of information required under laws regulating the manufacture, sale, or use of drugs and medicines.
  5. Individual Pharmacy / Medical Prescription Exception (Sec. 72.5): Where the act consists of the preparation for individual cases, in a pharmacy or by a medical professional, of a medicine in accordance with a medical prescription or acts concerning the medicine so prepared.
  6. Foreign Vessels, Aircraft, or Land Vehicles in Transit (Sec. 72.6): Where the invention is used in any ship, vessel, aircraft, or land vehicle of any other country entering the territory of the Philippines temporarily or accidentally, provided that such invention is used exclusively for the needs of the ship, vessel, aircraft, or land vehicle and not used for the manufacturing of anything to be sold within the Philippines.

Doctrine of Prior User in Good Faith (Sec. 73, IP Code)

Under Section 73.1 of the IP Code, any prior user, who, in good faith was using the invention or has undertaken serious preparations to use the invention in his enterprise or business, before the filing date or priority date of the application on which a patent is granted, shall have the right to continue the use thereof as envisaged in such preparations within the territory where the patent produces its effect.

  • Limitation on Transfer of Prior User Right (Sec. 73.2, IP Code): The right of the prior user may only be transferred or assigned together with his enterprise or business, or with that part of his enterprise or business in which the use or preparations for use have been made.

Use of Invention by Government (Sec. 74, IP Code)

Under Section 74.1 of the IP Code (as amended by R.A. No. 9502), a Government agency or third person authorized by the Government may exploit the invention even without agreement of the patent owner where:

  1. The public interest, in particular, national security, nutrition, health, or the development of other sectors, as determined by the appropriate agency of the government, so requires; or
  2. A judicial or administrative body has determined that the manner of exploitation, by the owner of the patent or his licensee, is anti-competitive; or
  3. In the case of drugs and medicines, there is a national emergency or other circumstance of extreme urgency, or there is public non-commercial use, or the demand for the patented drug is not being met to an adequate extent and on reasonable terms.

X. PATENT INFRINGEMENT: TESTS, CONTRIBUTORY LIABILITY, REMEDIES, AND DEFENSES

Concept of Patent Infringement (Sec. 76.1, IP Code)

Under Section 76.1 of the IP Code, the making, using, offering for sale, selling, or importing a patented product or a product obtained directly or indirectly from a patented process, or the use of a patented process without the authorization of the patentee constitutes patent infringement.

Two Tests of Patent Infringement

To determine whether an accused device or process infringes upon an existing patent, Philippine courts employ two complementary tests:

  1. Literal Infringement Test: Resort must be had, in the first instance, to the words of the claim. If the accused matter clearly falls within the claim—meaning there is exact identity of the specifications, claims, and material elements between the patented invention and the accused product through a juxtaposition of the two—then literal infringement exists and that is the end of the inquiry.
  2. Doctrine of Equivalents (Function-Means-Result Test): Recognizing that unscrupulous manufacturers often make minor, insubstantial alterations to a patented product to evade the literal wording of a patent claim, the Doctrine of Equivalents provides that an infringement also occurs when a device appropriates a prior invention by incorporating its innovative concept and, albeit with some modification and change, performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result.
Parameter Literal Infringement Test Doctrine of Equivalents
Primary Focus Exact textual wording of the patent claims and specifications juxtaposed with the accused device. Substantial identity of the underlying innovative concept despite minor structural or material variations.
Standard Applied Exact identity of material elements, structure, and specifications. Function-Means-Result Test: Performs substantially the same function, in substantially the same way (means), to achieve substantially the same result.
Purpose Protects the clear literal boundaries of the patent grant. Prevents fraud on a patent through cosmetic or insubstantial modifications by copycats.

Smith Kline Beckman Corporation v. Court of Appeals, G.R. No. 126627 (2003)

  • Facts: Smith Kline held a Philippine patent over the compound methyl 5 propylthio-2-benzimidazole carbamate (Albendazole) used to combat gastrointestinal parasites in livestock, and sued Tryco Pharma for patent infringement for selling "Imidazole," which contained Albendazole. The lower courts absolved Tryco, leading Smith Kline to invoke the Doctrine of Equivalents.
  • Issue: Did Smith Kline establish patent infringement under the Doctrine of Equivalents against Tryco Pharma?
  • Ruling / Ratio: No, the Doctrine of Equivalents requires the patentee to prove with concrete evidence that the accused product performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve the same result. While both compounds fought parasites, Smith Kline failed to adduce scientific proof showing that the two compounds operated in substantially the same manner or means. Without satisfying all three prongs of the Function-Means-Result test, infringement cannot be sustained.

Contributory Infringement (Sec. 76.6, IP Code)

Liability for patent infringement is not confined to the direct infringer. Under Section 76.6 of the IP Code, anyone who actively induces the infringement of a patent or provides the infringer with a component of a patented product or of a product produced because of a patented process, knowing it to be especially adopted for infringing the patented invention and not suitable for substantial non-infringing use, shall be liable as a contributory infringer and shall be jointly and severally (solidarily) liable with the infringer.

Remedies of the Patent Owner (Secs. 76 & 84, IP Code)

  1. Civil Action for Infringement (Sec. 76.2–76.4, IP Code): The patentee, or anyone possessing any right, title, or interest in and to the patented invention (including a foreign national or juridical entity satisfying reciprocity under Sec. 77, even if not licensed to do business in the Philippines), may bring a civil action before a Regional Trial Court designated as a Special Commercial Court to recover:
    • Actual Damages sustained, plus attorney's fees and other expenses of litigation;
    • Reasonable Royalty: If damages are inadequate or cannot be readily ascertained with reasonable certainty, the court may award by way of damages a sum equivalent to reasonable royalty (Sec. 76.3);
    • Treble Damages: The court may, according to the circumstances of the case, award damages in a sum above the amount found as actual damages sustained, provided that the award does not exceed three (3) times the amount of such actual damages (Sec. 76.4).
  2. Provisional Remedies: Injunction and Destruction of Infringing Goods (Secs. 76.2 & 76.5, IP Code):
    • The patentee may secure a preliminary or final injunction to restrain the continuation of infringing acts; and
    • The court may, in its discretion, order that the infringing goods, materials, and implements predominantly used in the infringement be disposed of outside the channels of commerce or destroyed, without compensation (Sec. 76.5).
  3. Criminal Action for Repetition of Infringement (Sec. 84, IP Code): Unlike trademark or copyright infringement where a criminal action can be filed at the first instance, a criminal action for patent infringement cannot be instituted for a first-time offense. Under Section 84, criminal liability attaches only if the infringement is repeated by the infringer or by anyone in connivance with him after finality of the judgment of the court against the infringer in the prior civil action.
    • Penalty: Imprisonment for a period of not less than six (6) months but not more than three (3) years and/or a fine of not less than PhP 100,000.00 but not more than PhP 300,000.00, at the discretion of the court.
    • Prescription of Criminal Action: The criminal action shall prescribe in three (3) years from the date of the commission of the crime.

Prescriptive Period for Damages and Notice Requirement (Secs. 79–80, IP Code)

  • Four-Year Limitation on Recovery of Damages (Sec. 79, IP Code): No damages can be recovered for acts of infringement committed more than four (4) years before the institution of the action for infringement.
  • Requirement of Notice / Constructive Notice (Sec. 80, IP Code): Damages cannot be recovered for acts of infringement committed before the infringer had known, or had reasonable grounds to know of the patent. It is conclusively presumed that the infringer had known of the patent if on the patented product, or on the container or package in which the article is supplied to the public, or on the advertising material relating to the patented product or process, are placed the words "Philippine Patent" with the number of the patent.

Defenses in an Action for Infringement (Sec. 81, IP Code)

In an action for patent infringement, the defendant, in addition to other defenses available to him (such as non-infringement, prior user in good faith, exhaustion, or four-year prescription), may show the invalidity of the patent, or any claim thereof, on any of the grounds on which a petition for cancellation can be brought under Section 61:

  1. That what is claimed as the invention is not new or patentable;
  2. That the specification does not disclose the invention in a manner sufficiently clear and complete for it to be carried out by any person skilled in the art;
  3. That the patent is contrary to public order or morality; or
  4. That the patent was issued not to the True and Actual Inventor (TAI) or that the plaintiff derives no valid rights from the true and actual inventor.

Voluntary Licensing / Technology Transfer Arrangements (Secs. 85–92, IP Code)

Because an individual inventor often lacks the capital and factory infrastructure to mass-produce an invention, the commercial model of patent exploitation relies on Voluntary Licensing, governed by Chapter IX of the IP Code on Technology Transfer Arrangements (TTAs). A voluntary license is a contract wherein the patent holder (licensor) authorizes a manufacturer or third party (licensee) to produce and commercialize the patented invention in exchange for royalties, subject to quality standards and defined market territories.

Prohibited Clauses in Technology Transfer Arrangements (Sec. 87, IP Code)

To prevent restraint of trade and abuse of patent monopoly, Section 87 of the IP Code enumerates Prohibited Clauses that are prima facie deemed to have an adverse effect on competition and trade. Their inclusion renders the TTA unenforceable unless granted an exceptional exemption under Section 91. Key prohibited stipulations include:

  1. Tie-In Clauses and Forced Employment (Sec. 87.1): Those which impose upon the licensee the obligation to acquire from a specific source capital goods, intermediate products, raw materials, and other technologies, or of permanently employing personnel indicated by the licensor;
  2. Resale Price Maintenance / Price-Fixing (Sec. 87.2): Those pursuant to which the licensor reserves the right to fix the sale or resale prices of the products manufactured on the basis of the license; and
  3. Production Volume and Structure Restrictions (Sec. 87.3): Those that contain restrictions regarding the volume and structure of production.

Compulsory Licensing (Secs. 93–102, IP Code)

Compulsory Licensing occurs when the State (through the Director of Legal Affairs of the IPOPHL, or the Director General in special cases) grants a license to a third person or government agency to exploit a patented invention even without the agreement or consent of the patent owner, subject to the payment of adequate remuneration to the patentee.

Six Statutory Grounds for Compulsory Licensing (Sec. 93, IP Code, as amended by R.A. No. 9502)
  1. National Emergency or Extreme Urgency (Sec. 93.1): National emergency or other circumstances of extreme urgency;
  2. Public Interest (Sec. 93.2): Where the public interest, in particular, national security, nutrition, health, or the development of other vital sectors of the national economy as determined by the appropriate agency of the Government, so requires;
  3. Anti-Competitive Exploitation (Sec. 93.3): Where a judicial or administrative body has determined that the manner of exploitation by the owner of the patent or his licensee is anti-competitive (e.g., monopolistic price-gouging or artificial market restriction);
  4. Public Non-Commercial Use (Sec. 93.4): In case of public non-commercial use of the patent by the patentee, without satisfactory reason;
  5. Failure to Work the Patent on a Commercial Scale in the Philippines (Sec. 93.5): If the patented invention is not being worked in the Philippines on a commercial scale, although capable of being worked, without satisfactory reason; Provided, That the importation of the patented article shall constitute working or using the patent; and
  6. Unmet Demand for Patented Drugs and Medicines (Sec. 93.6, added by R.A. No. 9502): Where the demand for the patented drug or medicine in the Philippines is not being met to an adequate extent and on reasonable terms, as determined by the Secretary of the Department of Health.
Period for Filing a Petition for Compulsory License (Sec. 94, IP Code)
  • For Failure to Work on a Commercial Scale (Sec. 94.1): A compulsory license may not be applied for on the ground of Section 93.5 (non-working in the Philippines) before the expiration of a period of four (4) years from the date of filing of the application or three (3) years from the date of the patent, whichever period expires last.
  • For All Other Grounds (Sec. 94.2): A compulsory license applied for on any of the grounds stated in Subsections 93.1, 93.2, 93.3, 93.4, and 93.6 may be applied for at any time after the grant of the patent.

Assignment and Transmission of Rights (Secs. 103–106, IP Code)

  • Scope of Assignment (Sec. 104, IP Code): An assignment may be of the entire right, title, or interest in and to the patent and the invention covered thereby, or of an undivided share of the entire patent and invention, in which event the parties become joint owners thereof. An assignment may be limited to a specified territory.
  • Form of Assignment (Sec. 105, IP Code): The assignment must be in writing, acknowledged before a notary public or other officer authorized to administer oaths or perform notarial acts, and certified under the hand and official seal of such notary or other officer.
  • Recording with the IPOPHL (Sec. 106, IP Code): An assignment or transfer shall be void as against any subsequent purchaser or mortgagee for valuable consideration and without notice, unless it is recorded in the IPOPHL within three (3) months from the date of said instrument, or prior to the subsequent purchase or mortgage.

  • Professor's Practical Commentary & AFASA Case Study (BDO Smishing Scam):
    • Real-World Smishing Attack: In relation to the Anti-Financial Account Scamming Act (AFASA), the Professor shared a personal experience involving a fraudulent link injected directly into the legitimate official SMS text thread of BDO offering a one-time credit limit increase. Clicking the link opened a cloned website where Chrome's browser auto-fill inadvertently populated saved credit card details and intercepted/auto-filled the One-Time Password (OTP), allowing an unauthorized transaction to push through before the card was blocked.
    • Banking Dispute Pitfalls: Banks refuse to tag or process a fraud dispute while an unauthorized transaction is still "floating" (pending posting), requiring the cardholder to wait until the charge is formally posted—and often demanding interim payment while the BSP/bank investigation is pending. Cardholders should immediately contact the merchant directly to cancel the unauthorized order before fulfillment, disable browser credit card auto-fill, and never click links embedded even within official bank SMS threads.
    • IP Litigation Practice Tip: Patent litigation is virtually non-existent in provincial courts and is heavily concentrated in Metro Manila Special Commercial Courts, whereas provincial commercial dockets more commonly encounter cybercrime, trademark counterfeiting, or financial scam disputes.
