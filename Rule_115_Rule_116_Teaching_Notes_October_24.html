I. GENERAL FRAMEWORK OF THE RIGHTS OF THE ACCUSED (RULE 115)

Why does criminal procedure protect someone accused of a crime?

Imagine that a neighbor is arrested after a stabbing. The community believes the police caught the right person, but a criminal trial is different from public suspicion. The government must use lawful procedures, allow the accused to challenge its proof, and establish criminal liability beyond reasonable doubt. Rule 115 supplies the procedural safeguards that make the State prove its accusation rather than merely announce it. It implements guarantees in Article III of the 1987 Constitution, especially due process, the rights of persons under custodial investigation, the presumption of innocence, speedy trial, and freedom from compelled self-incrimination.

The rights operate at different stages. During custodial investigation, Article III, Section 12 governs police questioning of a suspect. During criminal prosecution and trial, Rule 115 governs such rights as presence, counsel, confrontation, compulsory process, and appeal. Rule 116 then describes the formal step—arraignment—at which the accused must be told the charge and enter a plea. One chapter leads naturally to the next: rights are the guarantees; arraignment is the procedure that begins their practical enforcement in court.

For recitation, learn the accurate Rule 115, Section 1 sequence:

Are these rights lost when counsel fails to object?

Professor's Illustration: An accused is released on bail. Arraignment is postponed repeatedly, while defense counsel stays silent because the accused is enjoying liberty. More than a year later the defense complains of delay. The lecture warns that inaction may seriously weaken a speedy-trial objection. Counsel should place timely objections and the reason for delay on the record.

That warning is excellent courtroom practice, but not every right is automatically waived by silence. A waiver must be evaluated according to the nature of the right and the circumstances. In particular, the constitutional speedy-trial inquiry is not an inflexible “no earlier objection = permanent waiver” rule. Courts examine such considerations as length of delay, reasons for delay, assertion of the right, and prejudice. An accused's acquiescence matters, but does not replace the full analysis. [Supreme Court discussion of the balancing test].

Memory distinction: Assert a right promptly as a litigation habit; analyze whether it has been waived as a legal question. Those are related propositions, not identical ones.

II. PRESUMPTION OF INNOCENCE, PROOF BEYOND REASONABLE DOUBT, AND EQUIPOISE

Who must prove what?

Pedro is charged with murder. He can remain silent and present no witnesses. The prosecution still cannot win by saying, “Pedro failed to explain where he was.” Under Rule 115, Section 1(a) and Article III, Section 14(2), the accused is presumed innocent until the contrary is established beyond reasonable doubt. The prosecution bears the burden of proof for the elements of the offense and for identifying Pedro as its author. That burden is not transferred merely because the defense appears weak.

Proof beyond reasonable doubt does not mean mathematical certainty or exclusion of every fantastic possibility. It is a degree of proof producing moral certainty—the conscientious conviction of an impartial mind after considering the evidence. In a theft case, for instance, proving that property went missing is insufficient if no reliable evidence links the accused to unlawful taking with intent to gain. The State cannot fill the gap by attacking the accused's personality or pointing out that his alibi is unpersuasive.

What is the equipoise rule?

Suppose an eyewitness saw someone near a dark alley and believes it was Pedro. The witness had only a brief view. Pedro submits immigration records showing he was abroad, and the documents are authenticated. You should not simply count one eyewitness against several documents. Ask instead whether, after weighing their quality, the total evidence generates reasonable doubt about identity.

The equipoise doctrine says that when inculpatory and exculpatory evidence is evenly balanced, or proved circumstances reasonably support both guilt and innocence, the balance must be resolved in favor of the accused. This is a consequence of the prosecution's continuing burden, not a device by which the accused becomes required to prove innocence.

Professor's Illustration: The notes describe a nighttime shooting, a prosecution eyewitness, a firearm registered to the accused, and the defense's proof of foreign travel. Registration may connect a person to a gun but does not necessarily prove who fired it. If genuine travel records make the eyewitness identification unreliable and prosecution proof fails to restore moral certainty, acquittal follows. If the travel records are forged, the assessment may change. The result turns on the evidence's reliability, not its number.

Is alibi always useless?

The lecture calls denial and alibi weak defenses because they are easy to assert and often difficult to verify. Properly understood, alibi requires credible proof that the accused was somewhere else and that being at the scene at the relevant time was physically impossible, not merely inconvenient. An accused claiming he was in Bauang while the crime occurred in San Fernando City might still have been able to travel there. Authenticated proof that he was abroad at the precise time is considerably stronger.

Do not say that an alibi shifts the burden of proof to the defense. The accused bears the practical burden of producing supporting evidence for an affirmative factual assertion, but the prosecution's ultimate burden of establishing guilt beyond reasonable doubt remains. A credible defense can create doubt even before it becomes conclusive proof of an alternative account.

Does conviction by the RTC end the presumption of innocence?

The uploaded notes emphasize that a conviction pending appeal is not yet a final conviction. For purposes of appellate review of guilt, the Supreme Court has said that the accused's presumption of innocence continues while the conviction remains nonfinal; the reviewing court independently evaluates proof beyond reasonable doubt. Cuico v. People, Supreme Court E-Library.

Important qualification: For bail pending appeal, Leviste v. Court of Appeals speaks of the trial court conviction as ending the accused's constitutional right to bail as a matter of right; bail then becomes discretionary, subject to Rule 114, Section 5. This does not mean that an RTC judgment is automatically immune from appellate reversal. The apparent tension is resolved by identifying which legal consequence is being discussed: the merits review of guilt or the status of bail after conviction. Leviste, G.R. No. 189122.

Recitation takeaway: A pending appeal preserves scrutiny of guilt beyond reasonable doubt, but an RTC conviction changes the legal standard for bail.

III. POSITIVE IDENTIFICATION AND THE CORPUS DELICTI

Two separate questions the prosecutor must answer

A corpse is discovered with a fatal gunshot wound. A medical expert can establish a criminal killing occurred. Does that prove Pedro killed the victim? No. Separate the fact that the crime occurred from the accused's identity as its perpetrator. The former is commonly called corpus delicti, literally the “body of the crime”; it does not necessarily mean the victim's literal body. The second requires reliable identification, whether through witnesses, physical proof, or a coherent chain of circumstantial evidence.

The prosecution must satisfactorily establish both. A perfect autopsy cannot cure a mistaken identification. Conversely, a witness who recognizes the accused cannot supply missing proof that an actual offense was committed. In drug prosecutions, this separation is particularly instructive because the identity and integrity of the seized dangerous drug are central to proving the offense.

Pre-trial stipulation: “the person arraigned” is not “the criminal”

Professor's Courtroom Observation: At pre-trial, a prosecutor asks defense counsel to admit the “identity of the accused.” A careful defense response might be: “We admit only that this is the person named in the Information and arraigned before this court.” This avoids confusing procedural identity with an admission that the accused committed the crime. Any actual admission of facts at pre-trial must also comply with Rule 118, Section 2, including reduction to writing and signatures of the accused and counsel before it can be used against the accused.

Why does this matter? An accused may later jump bail. If lawfully arraigned and properly notified, trial may proceed in absentia. An eyewitness cannot literally point to someone who is absent, but the prosecutor can attempt to prove identity through photographs, prior identification, testimony, and other admissible evidence. The absence of an in-court pointing gesture does not automatically require acquittal. The prosecution still bears the same burden of proving the defendant is the perpetrator.

Three out-of-court identification methods

Police may bring one suspect alone before a witness (show-up), present photographs (mug shots), or present the suspect with several people (line-up). All can be useful, but they carry different risks of suggestion. A witness shown only one handcuffed man surrounded by police might feel that this is the answer investigators expect. A lineup may reduce that danger, but is not necessarily reliable if every other participant looks entirely different.

Courts assess identification using the totality of circumstances test, considering six factors: (1) opportunity to view the offender; (2) degree of attention; (3) accuracy of an earlier description; (4) certainty expressed at identification; (5) elapsed time between incident and identification; and (6) suggestiveness of the police procedure. Cariño v. People, Supreme Court E-Library.

Application Example: The witness saw the robber's face for five minutes in a well-lit store, gave a detailed description immediately, and selected the suspect from a fair lineup the next morning. That identification is stronger than one obtained months later after police repeatedly displayed a single photograph. Common confusion: Confidence alone is one factor, not conclusive proof of accuracy.

Drug seizures and the chain of custody

Section 21 of R.A. No. 9165, as amended by R.A. No. 10640, protects against substitution or tampering. When authorities seize suspected shabu, the prosecution must connect the item seized to the item examined and the item produced in court through credible custodial links, including required marking, inventory, photographing, turnover, and presentation. The amended law requires specified witnesses in the inventory and photographing: an elected public official and a representative of the National Prosecution Service or the media, alongside the accused or a qualifying representative subject to the applicable statutory formulation and circumstances.

Professor's Illustration: In a recounted case, a forensic chemist allegedly presented a sachet from which the shabu had “evaporated.” The legal point is unmistakable: the physical exhibit and its recorded handling must make sense. A supposed drug exhibit that cannot be accounted for casts serious doubt on the identity and integrity of the corpus delicti. Treat the professor's anecdote as classroom illustration, not an independently verified report of the specific court's judgment.

Legal qualification: A defect in Section 21 procedure does not mechanically decide every case. The statutory saving clause may apply if the prosecution proves justifiable grounds for noncompliance and that the seized items' integrity and evidentiary value were properly preserved. An unsubstantiated excuse that required witnesses “were unavailable” is not enough. The question is whether the evidentiary chain remains dependable and the departure justified.

IV. THE RIGHT TO BE INFORMED OF THE NATURE AND CAUSE OF THE ACCUSATION

A charge must be understandable enough to answer

A person cannot prepare a defense against an accusation he does not understand. Rule 115, Section 1(b) and Article III, Section 14(2) protect the right to know what act is charged and why that act allegedly constitutes an offense. Rule 110, Sections 8–11, together with its other pleading requirements, call for an Information that identifies the offense and describes the acts or omissions in ordinary, concise language. Legal nomenclature alone is not a substitute for alleging facts constituting the elements.

Professor's Illustration: A person understands Ilocano but not English. At arraignment, the judge or clerk must ensure the Information is read and explained in a language or dialect known to him, and then ask for his plea. The procedural formality is not satisfied simply by handing over a document full of legal English. If the accused has a hearing or communication disability, the court must provide suitable interpreting assistance so that understanding and a meaningful plea are possible.

Cases involving minor victims require an additional sensitivity. The professor highlights confidentiality and identity protection in Family Court proceedings. The goal is to preserve the accused's right to know the allegation while shielding children from unnecessary exposure, trauma, and identification in public records. The default right to a public trial is subject to lawful protections; not every Family Court hearing is automatically a closed hearing without a court-specific legal basis.

Part 1 memory anchor: The State must prove a crime occurred, prove who committed it, and tell that person what charge must be answered. Those are different safeguards, all necessary to a fair criminal process.

V. THE RIGHT TO COMPETENT AND INDEPENDENT COUNSEL

Counsel must protect, not merely accompany

Police arrest a suspect without a warrant. An officer asks him to sign a written confession while a lawyer sits nearby scrolling through a phone. Is the presence of someone with a law license enough? No. The constitutional promise is meaningful legal assistance, not a signature witnessing exercise. Under Article III, Section 12, a person under custodial investigation has the right to remain silent and to competent and independent counsel, preferably of his own choice, or counsel provided when he cannot afford one. The suspect must be informed of these rights; a waiver of the specified custodial rights must satisfy the Constitution's strict requirements.

At trial, the right to defend in person and through counsel comes from Rule 115, Section 1(c). Do not mix these stages. The rule requiring counsel during custodial questioning responds to the powerful coercive setting of police interrogation. Trial counsel has a broader obligation to scrutinize witnesses, object when warranted, present lawful defenses, and explain the proceedings. Competence involves active assistance, preparation, independence from conflicting interests, and communication with the accused.

Inquest: what does effective advice look like?

Professor's Illustration: A warrantless arrestee is brought before the inquest prosecutor on a weekend. Instead of telling him “Sign immediately,” counsel must examine whether the arrest was lawful, explain the charge, consider the availability of bail, and explain the choice between prompt inquest disposition and requesting a regular preliminary investigation.

Under the applicable provision of Rule 112 on warrantless arrest, an arrested person entitled to preliminary investigation may request it before the Information is filed, subject to a waiver of Article 125 of the Revised Penal Code signed in counsel's presence. The investigation must be terminated within 15 days from its inception, and bail may still be sought. Once an Information was filed without preliminary investigation following an inquest, a timely request for preliminary investigation may be made within five days of learning of the filing. A waiver of Article 125 is not permission to detain someone indefinitely. In re Senin, G.R. No. 232413.

The lecture relates Article 125's 12-, 18-, and 36-hour periods, depending on the classification of the offense. This explains why prosecutors, PAO lawyers, and the courts arrange weekend or holiday duty coverage. The important principle is that detention must have a continuing legal basis; an inquest delay cannot be excused merely because ordinary government offices are closed.

Police lineups, extrajudicial statements, and spontaneous admissions

The professor distinguishes a standard police lineup, which seeks visual recognition, from custodial interrogation, which seeks testimonial statements. A lineup ordinarily does not require counsel merely because a witness views the suspect, but if police begin questioning designed to elicit incriminating answers, custodial-investigation safeguards become crucial. The label “lineup” does not insulate an actual interrogation.

A purported extrajudicial confession obtained during custodial questioning is subject to constitutional and statutory requirements, including voluntary execution and effective assistance of counsel; an uncounseled custodial admission cannot be salvaged merely by producing a written document. By contrast, the lecture describes a spontaneous statement to a barangay chairperson. The point is that an unsolicited admission to a person not acting as a law-enforcement interrogator may fall outside Article III, Section 12's custodial interrogation rule. Do not recite that all statements before barangay officials are automatically admissible: capacity, voluntariness, agency, and evidentiary rules matter.

Professor's Courtroom Observation: Counsel who deliberately refuses to defend a client because counsel personally believes the client guilty defeats the purpose of representation. The source recounts a conviction set aside and a lawyer suspended for six months. Because the case name is not supplied, retain the duty to render an actual defense without memorizing an unverified case citation or assuming every tactical decision not to present evidence proves incompetence.

Common confusion: A competent defense does not mean inventing testimony, suppressing truth, or promising acquittal. It means testing the State's proof and presenting the client's lawful case with diligence.

VI. SPEEDY TRIAL AND THE CONTINUOUS TRIAL GUIDELINES

Speedy trial is about unjustified delay, not instant judgment

Suppose the prosecutor fails to produce a witness over four hearing settings. The accused remains under the strain of a pending criminal charge, possibly while detained. Article III, Section 14(2) and Rule 115, Section 1(h) protect the accused's speedy, impartial, public trial. Article III, Section 16 separately protects speedy disposition of cases before judicial, quasi-judicial, and administrative bodies. The rights overlap, but their coverage is not identical.

The constitutional analysis asks whether the delay is vexatious, capricious, or oppressive in the circumstances. Consider its duration, cause, the accused's assertion of the right, and any prejudice. A postponement prompted by a typhoon or a legitimately unavailable witness is different from months of prosecutorial indifference. Neither the mere passage of time nor a specific number of resets alone invariably proves a constitutional violation.

The professor's continuous-trial calendar

The Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial of Criminal Cases (A.M. No. 15-06-10-SC) seek to make procedural stages prompt and consecutive. The lecture stresses that detained accused ordinarily require arraignment within 10 calendar days of the relevant receipt/raffle period, while nondetained accused are generally set within 30 calendar days from acquisition of jurisdiction over the person, subject to shorter special rules. The guidelines also arrange arraignment and pre-trial on the same setting. [Official Continuous Trial Guidelines].

Why postponements receive strict scrutiny

Professor's Illustration: A lawyer asks to postpone trial to celebrate a birthday, and another requests postponement because the region has been struck by a major typhoon. The professor contrasts an ordinary personal preference with a genuine impediment. The Guidelines generally prohibit postponement motions except for acts of God, force majeure, or physical inability of the witness to appear and testify. A granted exception does not give counsel unlimited additional time; the trial calendar is designed to remain continuous. The Guidelines also address postponement fees, so a practitioner must check the applicable procedure instead of assuming any flat peso amount remains universal.

The source lists matters such as judges' illness, additional court assignments, absent branch staff, and delayed transcripts as insufficient automatic excuses for overdue judgments. A judicial officer who genuinely cannot meet a decision deadline should pursue the authorized extension procedure, not silently allow the deadline to expire. This is the judicial-accountability side of the accused's right: the court has obligations independent of the litigants' preferences.

When should defense counsel object?

Professor's courtroom strategy: The first absent-witness request may be granted as a professional courtesy. A second may be noted. By the third, defense counsel warns that another unjustified postponement will be opposed. On the fourth, counsel objects and asks the court to enforce the speedy-trial guarantee. This four-setting sequence is a litigation illustration, not a statutory four-strikes formula. The legal assessment still depends on the total record and relevant time limits.

If the right has truly been violated, dismissal on speedy-trial grounds may operate as an acquittal for double-jeopardy purposes where the prerequisites are met. But do not answer every delay problem with “dismissal and automatic double jeopardy.” First ask whether the delay is chargeable to the State, whether it is justified or excluded, whether dismissal is legally warranted, and whether first jeopardy had attached. [Rules 119 and 117, Supreme Court E-Library].

VII. THE PRIVILEGE AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION

The core distinction: testimony versus the body as evidence

Police can obtain an accused's DNA through lawful procedures, but cannot threaten him until he narrates how he committed the crime. Why? Article III, Section 17 and Rule 115, Section 1(e) protect against compelled testimonial or communicative self-incrimination. The State generally cannot force a person to supply evidence through his own testimonial assertions, thoughts, or admissions. Physical or mechanical examination, in contrast, may be constitutionally permissible subject to other safeguards such as lawful search, privacy, bodily integrity, and evidentiary relevance.

The Latin maxim is nemo tenetur seipsum accusare: no person is bound to accuse himself. The guarantee protects a person from the cruel choice of incriminating himself, disobeying official compulsion, or lying under oath. It is a personal privilege of natural persons, not a general corporate right to withhold all business records on the corporation's own self-incrimination claim.

Legal clarification: The lecture's handwriting distinction has support in Beltran v. Samson. The Supreme Court later distinguished uses of a signature while recognizing the possible inadmissibility of signatures obtained from arrested suspects without counsel. The result turns on what the compelled act communicates and the circumstances, not merely the involvement of a pen. People v. Olvis, G.R. No. 109242.

Accused as witness versus ordinary witness

The accused cannot be compelled by the prosecution to take the stand and explain his innocence. Under Section 1(d), he may voluntarily testify for his own defense. If he does, he becomes subject to cross-examination on matters covered by direct examination; the privilege has not vanished for every conceivable unrelated subject.

An ordinary witness generally must attend when lawfully subpoenaed. That witness ordinarily claims the privilege when a specific question calls for an incriminating answer. This distinction also explains why the privilege is relevant beyond criminal trials—in legislative inquiries, civil cases, or administrative proceedings where an answer could expose the person to criminal liability.

The professor also contrasts transactional immunity, which bars prosecution concerning the covered transaction, with use and derivative-use immunity, which prevents the State from using compelled testimony and its fruits but may allow prosecution on truly independent proof. The type and breadth of immunity depend on the governing statute, not a generic promise made by an investigating official.

VIII. PRESENCE, DEFENSE, CONFRONTATION, COMPULSORY PROCESS, AND APPEAL

What happens when the accused disappears after arraignment?

Under Article III, Section 14(2), trial in absentia may proceed when (1) arraignment was valid, (2) the accused was duly notified, and (3) absence is unjustified. Imagine a man who entered a not-guilty plea and then vanished while out on bail. His flight ordinarily waives personal attendance, not his right to counsel or the requirement that the prosecution prove the charge.

Professor's Illustration: After the prosecution rests, counsel cannot contact the fugitive to obtain defense witnesses. Counsel may be forced to tell the court there is no evidence available for the defense and submit the case on the record. But this is not an automatic duty to waive the defense whenever an accused absconds. Counsel should first examine available evidence, seek permissible instructions or relief, and continue to cross-examine witnesses; strategic abandonment without reason would raise the competence concerns discussed earlier.

Three distinct trial protections

Confrontation (Section 1[f]) permits testing adverse testimony by cross-examination. The rule permits certain former testimony of a deceased or otherwise unavailable witness from another proceeding involving the same parties and subject matter, where the adverse party had an opportunity to cross-examine. Mere death alone does not automatically admit every affidavit written by that person.

Compulsory process (Section 1[g]) allows the defense to request subpoena ad testificandum for witnesses and subpoena duces tecum for specified documents or evidence. It does not allow irrelevant fishing expeditions; the request must satisfy the court's lawful standards. Appeal (Section 1[i]) is a statutory right, not an unrestricted constitutional entitlement to endless review. A timely, properly perfected appeal may open a criminal conviction to appellate scrutiny; noncompliance with procedural requirements may forfeit the remedy.

Part 2 memory anchor: Counsel makes defense meaningful; speedy trial prevents oppression; self-incrimination limits compelled evidence; confrontation, subpoenas, and appeal make the proceedings reviewable and fair.

IX. THE IMPORTANCE OF ARRAIGNMENT (RULE 116)

What happens at arraignment—and why can't it be skipped?

Imagine Mike is charged with a criminal offense. Police reports describe the alleged incident, and his lawyer has already read the Information. Mike has not yet been formally arraigned. Can the court simply start hearing prosecution witnesses? Generally, no. Arraignment is the formal judicial act of furnishing the accused a copy of the complaint or Information, reading it in a language or dialect known to him, and asking whether he pleads guilty or not guilty. It operationalizes the constitutional right to be informed of the accusation and formally joins the issue for trial. See Rule 116, Section 1(a)–(b).

Two purposes should be separated. First, arraignment informs the accused which factual criminal charge he must answer. Second, his plea determines the route of the case: not guilty ordinarily leads to trial; a legally sufficient guilty plea may allow judgment, subject to special safeguards. The presence of a lawyer who knows the charge does not substitute for the accused's own plea. Arraignment is not the same event as filing an Information or issuing a warrant.

The consequence of disappearing before arraignment

Professor's Illustration: An accused posts bail, receives a summons to arraignment, then disappears. The court cannot commence trial in absentia, because the constitution requires that the accused first be arraigned. The court can take lawful measures to secure attendance, including a warrant and proceedings concerning bail subject to Rule 114's forfeiture safeguards. If the person remains at large, the case may be archived according to applicable administrative guidelines and later revived upon apprehension or surrender.

Contrast the case of an accused who was arraigned, was duly notified of trial, and absents himself without justification. Trial in absentia can then proceed. The timing of the flight changes the result: before arraignment, no proper start to trial; after valid arraignment, unjustified absence generally does not obstruct trial.

Deadline and assistance of counsel

The lecture emphasizes the standard 10-day arraignment period for a detained accused and 30-day period for a nondetained accused, subject to governing rules, excluded periods, and special provisions. The Continuous Trial Guidelines generally schedule arraignment and pre-trial together, so counsel must be ready to address stipulations, exhibits, witnesses, and possible plea bargaining rather than prepare only for a two-minute reading of the Information.

Under Rule 116, Sections 6–8, the judge must inform the accused of the right to counsel, ask whether counsel is desired, and provide counsel de oficio if the accused has neither retained one nor been properly permitted to defend himself. Assigned counsel must be allowed reasonable time to confer about the plea. The professor's practical point is that a private counsel de parte already formally appearing may make repeated inquiries about obtaining another lawyer unnecessary. What matters is the existence of real and competent representation, not a ritualistic exchange.

Common confusion: A lawyer standing beside the accused cannot personally enter the plea for him. The accused must personally plead, whether in a proper courtroom setting or through a duly authorized videoconferencing procedure.

X. PRE-ARRAIGNMENT REMEDIES AND SUSPENSION

Why should counsel review the case before the plea?

Before arraignment, counsel must decide whether a problem is one of clarity, legal validity, or missing preliminary investigation. These questions call for different remedies. Filing the wrong motion, or waiting until after plea, can waive important objections. The professor repeatedly emphasizes that pre-arraignment preparation is not a formality.

Problem Appropriate route What it accomplishes
Information alleges an offense but facts are vague Bill of particulars, Rule 116, Sec. 9 Requests details needed to plead and prepare defense.
Information is legally defective under a Rule 117 ground Motion to quash before plea Tests a recognized defect, subject to amendment and waiver rules.
Lawful warrantless arrest followed by inquest without preliminary investigation Timely request for preliminary investigation under Rule 112 Permits statutory review and defense evidence when applicable.
Prosecution holds material evidence needed for preparation Production/inspection, Rule 116, Sec. 10 Requests court-authorized access to qualifying material.

Application Example: An Information says only that Pedro “committed fraud” without sufficient facts explaining the conduct. If it is vague but potentially sufficient, counsel may seek particulars. If the facts alleged, even assumed true, do not constitute an offense, the Rule 117 motion-to-quash ground may be appropriate. If Pedro was detained after a warrantless arrest and denied a preliminary investigation to which he was entitled, counsel should examine the five-day post-filing rule and other requirements. Those remedies are not interchangeable.

The lecture says that counsel should not move for a judicial redetermination of probable cause or to withhold issuance of a warrant simply to delay proceedings. The Continuous Trial Guidelines list a motion for judicial determination of probable cause among prohibited motions and restrict several forms of late or unauthorized preliminary-investigation or reinvestigation motions. Legal qualification: This is not an assertion that a judge's initial probable-cause determination is constitutionally unreviewable in every circumstance. Distinguish prohibited routine motions from an independently proper remedy for genuine jurisdictional or constitutional abuse.

Three grounds for suspending arraignment

Under Rule 116, Section 11, arraignment may be suspended upon proper motion for three reasons:

🧠
1. Unsound Mental Condition
Prevents understanding charge or pleading intelligently; court orders examination
⚖️
2. Prejudicial Question
Previously instituted civil action intimately determinative of criminal action
⏳
3. DOJ / OP Petition for Review
Pending appeal from prosecutor's resolution; suspension capped at 60 days
  1. Unsound mental condition that effectively prevents the accused from understanding the charge and intelligently pleading. The court orders mental examination and, where needed, appropriate confinement for that examination. This is a functional legal assessment, not an automatic finding based on unusual behavior or a fixed professional title for the examiner.
  2. Prejudicial question: a previously instituted civil action raises a determinative issue so intimately related to the subsequent criminal action that the civil resolution affects whether prosecution may proceed. The formal requisites are developed in Rule 111, Sections 6–7.
  3. A pending petition for review of the prosecutor's resolution with the Department of Justice or the Office of the President, with suspension not exceeding 60 days counted from filing the petition with the reviewing office.

Legal clarification: The uploaded notes identify the DOJ or Regional State Prosecutor as the third category. Rule 116, Section 11(c) expressly states DOJ or Office of the President. Use the latter formulation when reciting the Rule. [Supreme Court E-Library, Rule 116].

Professor's Illustration: An accused appears unable to comprehend what the charge means. Proceeding with the plea merely because the case is old risks meaningless arraignment. A medical or forensic examination may be necessary. Conversely, a pending petition for review does not allow indefinite postponement: the Rule imposes the 60-day ceiling. This is the distinction between legitimate delay protecting due process and open-ended delay defeating it.

XI. THE MECHANICS OF A VALID PLEA

Personal presence, translation, and a possible reading waiver

The judge or clerk must ensure the accused understands the complaint or Information. In multiple-count cases, such as numerous B.P. Blg. 22 charges, the Continuous Trial Guidelines permit a waiver of the reading of the Informations under safeguards: the accused must understand the consequence, consent expressly with counsel, and have the waiver properly documented after judicial examination. This is not a blanket power for counsel alone to waive the accused's constitutional notice.

The private offended party is ordinarily required to attend arraignment for plea bargaining, determination of civil liability, and other necessary matters under Section 1(f). If the offended party fails to appear despite due notice, the court may permit a plea to a necessarily included lesser offense with the trial prosecutor's conformity alone. This exception does not compel the prosecutor or court to approve every proposed compromise.

When the court itself enters “not guilty”

Consider three variants:

  1. The accused refuses to plead or remains silent. Under Section 1(c), the judge enters not guilty. Silence does not automatically amount to an admission.
  2. The accused says, “I plead guilty only if I receive probation.” This is a conditional plea; the court enters not guilty because guilt cannot depend on an unapproved sentencing bargain.
  3. The accused says, “I plead guilty, but I acted in lawful self-defense.” That assertion can negate unlawful criminal responsibility. Section 1(d) directs withdrawal of the guilty plea and entry of not guilty when exculpatory evidence accompanies the plea.

The third situation may warrant a modified or reverse order of trial because the defense admits the physical act but interposes a lawful justification; Rule 119, Section 11(e) allows modification. This is distinct from a voluntary, unequivocal confession of the offense.

How does arraignment relate to double jeopardy?

A valid complaint or Information, competent court, arraignment and valid plea, and a qualifying termination without the accused's consent are central to the double-jeopardy inquiry. A void or absent plea may prevent first jeopardy from attaching. However, do not conclude that every minor defect in documenting arraignment makes proceedings void. Rule 116, Section 1(b) expressly says failure to enter arraignment and plea of record does not itself affect validity. Distinguish no valid arraignment occurred from a valid arraignment occurred but the recording is defective.

Memory aid: Charge → Understand → Personally Plead → Trial or lawful guilty-plea procedure. An invalid shortcut at the plea stage can undermine the fairness of everything that follows.

XII. PLEA BARGAINING AND GUILTY PLEAS

Bargaining is constrained by the charge and the law

Plea bargaining, under Rule 116, Section 2, permits the accused—subject to required consent and court approval—to plead guilty to a lesser offense necessarily included in the charge. The prosecutor and offended party generally consent, and the court retains discretion. A bargain is not an entitlement just because it would save time or produce probation eligibility. Sayre v. Xenos.

Professor's illustrations: Murder → homicide is the classic illustration: removing a qualifying circumstance may leave the necessarily included killing offense. Robbery → theft can similarly be understandable when the additional robbery element is not established. By contrast, do not mechanically treat homicide → physical injuries, rape → unjust vexation, or a lower alleged property value as automatically lawful pleas. Necessary inclusion depends on the elements and specific allegations, and special statutory or Supreme Court rules may regulate particular offenses, especially drug cases.

Timing clarification: The source broadly says bargaining may occur any time before judgment. The text of Rule 116, Section 2 expressly provides for a plea at arraignment, or after arraignment but before trial after withdrawing an earlier not-guilty plea. Earlier cases have discussed offers after the prosecution rested, but that history must not be recited as though the present Rule unconditionally authorizes late bargaining. Ask whether an applicable special rule or controlling jurisprudence permits the particular proposal.

An amended Information is ordinarily unnecessary for an approved plea to a necessarily included lesser offense. Where a new plea is permitted after an earlier not-guilty plea, the prior plea is withdrawn and the changed plea is personally recorded with the court's approval. A voluntary plea of guilt as an ordinary mitigating circumstance under Article 13(7), RPC has separate requirements, including its timing and relation to the charge; do not automatically claim the mitigating circumstance merely because a lighter charge was negotiated.

Guilty to a capital offense: three mandatory judicial safeguards

Professor's Illustration: Three persons charged with murder admit guilt at arraignment. May the judge immediately convict them and dispense with all evidence? Under Rule 116, Section 3, no. The court must (1) conduct a searching inquiry establishing voluntariness and understanding of the plea's consequences; (2) require prosecution evidence proving guilt and the precise degree of culpability; and (3) ask whether the accused wishes to present evidence and allow it if desired.
🔍
1. Searching Inquiry
Voluntary, informed understanding of nature, elements & penalty
⚖️
2. Prosecution Evidence
Mandatory proof of guilt and precise degree of culpability (Pagal)
🛡️
3. Accused's Evidence
Court must ascertain if accused desires to present evidence

The searching inquiry is personal and substantive. The judge should determine whether each accused understands the acts charged, the gravity and penalty, and that pleading guilty means admitting the offense. It is especially important when several accused may have different roles: principal, accomplice, or accessory cannot simply be assumed identical. Under People v. Pagal, Supreme Court E-Library, the prosecution's duty to prove guilt remains despite the plea; failure of that proof can require acquittal.

R.A. No. 9346 prohibits imposition of the death penalty, substituting reclusion perpetua or life imprisonment as applicable. The historical term capital offense persists in Rule 116, Section 3 and its jurisprudence; do not infer that judges may presently impose death. The special caution for extremely grave charges remains an important teaching point.

For non-capital offenses, Rule 116, Section 4 makes reception of evidence to determine the penalty discretionary, unlike Section 3's mandatory prosecution evidence. The court must still ensure the plea is valid and address the proper penalty and civil liability where applicable. An improvident plea—a guilty plea entered without informed, voluntary understanding—may be withdrawn and replaced by not guilty before the conviction becomes final, under Section 5.

Final application check: A man pleads guilty to murder but tells the judge he merely defended himself. Do not automatically convict. The exculpatory claim calls for not guilty under Section 1(d), and the case proceeds appropriately. By contrast, an unequivocal, informed guilty plea to a capital charge triggers the three Section 3 safeguards. Always identify the nature of the plea before deciding the next procedure.
