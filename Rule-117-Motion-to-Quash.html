1. Nature of a Motion to Quash (Bar 2016)

A motion to quash is a special procedural remedy governed by Rule 117 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure. Jurisprudence highlights the following key characteristics:

📜
Facial Validity
Attacks insufficiency apparent on the face of the charge sheet
⚖️
Hypothetical Admission
Averred facts assumed true to test legal sufficiency
🚫
No Matters Aliunde
Extrinsic evidence excluded (save double jeopardy & prescription)
📥
Omnibus Rule
All available grounds must be raised or deemed waived
  • Mode of Assailing Validity: It is the procedural mode by which an accused assails the validity of a criminal complaint or information filed against him for insufficiency on its face in point of law, or for defects that are apparent on the face of the charge sheet.
  • Hypothetical Admission: Filing a motion to quash operates as a hypothetical admission of the facts alleged in the information.
    • The Fundamental Test: The court determines the sufficiency of the averments in the information by assessing whether the facts alleged, if hypothetically admitted to be true, would establish the essential elements of the crime defined by law.
  • Exclusion of Extrinsic Evidence (General Rule): Evidence aliunde or matters extrinsic to the information are generally not considered. The motion must be decided based solely on the face of the information.
    • Exceptions: Motions to quash anchored on double jeopardy or extinction of criminal action/liability may, by their nature, be proved using facts outside the allegations of the information.
  • Omnibus Motion Principle: Under Section 8, Rule 15 in relation to Section 9, Rule 117, a motion to quash functions as an omnibus motion. An accused must include all available objections in the motion; any objection not included is deemed waived.
    • Waiver mechanisms: A ground is waived if the accused either (1) fails to file a motion to quash before entering a plea, or (2) files a motion to quash but omits an available ground. For example, failure to object to duplicity of offenses or formal defects before entering a plea waives those objections, and they cannot be raised for the first time on appeal.

2. Grounds Not Waived

Under Section 9, Rule 117, while the general rule mandates that any ground not asserted prior to a plea is deemed waived, there are four (4) explicit exceptions—grounds that are NOT waived even if omitted from a motion to quash or raised for the first time later:

1️⃣
No Offense Charged
Sec. 3(a): Facts charged do not constitute an offense
2️⃣
Subject-Matter Jurisdiction
Sec. 3(b): Court has no jurisdiction over offense charged
3️⃣
Extinction of Liability
Sec. 3(g): Criminal action or liability extinguished
4️⃣
Double Jeopardy
Sec. 3(i): Previous conviction, acquittal, or dismissal
  1. Failure to Charge an Offense: That the facts charged do not constitute an offense (Sec. 3[a], Rule 117).
  2. Lack of Subject-Matter Jurisdiction: That the court trying the case has no jurisdiction over the offense charged (Sec. 3[b], Rule 117).
  3. Extinction of Liability: That the criminal action or liability has been extinguished (Sec. 3[g], Rule 117).
  4. Double Jeopardy: That the accused has been previously convicted or acquitted of the offense charged, or the case against him was dismissed or otherwise terminated without his express consent (Sec. 3[i], Rule 117).

3. Time for Filing the Motion to Quash (Bar 1994)

  • General Rule: A motion to quash may be made at any time before the accused enters his plea (Sec. 1, Rule 117).
  • Cases Subject to Summary Procedure: Under Section 19 of the 1991 Rule on Summary Procedure, a motion to quash is prohibited, EXCEPT on two specific grounds:
    1. Lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter; or
    2. Failure to comply with mandatory barangay conciliation proceedings under Section 18 of the Rule on Summary Procedure.

4. Form and Contents of the Motion to Quash

A motion to quash cannot be made orally in open court. Under Section 2, Rule 117, the motion must strictly comply with three (3) statutory requisites:

✍️
1. In Writing
Oral motions in open court are strictly unauthorized
🖋️
2. Signed
Must be subscribed by the accused or counsel of record
🎯

Distinctly specify factual and legal grounds relied upon
  1. It must be in writing;
  2. It must be signed by the accused or his counsel; and
  3. It must distinctly specify the factual and legal grounds upon which it is based.

6. Court to Consider Only Those Grounds Stated in the Motion

Under Section 2, Rule 117, the trial court is strictly confined to the motion's contents:

  • General Rule: The court shall consider no ground other than those expressly stated in the motion to quash.
  • Sole Statutory Exception: Lack of jurisdiction over the offense charged. The court may consider and dismiss the case on this ground motu proprio even if omitted from the motion.
  • Application: Even if an information contains statements showing a legal excuse or justification, the court cannot quash the case on that basis unless the accused explicitly stated that ground in his motion.

7. Grounds for a Motion to Quash (Bar 1987, 1990, 1991, 1992, 1993, 1994, 1995, 1996, 1998, 1999, 2000, 2002, 2003, 2004, 2005, 2009, 2010, 2016)

Under Section 3, Rule 117, a complaint or information may be quashed on any of the following nine (9) statutory grounds:

  1. Sec. 3(a): That the facts charged do not constitute an offense.
  2. Sec. 3(b): That the court trying the case has no jurisdiction over the offense charged.
  3. Sec. 3(c): That the court trying the case has no jurisdiction over the person of the accused.
  4. Sec. 3(d): That the officer who filed the information had no authority to do so.
  5. Sec. 3(e): That it does not conform substantially to the prescribed form.
  6. Sec. 3(f): That more than one offense is charged, except when a single punishment for various offenses is prescribed by law (duplicity of offenses).
  7. Sec. 3(g): That the criminal action or liability has been extinguished.
  8. Sec. 3(h): That it contains averments which, if true, would constitute a legal excuse or justification.
  9. Sec. 3(i): That the accused has been previously convicted or acquitted of the offense charged, or the case against him was dismissed or otherwise terminated without his express consent (Double Jeopardy).
  • Continuous Trial Rule Note: Under the Revised Guidelines for Continuous Trial of Criminal Cases (A.M. No. 15-06-10-SC), a motion to quash based on any ground other than those listed above is classified as a prohibited motion.

8. Execution of an Affidavit of Desistance Is Not a Ground for a Motion to Quash

  • Not a Statutory Ground: An affidavit of desistance is not among the grounds enumerated under Section 3 of Rule 117.
  • Incurable Once Instituted: Once a criminal action has been instituted in court, an affidavit of desistance or pardon executed by the complainant does not warrant the dismissal of the case (People v. Ramirez; People v. Salazar).
  • Low Probative Value: Recantations and retractions are viewed with extreme suspicion and disfavor by the Supreme Court (People v. Lamsen). A retraction executed after conviction is treated as an unreliable afterthought because affidavits of retraction are easily secured through intimidation or monetary consideration.

9. Absence of Probable Cause Not a Ground to Quash an Information (Bar 2015)

  • Proper Action vs. Quashal: The absence of probable cause for the issuance of a warrant of arrest is a ground for the dismissal of the case, but it is NOT a ground for quashing the information (People v. Sandiganbayan).
  • Legal Meaning of "Quash": "To quash" means to annul, vacate, or overthrow. Quashing an information attacks the formal or substantive validity of the charge sheet itself. When a motion to quash is sustained, the court may simply order the prosecutor to file a corrected complaint or information under Sections 5 and 6 of Rule 117.

10. Matters of Defense Are Not Grounds for a Motion to Quash

  • General Rule: Affirmative defenses cannot be raised in a motion to quash; they must be proved by the accused during a full-blown trial (Soriano v. People).
    • Example: If an accused charged with homicide files a motion to quash claiming he acted in self-defense, but self-defense is not stated on the face of the information, the motion must be denied. The court must proceed to trial to evaluate the defense.
  • Exceptions: Defenses explicitly authorized by Section 3 of Rule 117 as statutory grounds to quash, specifically double jeopardy (Sec. 3[i]) and extinction of criminal liability (Sec. 3[g]).

11. Absence of a Preliminary Investigation Is Not a Ground to Quash an Information (Bar 2009)

  • Non-Jurisdictional Defect: The absence, denial, or deficiency of a preliminary investigation does not impair the court's jurisdiction over the case nor affect the legal validity of the information (Rodis v. Sandiganbayan; Vasquez v. Hobilia-Alinio). Consequently, it is not a valid ground for a motion to quash.
  • Proper Remedy: The accused must file a motion for reinvestigation or a motion to suspend proceedings. The trial court should hold the criminal case in abeyance and remand the records to the prosecutor or Ombudsman to conduct or complete the preliminary investigation.

12. Test in Appreciating a Motion to Quash

The analytical approach taken by the court depends on the specific ground invoked:

  1. When Based on "Facts Charged Do Not Constitute an Offense" (Sec. 3[a]):
    • The Determinative Test: The court looks strictly at the sufficiency of the averments in the information. The test is whether the facts alleged, if hypothetically admitted, would establish the essential elements of the crime as defined by law, without considering matters aliunde (Lazarte v. Sandiganbayan; Santiago v. Sandiganbayan).
  2. When Based on Extinction of Liability (Sec. 3[g]) or Double Jeopardy (Sec. 3[i]):
    • Because Section 2 of Rule 117 requires the motion to state factual and legal grounds, facts and evidence outside the four corners of the information (matters aliunde) may be introduced and evaluated by the court to determine these specific defenses (Garcia v. Court of Appeals).

13. Alternative Actions of the Court When a Motion to Quash Is Filed

When faced with a motion to quash, the trial court has three (3) alternative options (De Lima v. Guerrero):

14. Amendment of the Information

  • Duty to Allow Amendment (Sec. 4, Rule 117): If a motion to quash is based on a defect in the information that can be cured by amendment, the court shall not immediately dismiss the case. Instead, it must order the prosecution to amend the charge sheet within a specified time (De Lima v. Guerrero).
  • Arbitrary Dismissal: A trial judge who outrightly quashes an information without giving the prosecution an opportunity to correct a curable defect commits an arbitrary act correctible by certiorari (De Lima v. Guerrero; Go v. BSP).
  • Application to Sec. 3(a): Even when the ground is "facts charged do not constitute an offense," the court must grant the prosecution a chance to supply the missing averment by amendment.
  • When Quashal Proceeds: If the prosecution (a) fails or refuses to make the ordered amendment, or (b) files an amended information that still suffers from the same defect, the court shall then grant the motion to quash.

15. Quashal of the Information (Bar 1994): Filing of Another Complaint or Information

When the court sustains a motion to quash and orders the information quashed, the following rules govern re-prosecution and custody:

A. Refiling Rules (Sec. 5 & 6, Rule 117)

  • General Rule: An order sustaining a motion to quash is not an acquittal and does not bar the filing of another complaint or information for the same offense (De Lima v. Guerrero).
  • Exceptions (Refiling Permanently BARRED - Sec. 6, Rule 117): Another complaint or information CANNOT be filed if the motion to quash was sustained on either of these two grounds:
    1. Extinction of criminal action or liability (Sec. 3[g]); or
    2. Double Jeopardy (Sec. 3[i]).

B. Custody Rules of the Accused (Sec. 5, Rule 117)

  1. If Another Information Is Ordered Filed: An accused who is in custody shall not be discharged or released, unless he is admitted to bail.
  2. If No Order Is Made / Time Lapses: If the court does not order the filing of another information, or if the prosecutor fails to file the new complaint/information within the time specified by the court, the accused in custody shall be released immediately, unless he is being held under another charge.

16. Denial of a Motion to Quash

  • Interlocutory Order: An order denying a motion to quash is interlocutory in nature.
  • Prohibition Against Appeal / Certiorari: Under Section 1(b), Rule 41 of the Rules of Court, an interlocutory order is not appealable, nor can it generally be the subject of a petition for certiorari (Miranda v. Sandiganbayan; De Lima v. Guerrero).
  • Proper Remedy: The accused must proceed to trial. If an adverse judgment of conviction is subsequently rendered, the accused can appeal the conviction and assign the improper denial of the motion to quash as an error committed by the trial court.
  • Strict Exception (Certiorari under Rule 65): A petition for certiorari against an order denying a motion to quash lies only when the denial was tainted with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction (Cagang v. Sandiganbayan).

Comprehensive Guide: Rule 117 (Motion to Quash — Double Jeopardy & Provisional Dismissal)

I. Concept, Nature, and Scope of Double Jeopardy

1. Constitutional and Statutory Foundations

Double jeopardy is anchored in Section 21, Article III of the 1987 Constitution, which provides:

"No person shall be twice put in jeopardy of punishment for the same offense. If an act is punished by a law and an ordinance, conviction or acquittal under either shall constitute a bar to another prosecution for the same act."

This constitutional command is mirrored in Section 7, Rule 117 of the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure:

"When an accused has been convicted or acquitted, or the case against him dismissed or otherwise terminated without his express consent by a court of competent jurisdiction, upon a valid complaint or information or other formal charge sufficient in form and substance to sustain a conviction and after the accused had pleaded to the charge, the conviction or acquittal of the accused or the dismissal...

Comprehensive Discussion: Double Jeopardy and Provisional Dismissal (Rule 117)

17. Fundamental Concepts of Double Jeopardy

A. Definition and Nature

The guarantee against double jeopardy is a fundamental constitutional protection under Article III, Section 21 of the 1987 Constitution. It prohibits the State from placing an individual in danger of conviction and punishment twice for the exact same offense.

  • The Essence of the Guarantee: The doctrine prevents the State, with all its resources and power, from subjecting a citizen to repeated criminal prosecutions for an alleged act. Repeated trials subject the accused to anxiety, expense, and continuous embarrassment, while raising the risk that an innocent person might ultimately be found guilty.
  • Jeopardy Defined: In legal terms, "jeopardy" refers to the danger of conviction and punishment that an accused incurs when charged under a valid indictment before a competent tribunal.

B. Res Judicata vs. Double Jeopardy ("Res Judicata in Prison Grey")

  • While res judicata is a doctrine originating in civil law, double jeopardy is often described in jurisprudence as "res judicata in prison grey".
  • It bars the prosecution from re-litigating a criminal charge once the accused has been validly convicted, acquitted, or when the case has been terminated without the accused's express consent.

18. Inapplicability of Double Jeopardy in Non-Criminal Proceedings

A. Administrative Cases

Double jeopardy does not apply to administrative proceedings.

  • Separate Remedies: A single wrongful act by a public officer may give rise to civil, criminal, and administrative liabilities. These three avenues are independent and distinct.
  • Different Standards of Proof:
    1. Criminal cases: Requires proof beyond reasonable doubt.
    2. Civil cases: Requires a preponderance of evidence.
    3. Administrative cases: Requires only substantial evidence.
  • Key Principle: Dismissal or acquittal in an administrative case does not bar a criminal prosecution for the same underlying conduct (Icasiano v. Sandiganbayan; Cayao-Lasam v. Ramolete). Conversely, a criminal dismissal does not automatically dismiss an administrative complaint.

B. Preliminary Investigation

Double jeopardy does not apply during a preliminary investigation.

  • Executive and Inquisitorial Nature: A preliminary investigation is purely executive and inquisitorial, designed merely to determine whether there is probable cause to hold a suspect for trial.
  • Not a Judicial Trial: Because a preliminary investigation is not a trial on the merits and does not involve an indictment before a court, jeopardy does not attach. Dismissing a complaint at the preliminary investigation stage does not prevent the prosecutor from re-investigating or refiling the case upon fresh evidence (Tandoc v. Resultan; Torres v. Aguinaldo).

20. Detailed Analysis of Each Requisite

A. Requisite 1: Valid Complaint or Information

  • The initial charge sheet must be sufficient in form and substance to sustain a conviction under Section 6, Rule 110.
  • Defective Information: If the original information fails to state an offense, double jeopardy cannot set in because the accused was never in peril of a valid conviction under that defective document.
  • Officer Lacking Authority: An information filed by a prosecutor who lacks territorial or subject-matter authority is a complete nullity (Cudia v. Court of Appeals). Dismissal of a void information does not bar a subsequent prosecution.

B. Requisite 2: Court of Competent Jurisdiction

  • The court that rendered the decision or dismissed the case must possess valid jurisdiction over the subject matter and territory.
  • Lack of Territorial Jurisdiction (Venue): Because venue in criminal law is jurisdictional, a dismissal by a court lacking territorial jurisdiction over the place of commission does not trigger double jeopardy.
  • Lack of Subject-Matter Jurisdiction: If a court tries a case over which it has no jurisdiction (e.g., a Municipal Trial Court trying a murder case punishable by reclusion perpetua), the entire proceeding is void ab initio. Subsequent indictment in the proper court (e.g., Regional Trial Court) is not barred by double jeopardy.

C. Requisite 3 & 4: Arraignment and Valid Plea

  • An accused must be formally arraigned and enter a valid plea for first jeopardy to attach.
  • Withdrawal Before Arraignment: If the prosecution withdraws an information before the accused is arraigned, double jeopardy cannot be invoked when a new charge is filed.
  • Invalid or Conditional Plea: A plea must be unconditional.
    • Void Plea Bargain: In People v. Magat, the accused entered a conditional guilty plea under an illegal plea bargain. The Supreme Court held that a conviction based on a void, conditional plea is void ab initio, meaning double jeopardy did not attach to bar a re-trial.
    • Exculpatory Testimony: In People v. Balisacan, the accused pleaded guilty but testified that he acted in self-defense. The Supreme Court ruled that testifying on self-defense vacated his guilty plea. Because the trial court acquitted him without entering a "not guilty" plea and conducting a trial, there was no standing plea, and double jeopardy did not bar the prosecution's appeal.

D. Requisite 5: Prior Conviction, Acquittal, or Dismissal Without Express Consent

First jeopardy terminates only upon:

  1. Conviction;
  2. Acquittal; or
  3. Dismissal/Termination without the express consent of the accused.
  • Pending Cases: If a prior criminal case for the same act is still pending and has not been terminated by conviction, acquittal, or dismissal, double jeopardy cannot be pleaded (Tangan v. People).

21. Express Consent vs. Dismissals Equivalent to Acquittal

A. Meaning of "Express Consent"

  • As a rule, if a case is dismissed upon the motion or with the express consent of the accused, he is deemed to have waived his right against double jeopardy.
  • Standard for Consent: Consent must be direct, positive, explicit, and unequivocal (People v. Vergara).
  • Provisional Dismissal: A dismissal requested or consented to by the accused operates as a waiver of double jeopardy for subsequent revival (Andres v. Cacdac; Saldariega v. Panganiban).
  • Ex Parte Dismissals: In People v. Espinosa, the prosecution moved ex parte to withdraw charges after the accused entered a simple, unconditional plea. Because the dismissal occurred without the knowledge or express consent of the accused, double jeopardy attached to bar reinstatement.

B. Distinction Between Acquittal and Dismissal

  • Acquittal: Always based on the merits of the case—either because the prosecution failed to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, or because the act/omission did not exist (Morillo v. People).
  • Dismissal: Terminates the proceeding on technical or jurisdictional grounds (e.g., lack of territorial jurisdiction or defective information) without resolving the guilt or innocence of the accused (Morillo v. People).

C. Dismissals Equivalent to Acquittal (Even With Consent of Accused)

Generally, a dismissal granted upon motion of the accused does not bar re-prosecution. However, three major exceptions treat a dismissal as an acquittal on the merits, barring re-prosecution even if initiated by the accused:

1. Demurrer to Evidence
Granted for insufficiency of prosecution evidence (Rule 119)

🤝

  2. Dismissal Based on Violation of Right to Speedy Trial / Inordinate Delay: When the prosecution fails to present evidence or proceed to trial despite the insistence of the accused (Esmeña v. Pogoy; Coscolluela v. Sandiganbayan).
  3. Discharge as a State Witness: Under Section 18, Rule 119, discharging an accused to serve as a state witness operates as an acquittal.

22. Effects of Double Jeopardy & The Finality-of-Acquittal Doctrine

A. The Criminal Aspect & Finality-of-Acquittal Doctrine

  • Immediate Finality: A judgment of acquittal in a criminal case is immediately final, unappealable, and executory upon promulgation.
  • No Appeal by Prosecution: The State cannot appeal an acquittal, whether by ordinary appeal or petition for review under Rule 45, as doing so puts the accused twice in peril.
  • Purposes of the Doctrine:
    1. Prevents the State from harassing citizens through repeated prosecutions.
    2. Prevents the State from retrying an acquitted defendant to refine its evidence.
    3. Guarantees the defendant's right to repose.

B. Appeals by the Accused

  • When an accused appeals a judgment of conviction, he waives his protection against double jeopardy. The appellate court opens the entire case for review and may increase the penalty, modify the offense, or correct legal errors (People v. Caraang).

C. Exception: Review of Erroneous Acquittal via Rule 65 Certiorari

An acquittal may be assailed by the prosecution ONLY through a Special Civil Action for Certiorari under Rule 65, subject to strict conditions:

  • Grounds Required: The prosecution must prove that the trial court acted with grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction, or that there was a denial of due process (mistrial) (Galman v. Sandiganbayan; People v. Asis).
  • Void Judgment: A decision rendered with grave abuse of discretion or in a sham/mock trial is void ab initio. In legal contemplation, double jeopardy does not set in because a void judgment cannot terminate the first jeopardy.

D. Effect of Double Jeopardy on the Civil Aspect

  • No Bar to Civil Appeals: Double jeopardy applies strictly to criminal liability (ex delicto), not civil liability.
  • Victim's Right to Appeal: Either the private offended party or the accused may appeal the civil aspect of a criminal judgment despite an acquittal (Cruz v. Court of Appeals; Salazar v. People).
  • Extinction of Accused: If an accused dies pending appeal, criminal liability is extinguished, but independent civil actions survive against his estate under Rule 111, Section 4.

23. Double Jeopardy in Quasi-Offenses (Article 365, RPC)

The Ivler Doctrine (Ivler v. Modesto-San Pedro)

In Ivler v. Modesto-San Pedro, the Supreme Court established landmark principles governing reckless imprudence under Article 365 of the Revised Penal Code:

Single Careless or Reckless Act (Art. 365, RPC)
The negligent act itself is penalized, NOT the resulting consequences
↓
Prior Conviction or Acquittal for ANY Resulting Consequence
e.g., Reckless Imprudence Resulting in Slight Physical Injuries
↓
ABSOLUTE BAR TO SUBSEQUENT PROSECUTION
Bars prosecution for homicide, damage to property, or serious injuries arising from the same negligent act
  • Single Quasi-Offense: Reckless imprudence under Art. 365 is a single quasi-offense in itself, not merely a modality for committing other crimes.
  • The Negligent Act is Penalized: The law penalizes the single negligent or careless act, regardless of the number or severity of resulting consequences (e.g., physical injuries, homicide, or damage to property).
  • Bar to Subsequent Prosecution: Conviction or acquittal of a quasi-offense (e.g., reckless imprudence resulting in slight physical injuries) bars any subsequent prosecution for another consequence arising from the exact same negligent act (e.g., reckless imprudence resulting in homicide).
  • Inaccurate Designations: Designating a crime as "falsification through reckless imprudence" is inaccurate; quasi-offenses must explicitly allege the resulting damage or injury (Sevilla v. People).

24. Exceptions: When Double Jeopardy Does NOT Apply Despite Prior Conviction

Under Section 7, Rule 117, a prior conviction does NOT bar a second prosecution for an offense that necessarily includes the former under three (3) statutory exceptions:

⚡
1. Supervening Fact
Graver offense developed from a fact arising later (e.g., victim dies)
🔍
2. Newly Discovered Facts
Facts constituting graver charge discovered only after plea
🚫
3. Invalid Plea to Lesser Offense
Plea to lesser offense without consent of prosecutor/victim
  1. Supervening Fact: The graver offense developed due to a supervening fact arising from the same act or omission.
    • Example: A conviction for serious physical injuries does not bar a subsequent prosecution for homicide if the victim dies later from the same injuries.
  2. Newly Discovered Facts: Facts constituting the graver charge became known or were discovered only after a plea was entered in the former case.
  3. Invalid Plea to Lesser Offense: The plea of guilty to a lesser offense was made without the consent of the prosecutor or offended party (except as provided under Sec. 1[f], Rule 116).

25. Meaning of "Same Offense" vs. "Different Offenses"

A. The Test for Identity of Offenses

Double jeopardy prohibits prosecution for the same offense, not the same act. Two offenses are not the same if there is a variance between their essential legal elements.

B. Illustrative Examples of Separate Offenses From Identical Acts

26. Provisional Dismissal (Rule 117, Section 8)

A. Definition and Purpose

Provisional dismissal is a temporary dismissal of a criminal case that does not terminate the action permanently and may be revived within specified statutory periods. It prevents cases from hanging indefinitely due to prosecutorial or judicial inaction (People v. Lacson).

B. Requisites for Valid Provisional Dismissal

Under Section 8, Rule 117, two (2) mandatory requisites must concur:

🗣️
1. Express Consent
Direct, positive, and unequivocal consent of the accused
📬
2. Notice to Offended Party
Mandatory written notice to the private complainant
  1. Express consent of the accused; and
  2. Notice to the offended party.

C. Why It Does Not Operate as an Acquittal

Because a provisional dismissal requires the express consent of the accused, the accused waives the right to invoke double jeopardy upon revival of the case (Saldariega v. Panganiban).

27. The Time-Bar Rule

Under Section 8, Rule 117 and the Guidelines for Continuous Trial (A.M. No. 15-06-10-SC), a provisional dismissal becomes permanently barred if the prosecution fails to revive the case within the following timelines:

1 YEAR
PENALTY ≤ 6 YEARS / FINE
One (1) Year Time-Bar
Offenses punishable by imprisonment not exceeding 6 years, or fine of any amount, or both
2 YEARS
PENALTY > 6 YEARS
Two (2) Years Time-Bar
Offenses punishable by imprisonment exceeding 6 years
  1. One (1) Year: For offenses punishable by imprisonment not exceeding six (6) years, or a fine of any amount, or both.
  2. Two (2) Years: For offenses punishable by imprisonment of more than six (6) years.
  • Computation: The period is reckoned strictly from the issuance of the order of provisional dismissal (A.M. No. 12-11-2-SC).
  • Effect of Expiration: Upon the lapse of 1 or 2 years without revival, the dismissal becomes permanent ipso facto, operating as an adjudication on the merits (People v. Lacson).

28. Additional Rules on Provisional Dismissal (A.M. No. 12-11-2-SC, Sec. 10)

In detention cases, provisional dismissal is governed by specific congested jail guidelines:

  1. Unknown Witness: If delays stem from the absence of an essential witness whose whereabouts are unknown or cannot be determined, and trial exceeds 180 days, the court shall provisionally dismiss the case with the express consent of the detained accused.
  2. Known Witness Unobtainable: If the essential witness's whereabouts are known but presence cannot be obtained despite due diligence, provisional dismissal proceeds provided:
    • The case was twice postponed due to non-appearance of the witness;
    • Warning notices were issued for the third hearing; and
    • Proof of service of subpoenas/notices exists.
